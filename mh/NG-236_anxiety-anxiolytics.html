Anxiolytics 💊

Benzodiazepines & Buspirone — Two Opposite Strategies for Anxiety

NG-236 MENTAL HEALTH ADHD-friendly visual edition

This page is the pharmacology companion to every anxiety-spectrum page in this batch. Two very different drugs both treat anxiety: benzodiazepines work in minutes by boosting GABA, but carry real dependence risk and are meant for short-term use only. Buspirone works on serotonin instead, takes weeks to kick in, and carries essentially no abuse potential — the trade-off is right there in the numbers.

🧪 Benzos = ↑GABAFast, effective, habit-forming — short-term use only.
🐢 Buspirone = delayedNon-habit-forming, but takes 2–4 weeks — not for an acute attack.
🆘 AntidotesBenzo → flumazenil. Opioid → naloxone (Narcan). Buspirone needs neither.
🚫 Never stop a benzo coldAbrupt discontinuation risks rebound anxiety and withdrawal seizures.

MECHANISM

STEP 1 · HOW IT WORKS

Two receptor systems, two speeds — GABA calms fast, serotonin calms slow.

🧬 GABA vs. serotonin — the whole page in one diagram

🧠 “Benzos open a door, buspirone retrains the doorman.” One is immediate mechanical relief; the other is a slow receptor-level adjustment — that's why the onset times are worlds apart.

💊 Benzodiazepines — the "-am/-pam" family

  • Alprazolam, Midazolam — "-lam"
  • Temazepam, Clonazepam, Diazepam — "-pam"

MOA: increases GABA (the brain's major inhibitory neurotransmitter) effect at the GABA-A receptor → widens the chloride channel → CNS-wide slowing.

Indications: anxiety, seizures, muscle spasm, alcohol/sedative withdrawal, procedural sedation.

🧠 “Low and slow vitals” — expect BP, HR, and RR to trend down with a working dose. Too far down = toxicity.

💊 Buspirone — the outsider

MOA: partial agonist at serotonin 5-HT1A receptors (with some dopamine activity) — a completely different pathway from benzos or barbiturates.

Key point: non-habit-forming, no abuse potential — and it does not potentiate CNS depression from alcohol the way benzos do.

Patient teaching: "drive the BUSpirone" — okay to drive/operate machinery, unlike a benzo.

🧠 NOT used for acute attacks — onset is 2–4 weeks, so it has no role in stopping a panic attack in progress.

💊 Barbiturates — the older, riskier cousin

Phenobarbital — increases GABA effect similarly to benzos, but by prolonging how long the chloride channel stays open rather than how wide. Lasts longer in the body ("takes longer to kick in, takes longer to get out"), which means a higher risk of toxicity — hypotension, respiratory depression. Largely replaced by benzodiazepines for anxiety because of this narrower safety margin, though still used for seizure control.

🧠 “PHENO-barbital, stuck behind bars a long time” — sedation lasts long, and so does the risk.

⏱️ Onset comparison — minutes vs. weeks

🧠 If the question says "acute panic attack, right now" — the answer is a benzodiazepine, never buspirone.

⚠️ Benzo dependence & withdrawal — the trade-off for speed

Physical dependence can develop with regular use, even at therapeutic doses. Never stop a benzodiazepine abruptly — withdrawal can include rebound anxiety, tremor, autonomic instability, and seizures. Always taper.

🧠 Don't confuse the two uses of benzos: here they're the drug being taken for anxiety (short-term only, dependence is the risk). Separately, benzodiazepines are also the drugs used to manage other withdrawal syndromes (like alcohol withdrawal, on a taper protocol) — a supervised, monitored, very different clinical context.

🧪 Side effects to monitor

  • Benzos: sedation, respiratory depression, hypotension, fall risk (especially older adults)
  • Additive risk: combining with alcohol or opioids compounds CNS/respiratory depression
  • Barbiturates: same risks, amplified by the longer half-life

TEACH

STEP 3 · PATIENT EDUCATION

Antidotes, interactions, and the exact points that show up on the exam.

🆘 Antidote pairing — memorize this pair

ToxicityAntidote
Benzodiazepine overdoseFlumazenil
Opioid overdoseNaloxone (brand: Narcan)
BuspironeNo specific antidote needed — minimal CNS-depressant overdose risk
🧠 “Flu-mazenil flushes the benzo. Nal-oxone knocks out the opioid.” Keep the two straight — a classic mix-and-match NCLEX question.

✅ Patient teaching — benzodiazepines

  • Take at bedtime if prescribed that way; don't skip doses
  • Stop drinking alcohol — dangerous additive sedation
  • Do not operate dangerous machinery or drive
  • Avoid muscle relaxants and other CNS depressants
  • Avoid valerian root, ginkgo, and ginseng — can add to sedation or affect anxiety/bleeding risk
  • Report any history of reaction to another benzodiazepine (e.g., midazolam) — cross-sensitivity within the class
  • Never stop abruptly — taper under supervision

✅ Patient teaching — buspirone

  • OK to drive — "drive the BUSpirone"
  • Explain the delayed onset (2–4 weeks) up front, or clients stop taking it thinking it "isn't working"
  • Not for an acute panic attack — it will not help in the moment
  • No physical dependence, no withdrawal taper required
🧠 If a question describes a client wanting relief "right now," buspirone is the wrong answer every time.

🚨 Never combos & caution flags

  • Never combine a benzodiazepine + opioid + alcohol — the classic respiratory-depression triad
  • Never discontinue a benzodiazepine abruptly after regular use — seizure risk
  • Never expect buspirone to abort an active panic attack
  • 🚨 Phenobarbital toxicity picture: hypotension + increasing sedation — report immediately

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: Why can't buspirone be used to stop an acute panic attack?
Its onset is delayed 2–4 weeks — it works on serotonin 5-HT1A receptors through gradual adaptation, not the immediate GABA boost a benzodiazepine provides.
Q2: What's the antidote for a benzodiazepine overdose? For an opioid overdose?
Flumazenil for benzodiazepines; naloxone (Narcan) for opioids.
Q3: Why should a client never stop a benzodiazepine abruptly?
Physical dependence can develop even at therapeutic doses; abrupt discontinuation risks rebound anxiety, tremor, autonomic instability, and seizures — it must be tapered.
Q4: A client on phenobarbital has low blood pressure and increasing sedation. What does this indicate?
Barbiturate toxicity — phenobarbital has a narrow safety margin and a long half-life; report immediately, monitor respiratory status closely.

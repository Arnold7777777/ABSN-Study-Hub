💐 Postmortem Care

The last nursing care a person receives, and the first hour of the family's grief. Almost every exam question here turns on one fork: is this a medical examiner case?

⚠️ Ask this first

Has the death been pronounced and documented, and is the case going to the medical examiner or coroner? Everything else follows from the answer.

✅ Expected death — ordinary postmortem care

Remove the tubes, lines and drains. Wash the body, take off soiled dressings, put on a clean gown.

Make her look like herself for the people about to walk in.

🚨 Medical examiner case — touch nothing

Leave every tube, line and drain exactly where it is. The endotracheal tube stays. The IV catheters stay. Do not bathe the body, and do not throw away tubing or dressings — they are evidence.

Clamp or cap lines if needed, and document.

Which deaths go to the medical examiner? Anything unexpected, any trauma — a motor-vehicle crash, a fall, a burn — anything violent or suspicious, a death soon after admission or during a procedure, and any suspected homicide, suicide or overdose. When in doubt, leave it in and ask.

📋 The workflow, in order

For an expected death. Time matters for only one reason: rigor mortis begins in about 2 to 4 hours, and after that the face cannot be arranged.

1

Confirm the death has been pronounced and documented. Nothing else starts until it has.

2

Gloves, and standard precautions throughout. They do not stop applying because the client has died.

3

Remove tubes, lines and drains — expected death only.

4

Wash the body, remove soiled dressings, apply a clean gown and fresh linen.

5

Dentures in, and close the mouth — now, before rigor. A denture that waits will not go back in.

6

Close the eyes. Hold the lids down gently for a few seconds.

7

Supine, arms at the sides, head of the bed slightly raised. Lying flat lets blood pool in the face and discolour it.

8

Valuables documented and handed over to the family or to security, with a witness. Jewellery does not go in a bedside drawer.

9

Identification tags on the body and on the shroud — both, always.

🧘 The family

  • They stay as long as they need. There is no correct amount of time, and the body is not waiting on anyone.
  • Dim the lights, give them privacy, offer a chair and a glass of water.
  • Touching and holding are encouraged, not discouraged — including holding an infant who has died.
  • Offer the chaplain, cultural or religious rites, and ask rather than assume which apply.
  • Say the person's name.

“Please step out so we can prepare the body” is the wrong answer on any exam and in any room.

🎁 Organ and tissue donation

The nurse notifies the organ procurement organization. The OPO does the asking. That is the whole answer, and it holds even when the family objects to a signed donor card.

Federal rules require every death to be referred to the OPO, and require the approach to be made by trained requestors — not by the bedside nurse, and not in an argument.

What the nurse does not do:

  • Tell the family the donor card is legally binding and proceed
  • Pressure them by naming the people waiting
  • Document the refusal and stop there — the referral is still required

Notify the OPO, support the family, and let the trained requestor have the conversation.

💕 The grief alongside it

Kübler-Ross — five stages, and nobody moves through them in order.

  • Denial — “the scan must belong to someone else”
  • Anger — at the staff, the family, the world, herself
  • Bargaining — “if I can just make it to my daughter's wedding in June” — a deal offered in exchange for time. This is the one most often tested.
  • Depression — the sadness of what is actually being lost
  • Acceptance — not happiness; quiet

Disenfranchised grief is grief the world will not let someone have. The loss is real, but it is not socially recognised, so there is no card, no leave from work, no funeral.

  • Early pregnancy loss — the classic example, grieved alone by someone whose pregnancy few people knew about
  • The death of an ex-partner, a pet, or a friend rather than a relative
  • Losses after a death by suicide or overdose, where the stigma silences the mourner
  • Grief in someone with dementia, assumed not to feel it

Contrast: crying over a parent who died 3 weeks ago is normal grief, and sadness at retiring is an ordinary life transition. What makes grief disenfranchised is that it goes unacknowledged.

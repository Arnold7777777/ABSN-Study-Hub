Abuse & Neglect 🛡️

Recognition · Mandatory Reporting · Safety Planning

NG-139 Mental Health ADHD-friendly visual edition

Nurses are mandated reporters. This page covers recognition of physical, emotional, sexual, financial abuse and neglect across child, elder, and intimate-partner contexts, plus the objective documentation and safety-planning steps that follow — clinical and factual, not graphic.

FORMS

STEP 1 · NAME THE TYPE

Abuse is not only physical — and it happens across every age group in different patterns.

🧩 5 forms of abuse/neglect

🧠 "PESFN" — say it like "pieces-fin": Physical · Emotional · Sexual · Financial · Neglect. Every abuse question is really asking "which piece is missing?"

👵 Elder neglect — definition

A form of abuse that happens when the caregiver fails to provide for the needs of the elderly client — either emotionally, physically, or socially.

🧠 Neglect is a failure to act, not a direct act — that's what separates it from active physical/emotional abuse in documentation.

💔 Intimate partner violence (IPV) — key word definition

Intimate partner violence is domestic violence or abusive behavior inflicted by one partner against the other — physically, emotionally, verbally, sexually, or financially.

  • Abusive partner traits: extreme jealousy & possessiveness
  • Abuse gets more intense during pregnancy
🧠 Pregnancy is a documented high-risk window for IPV escalation — screen every pregnant client privately, every visit.
🔎

RECOGNIZE

STEP 2 · SPOT THE PATTERN

Signs cluster differently by population — know the elder-neglect picture and the IPV screening questions cold.

👵 Elder neglect — what the nurse observes

🧠 Assessment case: a 79-year-old weighs 93 lbs, wears old dirty clothes, and is admitted with pneumonia. The comment that suggests significant abuse risk: "Our family is poor, so my daughter gets my monthly retirement and Social Security checks." → financial exploitation as part of neglect.

🩹 Caregiver Role Strain (CRS) — a risk factor to assess, not blame

  • Assess stressors & unmet needs of the caregiver
  • Ask about the nature & requirements of providing daily care
🧠 Neglect isn't always malicious — an overwhelmed, unsupported caregiver is a known risk factor. Assessing CRS is prevention, not accusation.

🚪 IPV — priority action & screening questions

PRIORITY Have the partner leave the room to speak with and examine the client in private.

  • "Is anyone hurting you?"
  • "When you and your spouse disagree, what happens?"
  • "Has your spouse ever threatened you verbally or with violence?"
🧠 Bruises in various stages of healing on assessment = ask these three direct, private questions — never in front of the suspected abuser.

⚠️ Why victims stay — and when danger peaks

  • Financial dependence
  • Fear of harm (to self, children, pets)
  • Child custody concerns
  • Religious or cultural beliefs

Abuse is expected to worsen when the victim moves toward independence from the abuser — leaving is statistically the most dangerous point in an abusive relationship.

🧠 Never judge "why don't they just leave." The nurse's job is safety planning for whenever the client is ready — not pressuring them to leave on the nurse's timeline.
🛡️

RESPOND

STEP 3 · REPORT, DOCUMENT, PLAN SAFETY

Reporting is mandatory; documentation and safety planning are the nurse's core interventions.

📣 Mandatory reporting — a nurse's legal duty

1
🚨 Ensure immediate safety — treat injuries, separate client from suspected abuser for the interview
2
📞 Report per state law & facility policy — nurses are mandated reporters for suspected child abuse/neglect and elder abuse in every U.S. state; reporting is not optional and does not require the client's/family's consent
3
📝 Document objectively — direct quotes, measured/described findings, no personal conclusions
4
🧷 Preserve evidence if applicable per facility protocol
5
🏠 Safety-plan & connect to resources before discharge
🧠 Reporting suspicion is enough — the nurse does not have to prove abuse occurred to make a report; "reasonable suspicion" triggers the mandatory-reporting duty.

✅ Interventions for the victim

  • Assure the victim they are not alone
  • Preserve physical evidence, if applicable
  • Convey concern & respect — nonjudgmental attitude
  • Affirm the client did not deserve or cause the abuse
  • Help identify triggers and destructive patterns; learn alternative responses (psychotherapy/talk therapy)
  • Develop a safety plan — local shelter contact information
🧠 Priority action before discharge home: provide contact information for the local shelter.

📝 Documentation — how, not just what

  • Use the client's own words in direct quotes
  • Describe findings objectively (size, location, color) — avoid concluding "abuse" in the chart; describe, don't diagnose intent
  • Use a body map/diagram where available
  • Note who was present during the exam and interview
  • Avoid leading questions; ask open-ended questions in private
🧠 Objective ≠ vague. "3 cm bruise, left forearm, purple-yellow" is objective. "Client was clearly beaten" is not — that's a conclusion, not a finding.

🎓 Client teaching that shows understanding

A client experiencing IPV states: "I should try to identify issues that increased my partner's stress level."

This statement needs correction — it reflects internalized responsibility for the abuser's behavior. Effective teaching redirects the client toward recognizing that the abuse is not their responsibility to prevent or manage, and toward safety planning instead.

🧠 A common NCLEX trap: a statement that sounds like insight ("I'll manage their stress") can actually indicate the client still blames themselves — watch for self-blame language disguised as a coping plan.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: A 79-year-old weighs 93 lbs, wears dirty clothes, and says a family member controls her retirement checks. What's the concern?
Elder neglect with financial exploitation — a caregiver failing to meet physical/financial needs.
Q2: A client has bruises in various stages of healing. What must the nurse ask?
"Is anyone hurting you?" / "What happens when you and your spouse disagree?" / "Has your spouse ever threatened you verbally or with violence?" — asked in private.
Q3: When is IPV danger expected to be highest?
When the victim moves toward independence from the abuser (e.g., leaving the relationship).
Q4: What is the nurse's priority action before an IPV victim returns home?
Provide contact information for the local shelter as part of a safety plan.

Lithium 🔋

The classic mood stabilizer — narrow therapeutic index, big teaching load

NG-250 MENTAL HEALTH ADHD-friendly visual edition

Lithium is the oldest, most classic mood stabilizer for bipolar disorder — and it has one of the narrowest therapeutic windows of any drug you'll give. The dose that treats mania and the dose that poisons the client are close together, so almost everything taught about lithium is really teaching about staying in that window.

CAUSE

STEP 1 · WHY WE GIVE IT

A long-term mood stabilizer — not a fast fix, and not forgiving of a narrow miss.

🎯 Indication & the therapeutic window

🧠 Never memorize just one number. Ranges are cited slightly differently across references — know the concept (narrow window, routine level checks) and confirm the exact cutoffs against your current course/facility reference.

🔋 What it treats

  • Bipolar disorder — primarily to control/prevent manic episodes and stabilize mood over the long term
  • Schizoaffective disorder

Given for long-term (maintenance) treatment — it is not a fast-acting rescue medication for acute agitation.

🧠 Lithium Battery — “B” for Bipolar. A battery is a long-lasting power source, and lithium is a long-term mood stabilizer — both about the long haul, not a quick charge.

🧠 Mechanism (high-level)

The exact mechanism isn't fully settled, but lithium is thought to stabilize neuronal cell membranes and alter sodium transport/second-messenger signaling in neurons, dampening the mania cycle over time.

Because lithium is handled by the kidneys largely like sodium, everything about its safety profile traces back to fluid and sodium balance — that connection is the highest-yield idea on this whole page.

🧠 Lithium rides the same train as sodium. Whatever changes sodium and fluid balance changes the lithium level too.
🔎

CLUES

STEP 2 · TOXICITY SIGNS

Early toxicity is mild and easy to miss — late toxicity is a medical emergency.

📈 Early → late toxicity — a progression, not a single moment

🧠 “GI + tremor + ringing ears = catch it early.” Any client on lithium with new nausea, a fine tremor, or tinnitus needs a level checked before symptoms progress to confusion and seizures.

🫘 Kidney signs the source flags directly

  • 🧪 Rising creatinine above the client's baseline — a general marker of reduced kidney function; know your lab's normal range and trend the client's own baseline
  • 🚽 Low urine output — roughly <30 mL/hr is a widely-used nursing threshold for concerning oliguria and should be reported
🧠 Kidneys clear lithium — hurt kidneys mean lithium has nowhere to go but up. That's why renal signs and lithium toxicity travel together.

🔔 Classic exam clue: tinnitus

Ringing in the ears is a distinctive, easy-to-remember early sign that shows up disproportionately often on exams for lithium specifically — flag it whenever it appears alongside a mood-stabilizer client.

🧠 If a psych-unit client on a mood stabilizer suddenly complains their ears are ringing, think lithium level before anything else.
🩺

CARE

STEP 3 · MONITOR & TEACH

Almost every teaching point here exists to protect the narrow therapeutic window.

🧂 The single highest-yield teaching point — sodium & fluid intake

  • Do NOT restrict sodium or water intake while on lithium — a low-sodium diet raises the lithium level toward toxic
  • Maintain consistent, adequate daily sodium and fluid intake — don't swing between diets
  • Replace fluids aggressively during vomiting, diarrhea, heavy sweating, or fever ("stomach flu" is a real high-risk toxicity trigger) — report excessive urination too, since both dehydration and unusual polyuria disturb the balance
  • Serum sodium <135 mEq/L (hyponatremia) raises toxicity risk and needs prompt attention
🧠 “No salt, no water = lithium goes up.” Sodium and lithium are reabsorbed together in the kidney — anything that makes the body hang onto sodium (dehydration, low-sodium diet, heavy sweating) also makes it hang onto more lithium.

🚫 Hold NSAIDs

Avoid NSAIDs — ibuprofen, naproxen — in clients on lithium.

NSAIDs decrease renal blood flow, which reduces lithium clearance and pushes levels up toward toxic. A client who says "I take ibuprofen for headaches" while on lithium needs further teaching.

🧠 Less blood to the kidney = less lithium leaving the body. Same underlying logic as the sodium teaching — anything that stresses the kidney raises the level.

🧪 Baseline & routine monitoring

  • 🫘 Renal function — baseline and periodic (BUN/creatinine) — lithium is renally cleared
  • 🦋 Thyroid function (TSH) — baseline and periodic — lithium can cause hypothyroidism/goiter over time
  • 🩸 Lithium level — routine trough levels, drawn per protocol (commonly ~12 hours after the last dose) to guide dosing
  • ❤️ Baseline ECG may be considered in some clients, per facility protocol
🧠 “Kidney, thyroid, level” — the routine lithium labs. All three get checked before starting and then on an ongoing schedule for the life of therapy — exact frequency is set by the prescriber/facility protocol.

✅ Continue vs. hold — reading a level correctly

If the lithium level comes back within the client's therapeutic range and the client has no toxicity symptoms, the correct answer is usually to continue the current dose — a therapeutic level is a normal, expected finding, not a reason to hold or adjust the medication.

  • ✅ Level in range, no symptoms → continue current dose
  • ⚠️ Level high or symptomatic → hold the dose, notify the HCP, reassess hydration/sodium/renal status
🧠 A common test trap is treating a normal, in-range level as if it were a problem. Read the level together with the symptoms — not either one alone.

🤰 Pregnancy caution

Lithium carries known reproductive/teratogenic risk, particularly with first-trimester exposure — this is generally taught as a reason for close specialist involvement and risk-benefit discussion, not a page to memorize an exact defect statistic from. Any client of childbearing age on lithium should be counseled about pregnancy planning and contraception.

🧠 Pair this with the reproductive-risk contrast already flagged for anticonvulsant mood stabilizers: different mechanisms, but the same nursing habit — always screen pregnancy status/plans before and during mood-stabilizer therapy.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: A client on lithium develops a stomach virus with vomiting and diarrhea. What's the nursing priority?
Aggressively replace fluids/sodium and monitor closely for toxicity — vomiting/diarrhea causes dehydration and sodium loss, which raise the lithium level toward toxic. Hold the dose and notify the HCP if toxicity signs appear.
Q2: A client on lithium reports taking ibuprofen regularly for headaches. What's the concern?
NSAIDs decrease renal blood flow and reduce lithium clearance, increasing the risk of toxicity — this needs further teaching / a non-NSAID alternative.
Q3: A client's lithium level returns within the therapeutic range and they have no symptoms. What should the nurse do?
Continue the current dose — a therapeutic level with no symptoms is the expected, desired finding.
Q4: Which early sign should prompt the nurse to have a lithium level checked?
New tinnitus (ringing in the ears), fine hand tremor, or GI upset (nausea/vomiting/diarrhea) — early, often mild toxicity signs.

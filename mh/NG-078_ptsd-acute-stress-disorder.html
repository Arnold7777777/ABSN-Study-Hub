PTSD 🌩️

& Acute Stress Disorder (ASD)

NG-078 MENTAL HEALTH ADHD-friendly visual edition

Both ASD and PTSD are anxiety-spectrum responses that can develop after exposure to a traumatic event. One clock separates them: symptoms in the first month = ASD; symptoms past one month = PTSD. Four symptom clusters describe the whole clinical picture, and the nurse's first job — every time — is a safety check before anything else.

CAUSE

STEP 1 · THE TIMELINE

Same trauma-response biology — the only thing that changes the diagnosis is the calendar.

🕐 ASD → PTSD: one timeline, one cutoff

🧠 “Under a month = ASD. Over a month = PTSD.” Same symptom clusters both sides of the line — only the duration changes the label.

🧬 What counts as "trauma" (diagnostic criteria)

  • Directly experiencing a threat to life, serious injury, or sexual violence
  • Witnessing the event happen to someone else
  • Learning it happened to a close family member or friend
  • Repeated professional exposure to trauma details (e.g., first responders)
🧠 Keep assessment clinical and non-graphic — you do not need event detail to identify the diagnosis or plan safe care.

⚠️ Risk factors that raise likelihood

  • Prior trauma history
  • Lack of social support after the event
  • Co-occurring substance use
  • Severity/duration of the traumatic exposure
🔎

CLUES

STEP 2 · FOUR CLUSTERS

Four symptom clusters cover the whole clinical picture — plus the physiology behind a flashback.

🧩 The 4 symptom clusters — memorize this grid

🧠 “I AM H” — Intrusion · Avoidance · Mood/cognition (negative) · Hyperarousal. All four must be present for the PTSD diagnosis, not just one.

🚨 A trauma reminder can trigger a full sympathetic surge

🧠 A war veteran hearing a car backfire and feeling like they're back in combat is hyperarousal + intrusion firing together — reassure them of present safety first.

🗣️ Say this — priority response example

Client: "The war was years ago, but I still remember my friends who were killed. I don't know why I lived and they died."

Nurse's priority response: "Are you having any thoughts of harming yourself?"

🧠 Survivor's guilt is a red flag for self-harm risk. Always screen for safety before exploring the feeling further.

⭐ Assessment priorities beyond safety

  • Self-harm: thoughts or plans
  • Substance use (drugs & alcohol) — common coping attempt
  • Relationships with family & friends — isolation is a warning sign
  • For sexual assault survivors: assess for guilt/shame; reinforce they could not have anticipated it and did not deserve it
🩺

CARE

STEP 3 · PACE & PROCESS

Let the client set the pace, normalize the reaction, and lean on trauma-focused therapy before medication.

✅ Nursing priority ladder

1
Safety screen first — self-harm/suicidal ideation, every encounter
2
Encourage the client to talk at their own pace — never force detail before they're ready
3
Explain that difficult symptoms after trauma are normal — normalize, don't pathologize the reaction
4
Assess substance use and relationships — common coping/withdrawal patterns
5
Connect to trauma-focused therapy — CBT, EMDR, or group therapy as tolerated
🧠 “Pace, Place, Process.” Let them set the pace, keep them safe in place, then help them process — in that order.

⭐ Trauma-focused therapy — first-line, non-drug

Trauma-focused CBT — helps the client reframe distorted trauma-related beliefs (guilt, danger, self-blame) and gradually process memories.

EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing) — the client recalls the trauma in brief segments while following bilateral stimulation (e.g., guided eye movements); thought to help the brain reprocess the memory so it feels less distressing.

Prolonged exposure therapy — structured, repeated recounting of the memory in a safe setting to reduce its emotional charge over time.

🧠 CBT is a type of talk therapy that helps clients reframe thought processes to prevent negative thought patterns and adapt to stress and anxiety.

💊 Pharmacology

Drug classNote
SSRIs — sertraline, paroxetineFDA-approved, first-line pharmacologic option
TCAs — amitriptyline, imipramineOlder alternative, more side effects
PrazosinOff-label, targets trauma-related nightmares specifically
🧠 Avoid long-term benzodiazepines in PTSD — dependence risk, and evidence doesn't support them as an effective core treatment; they don't treat the underlying avoidance/re-experiencing symptoms.

🚨 Never do this with a trauma client

  • Never push the client to describe trauma details before they're ready
  • Never minimize the reaction ("it was years ago, just move on")
  • Never assume every trauma survivor's symptoms look the same
  • ✅ Do reinforce: the feelings are a normal response to an abnormal event
  • ✅ For assault survivors: reinforce they could not have anticipated it and did not deserve it

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What separates Acute Stress Disorder from PTSD?
Duration. ASD occurs within the first month after trauma; if symptoms persist beyond one month, it's PTSD.
Q2: Name the 4 PTSD symptom clusters.
Intrusion/re-experiencing, avoidance, negative alterations in mood/cognition, and hyperarousal/reactivity.
Q3: A veteran expresses survivor's guilt about friends killed in combat. What's the nurse's priority response?
Ask directly: "Are you having any thoughts of harming yourself?" Safety assessment always comes first.
Q4: What is first-line, non-drug treatment for PTSD?
Trauma-focused CBT, with EMDR as another evidence-based option; benzodiazepines are not recommended long-term.

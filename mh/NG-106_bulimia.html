Bulimia Nervosa 🔄

A serious, treatable eating disorder — the electrolytes are the emergency

NG-106 Mental Health ADHD-friendly visual edition

Bulimia nervosa is defined by recurrent episodes of binge eating followed by compensatory behaviors to prevent weight gain — self-induced vomiting, laxative or diuretic misuse, fasting, or excessive exercise. Clients often maintain a normal or near-normal body weight, which can hide the diagnosis. The #1 nursing priority is fluid & electrolyte monitoring — especially hypokalemia, because it can trigger a fatal cardiac dysrhythmia.

CAUSE

STEP 1 · THE CYCLE

One repeating cycle explains the whole disorder — and it's driven by a distorted body image, not appetite.

🔄 Pathophysiology: the binge–compensate cycle

EXAM TIP Bulimia nervosa involves 2 linked phases: 1) recurrent episodes of binge eating — consuming a large amount of food with a sense of loss of control — 2) followed by compensatory behavior to prevent weight gain, such as self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives/diuretics, fasting, or excessive exercise. Underneath it all sits a distorted body image and an obsessive drive to control weight.

🧠 "Binge, then bounce back the weight." The compensatory step is what defines bulimia — it's the attempt to "undo" the binge, and it's what drives every physical complication on this page.

🧠 What's underneath it

  • Distorted body image — perception doesn't match reality
  • Obsessive desire to control or lose weight
  • Often coexists with anxiety, depression, or low self-esteem
🧠 The behaviors are about control, not hunger — treat the underlying emotional driver, not just the eating pattern.

⚖️ Bulimia vs. Anorexia — don't confuse them

Bulimia NervosaAnorexia Nervosa
Body weightUsually normal or near-normalSignificantly low for age/height
PatternBinge eating + compensatory behaviorSevere, sustained food restriction
InsightOften feels shame/guilt, more likely to recognize the behavior is a problemFrequently denies the severity of low weight
🧠 "Bulimia hides in a normal-looking body." Don't rule out bulimia just because weight looks unremarkable — this is the exact trap the exam sets.
🔎

CLUES

STEP 2 · SPOT IT

The body keeps the score — dental, glandular, and electrolyte clues, plus the environmental evidence.

🩺 Physical exam findings

🧠 "Teeth, cheeks, knuckles, skin." These four findings appear even when the scale gives no clue at all.

🚨 Highest-yield lab: hypokalemia

Repeated vomiting and laxative/diuretic misuse drain potassium, chloride, and hydrogen ions, producing hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis.

🧠 "K+ below normal = call it a cardiac problem." Hypokalemia is the single most dangerous complication of bulimia — it can trigger a fatal dysrhythmia.

🔍 Hidden evidence — assessment clues

  • Hidden or discarded food wrappers
  • Laxative or diuretic boxes found discarded
  • Frequent bathroom trips right after meals
  • Weight fluctuations without an obvious dietary explanation
🧠 The evidence is usually environmental, not verbal — clients rarely disclose these behaviors on their own.

😌 A therapeutic, non-judgmental approach

Bulimia is a medical and psychiatric condition, not a choice or a moral failing. Approach assessment and conversation with the same clinical neutrality used for any other disease — avoid commenting on weight, food choices, or appearance, and focus questions on feelings, stress, and control.

🧠 Sample short-term goal (ATI): "Verbalizing the desire to increase control over stressful situations" — reframes the goal around coping, not weight.
🩺

CARE

STEP 3 · STABILIZE & TREAT

Electrolytes first, structure second, therapy for the long haul — and watch for refeeding syndrome if nutrition rehab begins.

🚨 #1 priority: monitor fluid & electrolyte balance

  • 🧪 Hypokalemia (K⁺ < 3.5 mEq/L) → cardiac dysrhythmia risk — obtain baseline ECG, trend labs
  • 🧪 Watch chloride and acid-base status — vomiting causes metabolic alkalosis
  • 💧 Assess for dehydration from vomiting, laxative, or diuretic misuse
🧠 "Electrolytes before everything." A bulimic client can look medically stable and still be in cardiac danger from their potassium level alone.

⚠️ Refeeding syndrome — risk during nutritional rehabilitation

If treatment includes structured nutritional rehabilitation after a period of chaotic or restricted intake, the reintroduction of food can trigger refeeding syndrome: an insulin surge drives potassium, phosphate, and magnesium into cells, causing a dangerous drop in serum levels.

See NG-298 (TPN & Enteral Feeding) for the full refeeding syndrome electrolyte workup — same mechanism applies here whenever malnourished clients begin structured nutrition rehabilitation.

🧠 "Feeding too fast can crash the labs." Start nutrition slowly and monitor phosphate, potassium, and magnesium closely in the first days of rehab.

🍽️ Structure the eating environment

1
👀 1-on-1 supervision during meals
2
⏱️ Supervise 1–2 hours after each meal to prevent compensatory behavior
3
📓 Food diary during hospitalization to track intake and patterns
4
🍽️ Regular, structured meals — helps break the binge/compensate cycle
🧠 "Watch the meal, then watch the hour after." The compensatory behavior almost always happens right after eating.

💊 Pharmacology

DrugRole
Fluoxetine (SSRI)FDA-approved pharmacologic option for bulimia nervosa
Bupropion (Wellbutrin)Avoided — associated with increased seizure risk in bulimia nervosa
🧠 "Fluoxetine yes, Bupropion no." If bupropion shows up as an answer choice for bulimia, it's the trap, not the treatment.

✅ Psychotherapy & the therapeutic relationship

  • Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is the first-line, evidence-based psychotherapy for bulimia nervosa
  • Explore stress, control, and coping — the eating pattern is a symptom, not the root problem
  • Approach with a calm, non-judgmental, therapeutic tone — shame worsens the cycle
  • Coordinate with dietitian, therapist, and medical team for a whole-person plan
🧠 "Treat the person, not the plate." Sustainable recovery comes from addressing the emotional drivers alongside the physical stabilization.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What electrolyte imbalance is the priority to monitor in bulimia nervosa, and why?
Hypokalemia (K⁺ below 3.5 mEq/L) — it can cause life-threatening cardiac dysrhythmias.
Q2: A client with bulimia nervosa has a normal body weight. Does that rule out the diagnosis?
No — clients with bulimia often maintain a normal or near-normal body weight, unlike anorexia nervosa. Don't rule it out based on appearance.
Q3: What callused marks on the knuckles are associated with repeated self-induced vomiting?
Russell's sign.
Q4: Why is bupropion (Wellbutrin) generally avoided in bulimia nervosa?
It is associated with an increased seizure risk in this population — fluoxetine (an SSRI) is the FDA-approved medication option instead.

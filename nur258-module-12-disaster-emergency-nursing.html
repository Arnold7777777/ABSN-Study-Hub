Legal scope, safety and sentinel events

The applicable state/territory Nurse Practice Act and board rules define nursing scope; competence, role, delegation rules and employer policy further constrain an assignment. A disaster does not automatically authorize unfamiliar invasive care or waive licensure rules. Identify formal emergency orders/waivers, use the incident command chain and request qualified supervision or a safe reassignment. Emergency duties also include timely screening/stabilization under applicable law, consent/capacity assessment, privacy, accurate contemporaneous documentation and required reporting. A sentinel event is a serious patient-safety event meeting Joint Commission criteria, including death, permanent harm or severe harm; certain listed events qualify regardless of outcome. Stabilize the patient, preserve facts, notify through the safety process and participate in systems analysis rather than concealment or blame.

Sources: NCSBN Nurse Practice Acts guide · Joint Commission sentinel events; Brunner & Suddarth and the supplied ATI adult medical-surgical/nutrition texts, reconciled with the current guidance linked here.

Primary survey, multisystem trauma and abdominal injury

Use the trauma team’s current survey protocol: control catastrophic external hemorrhage promptly, protect the cervical spine when indicated, assess airway, breathing, circulation, neurologic disability and full exposure with warming. Treat threats as identified and repeat the survey after every deterioration or intervention. The secondary survey adds mechanism, allergies/medications/history and head-to-toe assessment once immediate threats are addressed. Abdominal bleeding can be occult: trend perfusion, mental status, abdominal findings, urine output and serial labs; a normal initial hemoglobin does not exclude acute hemorrhage. FAST/CT or operative evaluation depends on stability and mechanism. Keep the patient NPO, secure access, prepare blood products/procedures and do not remove an impaled object; support it in place.

Brunner & Suddarth and the supplied ATI adult medical-surgical/nutrition texts, reconciled with the current guidance linked here.

Wound assessment, cleaning and closure

Assess mechanism/time, contamination, foreign body, tissue viability, neurovascular/tendon function and tetanus history; control bleeding and provide analgesia. Irrigate with appropriate fluid and remove foreign material/debride devitalized tissue as ordered. Primary closure approximates a suitably clean, viable wound promptly after evaluation; delayed primary closure leaves a contaminated/high-risk wound open initially for cleaning and reassessment, then closes it when infection is absent and conditions permit. Secondary intention is healing without later primary closure. Nursing care includes dressing/protection, pain management, infection surveillance and follow-up instructions; closure decisions are not based on a single universal hour cutoff.

Brunner & Suddarth and the supplied ATI adult medical-surgical/nutrition texts, reconciled with the current guidance linked here.

Tetanus prophylaxis

With a completed primary series, give a booster at 10 years for clean minor wounds or 5 years for dirty/major wounds; no booster is needed if the last dose was under 5 years. Unknown/incomplete vaccination requires vaccination for any wound. Tetanus immune globulin is never indicated for a clean minor wound and is used for selected dirty/major wounds, including unknown/incomplete history or severe immunodeficiency. Clean/debride the wound; antibiotics are not tetanus prophylaxis.

Sources: CDC tetanus wound prophylaxis; Brunner & Suddarth and the supplied ATI adult medical-surgical/nutrition texts, reconciled with the current guidance linked here.

Crush injury and chest-tube priorities

Crush injury can release potassium and myoglobin, causing dysrhythmias, shock, compartment syndrome and acute kidney injury. Coordinate safe extrication and begin resuscitation early when feasible rather than waiting for release; monitor ECG, potassium, renal function, urine, CK and limb neurovascular status. Treat hyperkalemia/compartment syndrome urgently through the trauma team. For a chest tube, assess the patient before the device, keep drainage below chest level and maintain a closed unobstructed system. Intermittent water-seal bubbling can occur as air evacuates; persistent bubbling suggests a leak requiring systematic assessment. Sudden respiratory distress, disconnection or dislodgement needs immediate assistance and the facility’s emergency protocol; do not routinely clamp a bubbling tube.

Brunner & Suddarth and the supplied ATI adult medical-surgical/nutrition texts, reconciled with the current guidance linked here.

Food poisoning

Ask what and when the patient ate, who else is ill, travel/exposure history, stool/vomit features and neurologic symptoms. Most presentations involve vomiting, diarrhea and cramps, but dehydration, bloody stool, high fever or high-risk hosts need further evaluation. Prioritize hydration/electrolytes, renal status and ordered stool/blood tests; use oral rehydration when tolerated and IV fluid when needed. Antibiotics and antidiarrheals are organism- and patient-specific, not routine for every illness. Diplopia, dysphagia, descending weakness or respiratory difficulty after food exposure raises concern for botulism and requires urgent airway/public-health/antitoxin evaluation. Teach hand hygiene, food safety and return precautions; report suspected clusters through the local public-health process.

Brunner & Suddarth and the supplied ATI adult medical-surgical/nutrition texts, reconciled with the current guidance linked here.

Sexual assault, trafficking and psychiatric emergencies

Provide private, trauma-informed care and address urgent injuries first. Obtain consent separately for examination, evidence collection and treatment; explain options and preserve forensic evidence/chain of custody when chosen, without making treatment conditional on a police report. Offer specialist forensic care, pregnancy/STI/HIV risk evaluation and time-sensitive prevention, advocacy and follow-up. For suspected trafficking, speak privately with a qualified interpreter, assess immediate safety without confronting a suspected trafficker and follow applicable age/safety/reporting requirements. For agitation or suicidality, assess medical causes, intoxication and immediate danger; use calm de-escalation and the least restrictive safe intervention. Ask directly about suicidal thoughts, intent, plan and access to means; institute indicated observation and environmental precautions, stay with an imminently unsafe patient, obtain urgent behavioral-health help and complete a direct handoff.

Brunner & Suddarth and the supplied ATI adult medical-surgical/nutrition texts, reconciled with the current guidance linked here.

Disaster ethics and allocation

Disaster allocation balances benefit, fairness, proportionality and available resources. Apply the approved triage/resource-allocation framework consistently; do not rank people by social worth or make unsupported bedside exclusions. Use incident command/ethics consultation for conflicts, document reasons and reassess as resources change. Expectant classification still requires comfort, dignity and reassessment. Nurses also face duty-to-care, personal/family safety and role-boundary conflicts; advance planning, appropriate PPE and a clear escalation process make these decisions safer.

Sources: HHS CHEMM SALT mass-casualty triage; Brunner & Suddarth and the supplied ATI adult medical-surgical/nutrition texts, reconciled with the current guidance linked here.

Outbreak, epidemic, pandemic and natural disasters

An outbreak or epidemic is disease occurrence above what is expected in a population; outbreak often describes a more localized event. A pandemic describes spread across countries/continents, not a guarantee of greater severity. Nursing actions are early recognition of unusual clusters, exposure history, prompt infection prevention, appropriate specimens, reporting and clear risk communication. Natural-disaster planning anticipates injury, unsafe water, displacement, medication interruption, extremes of temperature and access barriers; plan power-dependent equipment, dialysis/oxygen needs, shelter accessibility and family reunification. Use facility incident command and local public-health direction. ASPR manages the Strategic National Stockpile; CDC provides public-health expertise and surveillance support.

Sources: CDC principles of epidemiology: outbreak, epidemic, pandemic · ASPR Center for the Strategic National Stockpile; Brunner & Suddarth and the supplied ATI adult medical-surgical/nutrition texts, reconciled with the current guidance linked here.

Vesicants

Vesicants damage skin, eyes and respiratory mucosa. Sulfur mustard may cause delayed erythema, blisters and eye/airway injury; absence of immediate pain does not exclude exposure. Lewisite can cause immediate pain and systemic arsenic toxicity. Protect rescuers, remove contaminated clothing and follow agent-specific decontamination without vigorous rubbing; prevent hypothermia. Irrigate exposed eyes and provide airway, burn, pain and fluid support as indicated. Sulfur mustard has no specific antidote; lewisite treatment requires specialist toxicology guidance. Phosgene is a pulmonary agent and is different from phosgene oxime.

Sources: CDC sulfur mustard · CDC/NIOSH mustard-lewisite emergency response; Brunner & Suddarth and the supplied ATI adult medical-surgical/nutrition texts, reconciled with the current guidance linked here.

Decontamination purpose and process

Decontamination removes the agent, limits continuing injury and prevents contamination of staff, equipment and the facility. Use controlled hot/warm/cold zones, hazard-appropriate PPE and trained personnel. Remove/bag contaminated clothing; choose dry removal or water-based decontamination according to the agent and exposure, protecting privacy, wounds, eyes and temperature. Prevent contaminated runoff and uncontrolled entry to clean areas. Airway support, major bleeding control and indicated antidotes may need to occur alongside decontamination with suitable responder protection; do not delay all lifesaving treatment until washing is finished.

Sources: HHS CHEMM SALT mass-casualty triage · CDC sulfur mustard; Brunner & Suddarth and the supplied ATI adult medical-surgical/nutrition texts, reconciled with the current guidance linked here.

💡 The one idea

Disaster triage inverts everyday nursing. Normally the sickest patient goes first. In a mass casualty you do the greatest good for the greatest number — so the most critically injured may be passed over.

▸🚑Triage and Mass Casualty ResponseThe rules invertOpenClose
📖Definition and Overview

Sorting clients so that limited resources do the most good. Everyday emergency department triage treats the sickest person first. Mass casualty triage does the opposite: it treats those most likely to survive with the resources available, and gives comfort care only to those who will not. That inversion — from 'sickest first' to 'greatest good for the greatest number' — is the single most tested idea in this module.

⚠️Causes and Risk Factors

A mass casualty incident is any event where the casualties overwhelm what is available: natural disasters, transport crashes, fires, explosions, shootings, industrial accidents, chemical release, and pandemics. What makes an event a disaster is the mismatch between need and resources, not the raw number of casualties.

🩺Clinical Manifestations

In everyday emergent (red) triage: airway compromise, respiratory distress, chest pain with cardiac features, active hemorrhage, altered consciousness, stroke symptoms, unstable vital signs. Urgent (yellow): severe pain, open fractures, moderate bleeding, high fever. Non-urgent (green): sprains, minor wounds, chronic complaints. In mass casualty START triage the assessment is deliberately crude and fast: can they walk (green); respiratory rate over 30; capillary refill over 2 seconds or absent radial pulse; can they follow a simple command.

🔬Assessment and Diagnostic Findings

Use the adopted disaster-triage algorithm and current resources, not fixed diagnoses or promised waiting times. Perform designated rapid lifesaving interventions and reassess. SALT separates dead from expectant; expectant patients still receive comfort care and may be reclassified. Everyday emergency triage follows a different system.

💊Medical Management

Incident command activates the facility's emergency operations plan. Decontamination before entry where indicated. Surge capacity: discharge or transfer stable inpatients, cancel elective surgery, open alternative care areas, call in staff. Definitive care follows priority order. Family reunification and behavioral health support are part of the plan, not an afterthought.

🩹Nursing Management and Client Education

Know where your facility's disaster plan is and what your role in it is before the day it is needed. Tag, document briefly, and keep moving — the commonest failure is a nurse stopping to give full care to the first casualty they meet. Reassess the yellow and green groups regularly. Delegate within scope: the walking wounded can be put to work holding pressure and comforting others. Expect and plan for the psychological toll on staff — debriefing and critical incident stress support are standard care, not weakness. Teach families about personal preparedness: a kit with water, food, medications, copies of prescriptions and documents, a plan for meeting, and an out-of-area contact.

▸☢️Bioterrorism AgentsOpenClose
📖Definition and Overview

Deliberate release of biological agents to cause harm. The Category A agents are the priority ones — easily disseminated or transmitted person to person, high mortality, and capable of causing public panic: anthrax, smallpox, plague, botulism, tularaemia and the viral hemorrhagic fevers. Recognizing an outbreak early is a nursing function, because nurses see the first cases.

⚠️Causes and Risk Factors

Deliberate release through air, water, food or mail. Suspect it when there is a cluster of unusual illness — the wrong disease, in the wrong season, in the wrong population, in unexpected numbers, or a disease not seen in the area at all. Health care workers, first responders and laboratory staff are at particular risk.

🩺Clinical Manifestations

Inhalational anthrax: flu-like illness for a few days, then abrupt severe respiratory distress and shock, with a widened mediastinum on chest x-ray. Not spread person to person. Smallpox: high fever and prostration, then a rash that starts on the face and limbs and spreads inward, with all lesions at the same stage — that is what distinguishes it from chickenpox, where lesions are at different stages and start centrally. Pneumonic plague: abrupt fever, cough, hemoptysis, rapid respiratory failure. Botulism: descending symmetrical flaccid paralysis with double vision, drooping lids, slurred speech and difficulty swallowing, and a fully alert client with no fever.

🔬Assessment and Diagnostic Findings

Report a suspected case to the health department immediately — notification is part of the assessment, not something that happens later. Cultures and specific testing through public health laboratories; chest x-ray; and a careful exposure history including travel, occupation, mail and contacts. Continuous respiratory and neurological assessment, since these agents kill through the airway.

💊Medical Management

Anthrax: ciprofloxacin or doxycycline, for treatment and for post-exposure prophylaxis of contacts. Smallpox: vaccination within 4 days of exposure can prevent or lessen disease; supportive care otherwise. Plague: streptomycin, gentamicin or doxycycline, with prophylaxis for close contacts. Botulism: antitoxin, which stops progression but does not reverse existing paralysis, plus ventilatory support that may last weeks.

🩹Nursing Management and Client Education

Match the precautions to the agent and get them on before the diagnosis is confirmed. Smallpox and viral hemorrhagic fevers need airborne and contact precautions; pneumonic plague needs droplet precautions; anthrax and botulism need standard precautions, because neither spreads person to person. Decontaminate before the client enters the department where there is a powder or liquid exposure: remove clothing (which removes most of the contamination), bag it as evidence, and shower with soap and water. Wear appropriate protective equipment and do not improvise it. Do not enter a contaminated area without training and protection — a contaminated rescuer becomes a second casualty and contaminates the department. Support clients through the isolation and the fear, and teach that prophylactic antibiotics must be completed in full — for anthrax, that is 60 days.

▸☠️Poisoning and OverdoseOpenClose
📖Definition and Overview

Injury from a substance taken by mouth, inhaled, injected or absorbed, whether accidentally or deliberately. The immediate work is support the airway, breathing and circulation; identify the substance; limit further absorption; and give an antidote if one exists — and most poisons have no antidote, so supportive care is the treatment.

⚠️Causes and Risk Factors

In children, unsecured household products and medicines. In adolescents and adults, deliberate self-harm and recreational drug use. In older adults, therapeutic error — a duplicate dose, a confused regimen, or a drug interaction. Also carbon monoxide from faulty heating, occupational chemical exposure, plants and mushrooms, and alcohol.

🩺Clinical Manifestations

Depends entirely on the agent, so learn the patterns. Opioids: pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression, coma. Anticholinergics: hot, dry, flushed, dilated pupils, confused, retention — 'mad as a hatter, dry as a bone'. Cholinergics or organophosphates: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI upset, emesis, plus bradycardia and small pupils. Acetaminophen: nothing much for the first 24 hours, then nausea and right upper quadrant pain, then liver failure — a well-looking client after an overdose is not reassuring. Salicylates: tinnitus, hyperventilation, fever, then mixed acid–base disturbance. Carbon monoxide: headache, nausea, confusion, with a falsely normal pulse oximetry reading.

🔬Assessment and Diagnostic Findings

Call the poison control center — they direct management, and the number belongs in your teaching too. Establish what, how much, when and by what route, and look for the container. Acetaminophen level at 4 hours post-ingestion plotted on the Rumack–Matthew nomogram. Salicylate level, ABGs, electrolytes and anion gap, glucose, creatinine, liver function, ECG, and a carboxyhemoglobin level where relevant. Continuous monitoring of airway, breathing, circulation and level of consciousness.

💊Medical Management

Support first: airway, oxygen, IV access, glucose, and treat seizures. Limit absorption with activated charcoal, useful within about an hour and only in an alert client with a protected airway; whole-bowel irrigation for some agents; and copious irrigation for skin or eye exposure. Antidotes to know: naloxone for opioids, N-acetylcysteine for acetaminophen, flumazenil for benzodiazepines (used cautiously), atropine and pralidoxime for organophosphates, and 100% oxygen for carbon monoxide. Sodium bicarbonate for salicylates and tricyclics. Hemodialysis for some.

🩹Nursing Management and Client Education

Airway before antidote. Position on the side if the client is drowsy and vomiting. Do not induce vomiting, and do not give syrup of ipecac — it is no longer recommended, and it is dangerous with corrosives and hydrocarbons. Naloxone wears off before most opioids do, so keep monitoring for re-sedation and expect acute withdrawal — agitation, vomiting, pain — when it works. Give N-acetylcysteine even if the client looks well; it is most effective within 8 hours. Save containers and specimens; document precisely, since this may become a legal matter. Every intentional overdose gets a suicide risk assessment, one-to-one observation and a mental health referral — do not treat the poisoning and discharge the person. Teach prevention: the poison control number on the fridge, locked and original containers, safe storage away from food, working carbon monoxide detectors, and a medication review for older adults.

▸🌡️Environmental EmergenciesOpenClose
📖Definition and Overview

Injuries caused by heat, cold, water or animals. They share a pattern: remove the person from the exposure, correct the core problem gradually rather than abruptly, and expect the body to behave badly while you do. Heat and cold emergencies in particular are far more dangerous in the very young, the very old and the chronically ill.

⚠️Causes and Risk Factors

Heat: exertion in high heat and humidity, age extremes, obesity, dehydration, alcohol, cardiovascular disease, and drugs that impair sweating — anticholinergics, antihistamines, beta blockers, diuretics and antipsychotics. Cold: homelessness, alcohol, immersion, inadequate clothing, hypothyroidism, immobility and age extremes. Drowning: inability to swim, alcohol, seizures, and unsupervised children near water. Bites and stings: outdoor exposure; known allergy makes a sting an anaphylaxis risk.

🩺Clinical Manifestations

Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache, tachycardia, normal or slightly raised temperature and an intact mental state. Heat stroke: core temperature above 104°F (40°C), hot skin that is often dry because sweating has stopped, and altered mental status — confusion, seizures, coma. That change in mental status is what separates heat stroke from heat exhaustion, and it is a true emergency. Hypothermia: shivering that stops as it worsens, slurred speech, clumsiness, confusion, bradycardia and bradypnoea, then rigidity and apparent death. Frostbite: white waxy hard skin, numb at first and intensely painful on rewarming.

🔬Assessment and Diagnostic Findings

Core temperature with a low-reading or esophageal thermometer — an ordinary oral thermometer cannot measure a hypothermic client. Continuous cardiac monitoring, because the cold heart is irritable and prone to ventricular fibrillation. Electrolytes, glucose, creatine kinase for rhabdomyolysis in heat stroke, coagulation studies, ABGs, and hourly urine output. Neurological status throughout.

💊Medical Management

Heat stroke: cool rapidly — remove clothing, cold water immersion or evaporative cooling with mist and fans, ice packs to the groin, axillae and neck, cooled IV fluids, and stop active cooling around 102°F (39°C) to avoid overshoot. Hypothermia: rewarm gradually — passive external warming for mild cases, active external and then core rewarming with warmed fluids, humidified oxygen and lavage for severe ones. Frostbite: rapid rewarming in circulating water at 98–104°F, analgesia, tetanus prophylaxis, and delayed debridement.

🩹Nursing Management and Client Education

Do not give antipyretics for heat stroke — the hypothalamic set point is normal, so paracetamol and aspirin do nothing and add liver and bleeding risk. Handle a hypothermic client gently: rough movement can trigger ventricular fibrillation. Nobody is dead until they are warm and dead — continue resuscitation through rewarming. For frostbite, never rub or massage the tissue, and never rewarm if there is any chance of refreezing — a thaw–refreeze cycle destroys more tissue than staying frozen. Monitor for compartment syndrome and rhabdomyolysis. Teaching is prevention: hydration and pacing in heat, checking on older neighbours during a heatwave, layered clothing and covered extremities in cold, life jackets and constant supervision around water, and an auto-injector for anyone with a known sting allergy.

▸📋Emergency Preparedness and the Nurse's RoleOpenClose
📖Definition and Overview

The planning, training and legal framework that lets a facility function when the day comes. It runs in four phases — mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery — and it is organized through the Incident Command System, a defined chain of command with one person in charge and a clear role for everybody else.

⚠️Causes and Risk Factors

Every facility is required to have a plan and to exercise it. The things that make a response fail are predictable: staff who do not know their role, communication that breaks down, supplies and surge capacity that were never counted, no plan for staff families, and no provision for the psychological aftermath.

🩺Clinical Manifestations

What a failing response looks like: nobody knows who is in command, staff self-deploy to where they feel useful rather than where they are assigned, documentation stops, families cannot find their people, and staff work past exhaustion without relief. Afterwards, watch for acute stress reactions and post-traumatic stress in staff and clients alike — sleep disturbance, intrusive memories, irritability, withdrawal and guilt.

🔬Assessment and Diagnostic Findings

Know before the event: where the plan is, what your assigned role is, where supplies and personal protective equipment are kept, the evacuation routes, and how communication works when phones and networks fail. During the event, assess continually: casualty numbers and categories, staffing, supplies, bed capacity and safety of the environment. Afterwards, the debrief is an assessment too — what worked and what did not.

💊Medical Management

Activation of the emergency operations plan and incident command. Surge capacity: early discharge, cancelling elective work, opening alternative sites. Mutual aid agreements between facilities. Under crisis standards of care, the standard shifts explicitly from the individual to the population, and that shift is declared, documented and time-limited — not improvised at the bedside. Recovery includes restoring services, restocking, and reviewing the response formally.

🩹Nursing Management and Client Education

Your role in the plan is something to know now, not to look up during the event. Follow the chain of command and do not self-deploy. Have a personal and family plan — childcare, elder care, pets, transport — because a nurse who cannot leave home safely cannot come to work. Document even briefly; triage tags and times matter afterwards. Delegate within scope and use unlicensed personnel and volunteers deliberately. Take the breaks, eat, drink and accept relief — and hold colleagues to the same, because fatigue causes the errors. Afterwards, attend the debriefing and take critical incident stress support seriously; distress after a disaster is a normal response to an abnormal event, and untreated it ends careers. Teach clients and families household preparedness: an emergency kit, at least a week of medication, copies of prescriptions and documents, a meeting place, and an out-of-area contact who is easier to reach than a local one.

👀 How it shows upAssessment — Risk Factors · Assessment — Expected Findings
Assessment — Risk Factors
  • Trauma victims risk hypothermia from exposure, cold oxygen, and cold IV fluids.
  • Children under 5 years old face the highest accidental poisoning risk.
  • Mid-to-late childhood kids face the highest snakebite risk.
  • Vulnerable clients face higher risk of trafficking exploitation.
  • A history of substance misuse raises trafficking vulnerability.
Assessment — Expected Findings
  • Hypothermia: shivering, impaired judgment, dysarthria, and drowsiness are common signs.
  • Heatstroke: temperature above 40° C (104° F) with altered mental status.
  • Heatstroke may show abnormal blood potassium or sodium levels.
  • Frostbite severity may not be clear until up to 24 hr after injury.
  • Full-thickness frostbite shows dark blisters, necrosis, and possible eschar.
  • Deep-tissue frostbite can cause gangrene and may require amputation.
  • Opioid poisoning: pinpoint pupils, low blood pressure, respiratory depression, and hypoxia.
  • Snakebite: pain, nausea, vomiting, numbness, and paresthesia at the site.
🧪 How it is confirmedLaboratory Tests · Diagnostic Procedures
Laboratory Tests
  • Point-of-care glucose on every altered mental status — it is fast, reversible, and missed embarrassingly often.
  • Type and crossmatch early in trauma; do not wait for the hemoglobin.
  • Lactate for occult shock and sepsis; arterial blood gas for ventilation.
  • Troponin, electrolytes, renal function, coagulation studies and toxicology as indicated.
  • Pregnancy test in any person of childbearing potential before imaging or medication.
Diagnostic Procedures
  • Primary survey: A B C D E — airway with cervical spine, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure. Fix each before moving on.
  • Secondary survey — head-to-toe, plus a full history.
  • Triage sorts by acuity, not arrival. In mass casualty the priority inverts to the greatest good for the greatest number.
  • FAST ultrasound for free fluid; imaging directed by mechanism.
  • Continuous monitoring — ECG, oximetry, capnography.
🩺 What you doNursing Care · Medications · Therapeutic Procedures
Nursing Care
  • Airway comes first; brain injury or death can occur within 3 to 5 min without a patent airway.
  • Use head-tilt/chin-lift only when no cervical spine injury is suspected.
  • Use a modified jaw thrust if trauma is suspected, to protect the spine.
  • Apply direct pressure for bleeding first; use a tourniquet proximally if that fails.
  • Insert large-bore IVs in both antecubital areas unless a limb is injured.
  • A GCS under 8 means serious brain injury; a full score of 15 is expected.
  • AVPU is a quick consciousness check: Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive.
  • Contact poison control at 800-222-1222 for suspected poisoning.
  • Gastric lavage is only useful within 1 hr of ingestion.
Medications
  • Epinephrine IM for anaphylaxis, first and immediately. Never delay it for an antihistamine or a steroid.
  • Naloxone for opioid toxicity; dextrose for hypoglycemia.
  • Isotonic fluid for shock; blood products in hemorrhage — and blood, not crystalloid, is the resuscitation fluid for bleeding.
  • Tetanus prophylaxis for wounds; analgesia early rather than withheld pending diagnosis.
  • Reversal agents: vitamin K and prothrombin complex for warfarin, idarucizumab for dabigatran.
Therapeutic Procedures

  • Triage nurses use the Emergency Severity Index to sort patients into five acuity levels within about a minute.
  • Level 1 marks a patient who needs an immediate life-saving intervention such as airway or hemodynamic support.
  • Level 2 covers high-risk, confused, or severely distressed patients, or stable patients with markedly abnormal vital signs.
  • Levels 3 through 5 are set by how many hospital resources the visit is expected to require.

Client Education
  • Wear lightweight, loose clothing and avoid excess sun to prevent hyperthermia.
  • Stay indoors with fans or AC during periods of high heat.

1. Emergency nursing principles

Emergency nursing involves rapid assessment, stabilization, treatment, reassessment, and coordination of care for patients with actual or potential life-threatening conditions.

The emergency nurse must:

  • Recognize life-threatening conditions immediately.
  • Prioritize care based on physiological instability rather than arrival order.
  • Initiate life-saving interventions within nursing scope and established protocols.
  • Coordinate care with emergency physicians, respiratory therapists, trauma teams, pharmacists, and other specialists.
  • Communicate with patients and families during stressful or traumatic situations.
  • Continuously reassess because emergency patients can deteriorate rapidly.

Special populations:

  • Older adults: May have atypical symptoms, multiple medications, decreased physiological reserve, and greater risk for hypothermia or shock.
  • Patients with obesity: May present airway-management challenges, require appropriately sized equipment, and have increased risk of respiratory compromise.
  • Family members: Provide history, medication information, and emotional support. Include them when appropriate without interfering with emergency care.

2. Emergency department triage

Triage means to sort patients according to the urgency of their condition.

The priority is the patient with the greatest immediate threat to life, not necessarily the patient experiencing the most pain.

The primary survey is the foundation of emergency nursing.

Assess and treat immediate life threats as they are identified. Repeat the survey after interventions or any deterioration.

For catastrophic external hemorrhage, trauma protocols may use XABCDE or C-ABC , prioritizing immediate control of exsanguinating bleeding while the team manages airway and breathing.

A — Airway and cervical spine protection

Assessment:

  • Can the patient speak?
  • Is there stridor, gurgling, snoring, or absent airflow?
  • Is blood, vomit, or another obstruction present?
  • Are facial injuries or swelling compromising the airway?

Interventions:

  • Maintain cervical spine stabilization in suspected trauma.
  • Use jaw-thrust maneuver for suspected spinal injury.
  • Suction blood or secretions.
  • Provide appropriate airway adjuncts and prepare for intubation if indicated.

NCLEX red flags:

  • Stridor = upper airway obstruction.
  • Gurgling = secretions or fluid in airway; suction.
  • Snoring in an unconscious patient = possible tongue obstruction.
  • Decreased consciousness = possible loss of airway protective reflexes.

Important: A Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 8 or lower indicates severe neurological impairment and prompts urgent airway assessment, but the decision to intubate also depends on ventilation, oxygenation, and airway protection.

B — Breathing

Assess respiratory rate, depth, effort, bilateral breath sounds, chest expansion, oxygen saturation, and signs of chest injury.

Emergency interventions:

  • Administer oxygen as indicated.
  • Assist ventilation using a bag-valve-mask if inadequate.
  • Prepare for endotracheal intubation if needed.
  • Recognize and immediately escalate suspected tension pneumothorax.

Critical distinction: Oxygenation and ventilation are different. A patient can have an acceptable oxygen saturation while inadequately ventilating and retaining carbon dioxide.

C — Circulation

Assess heart rate, blood pressure, peripheral pulses, capillary refill, skin temperature/color, mental status, and bleeding.

Priority interventions:

  • Control life-threatening external hemorrhage with direct pressure or a tourniquet when indicated.
  • Establish two large-bore IV lines for significant trauma or hemorrhage when feasible.
  • Administer blood products for major hemorrhagic shock according to protocol.
  • Monitor ECG and perfusion.
  • Prepare for definitive bleeding control, including surgery.

NCLEX red flag

Hypotension is often a late sign of hemorrhagic shock. Tachycardia, cool clammy skin, delayed capillary refill, and altered mental status may appear earlier.

D — Disability

Perform a rapid neurological assessment.

AVPU:

  • A: Alert
  • V: Responds to verbal stimulation
  • P: Responds to painful stimulation
  • U: Unresponsive

Also assess pupil response, movement, blood glucose, and GCS when appropriate.

GCS scoreSeverity
13–15Mild impairment
9–12Moderate impairment
3–8Severe impairment

NCLEX: Sudden decrease in GCS is an emergency requiring immediate reassessment of airway, oxygenation, perfusion, and possible intracranial pathology.

E — Exposure and environment

Remove clothing to inspect for hidden injuries, then prevent heat loss.

  • Examine anterior and posterior surfaces while maintaining spinal precautions.
  • Remove wet clothing.
  • Provide warm blankets and warmed IV fluids when indicated.
  • Maintain patient dignity and privacy.

Key concept: Trauma patients are vulnerable to the lethal triad of hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy , which worsens bleeding and mortality.

4. Secondary survey

The secondary survey is a comprehensive head-to-toe assessment performed after immediate life threats have been addressed.

Includes:

  • Complete vital signs and pain assessment.
  • Head-to-toe examination.
  • Focused neurological examination.
  • Medical history, medications, and allergies.
  • Laboratory tests and diagnostic imaging as indicated.

Multiple trauma involves injuries to at least two organs or organ systems, often following a single catastrophic event.

7. Abdominal trauma

Blunt or penetrating abdominal injuries may cause severe internal hemorrhage or organ perforation.

Common injuries include liver laceration, splenic rupture, bowel perforation, and retroperitoneal bleeding.

Assessment findings:

  • Abdominal tenderness or rigidity.
  • Distention.
  • Bruising or penetrating wounds.
  • Tachycardia, hypotension, or signs of shock.
  • Referred shoulder pain from diaphragmatic irritation.

Nursing priorities:

  • Maintain ABCs and spinal precautions as indicated.
  • Keep NPO (nothing by mouth).
  • Establish IV access and monitor hemodynamics.
  • Prepare for focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST), CT if stable, or urgent surgery if unstable.
  • Document wounds and administer prescribed prophylaxis.

Evisceration: Cover exposed abdominal organs with sterile saline-moistened dressings. Do not attempt to replace organs into the abdomen.

8. Crush injuries and rhabdomyolysis

Crush injuries occur when prolonged or severe compression damages muscle and surrounding tissues.

Damaged muscle cells release intracellular substances, including myoglobin and potassium .

  • Release of myoglobin, potassium, phosphate, and creatine kinase

  • Dark, tea-colored urine due to myoglobinuria.
  • Elevated creatine kinase (CK).
  • Hyperkalemia.
  • Muscle pain, swelling, weakness.
  • Acute kidney injury.
  • Compartment syndrome.

  1. Address airway, breathing, hemorrhage, and shock.

  1. Treat dangerous hyperkalemia immediately.

Increasing pressure inside a muscle compartment compromises circulation and nerve function.

Early findings: Severe pain disproportionate to injury, pain with passive stretch, tense swelling, and paresthesia.

Frostbite occurs when tissue freezes, causing ice crystal formation, vascular injury, and potential tissue necrosis.

  • Do not apply direct dry heat, such as heating pads.
  • Do not allow walking on frostbitten feet unless necessary for safety.

12. General management of poisoning

The initial priority for any poisoning is ABCs and prevention of further exposure .

Assess the following:

  • What substance was involved?
  • How much was taken or encountered?
  • When did exposure occur?
  • What was the route: ingestion, inhalation, skin, or injection?
  • Was the exposure accidental or intentional?
  • What are the current vital signs, neurological findings, and ECG changes?

Potential diagnostics: Glucose, electrolytes, renal/liver function, ECG, blood gas, acetaminophen level, salicylate level, and toxicology testing as clinically indicated.

In the United States, Poison Control is available at 1-800-222-1222 .

Activated charcoal

Activated charcoal binds certain toxins in the gastrointestinal tract, reducing absorption.

Key points:

  • Most useful when administered early, often within 1 hour, with selected later indications.
  • Requires an intact or protected airway.
  • Does not effectively bind alcohols, iron, lithium, or many corrosive substances.
  • Not routinely used for caustic ingestion or high aspiration risk.

Important clinical update: Gastric lavage is rarely indicated in modern toxicology because of its risks and limited benefit. The PPT lists nasogastric suction and gastric lavage, but these should not be considered routine poisoning treatments.

Corrosive poisoning

Acids and alkalis damage tissues on contact.

  • Assess for drooling, dysphagia, burns, stridor, and respiratory distress.
  • Maintain NPO until evaluated.
  • Prepare for airway intervention if swelling threatens ventilation.
  • Do not induce vomiting.
  • Do not attempt chemical neutralization.
  • Do not routinely administer activated charcoal.

14. Carbon monoxide poisoning

Carbon monoxide (CO) binds to hemoglobin, forming carboxyhemoglobin and impairing oxygen delivery.

Causes

  • Automobile engines in enclosed garages.
  • Faulty gas or oil furnaces.
  • Indoor generators or grills.
  • Smoke inhalation during fires.

Manifestations

  • Headache and dizziness.
  • Nausea and weakness.
  • Confusion and altered mental status.
  • Chest pain and cardiac ischemia.
  • Seizures or coma with severe exposure.
  • Major NCLEX trap

A normal pulse oximeter reading does NOT exclude carbon monoxide poisoning.

Standard pulse oximetry cannot reliably distinguish oxyhemoglobin from carboxyhemoglobin. Confirm exposure using blood co-oximetry.

Treatment:

  • Remove patient from the source safely.
  • Administer 100% oxygen promptly.
  • Monitor cardiac rhythm and neurological status.
  • Obtain carboxyhemoglobin testing.
  • Consider hyperbaric oxygen for selected severe cases, including significant neurological or cardiac involvement and pregnancy.

Another exam trap: Cherry-red skin is a classic textbook description but is uncommon and unreliable.

15. Food poisoning

Food poisoning may result from bacteria, viruses, parasites, or preformed toxins.

Common manifestations are nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, and diarrhea.

Priority treatment: Fluid and electrolyte replacement.

  • Use oral rehydration when possible.
  • Administer IV fluids for significant dehydration or shock.
  • Monitor electrolytes and renal function.
  • Consider stool testing for severe, bloody, persistent, or outbreak-associated illness.
  • Use antimicrobials only when indicated for the identified or suspected cause.

Antidiarrheal nuance: The PPT advises avoiding antidiarrheals to allow elimination. For the NCLEX, avoid antimotility drugs with bloody diarrhea, high fever, or suspected invasive infection. They may be appropriate for selected adults with uncomplicated watery diarrhea.

Botulism: highly testable

Botulism is a neurotoxin-mediated illness causing descending flaccid paralysis.

Hallmark findings:

  • Diplopia and ptosis.
  • Dysphagia and dysarthria.
  • Descending muscle weakness.
  • Respiratory muscle paralysis.

Infant botulism is associated with exposure to Clostridium botulinum spores, including from honey.

Priority: Monitor respiratory function, prepare for ventilation, and administer antitoxin when indicated.

Alcohol withdrawal

Alcohol withdrawal can progress to seizures and delirium tremens (DTs).

Common progression:

  • Approximately 6–24 hours: Tremor, anxiety, nausea, sweating, tachycardia.
  • Approximately 6–48 hours: Withdrawal seizures.
  • Approximately 48–96 hours: Delirium tremens may occur, sometimes later.

DT findings: Severe agitation, confusion, hallucinations, autonomic instability, fever, and potential cardiovascular collapse.

CIWA-Ar

Interventions:

  • Benzodiazepines as prescribed.
  • Seizure precautions.
  • Frequent vital signs and neurological assessments.
  • Thiamine replacement when indicated.
  • Fluid and electrolyte correction.
  • Escalation for severe withdrawal.

CIWA-Ar requires a patient who can participate reliably; other assessment protocols are used in patients who are delirious, intubated, or unable to communicate.

18. Venomous snakebite management

Venomous bites may cause tissue destruction, bleeding abnormalities, neurological dysfunction, and shock.

Assessment:

  • Local swelling, pain, bruising, and blistering.
  • Progressive edema.
  • Nausea, weakness, or neurological symptoms.

19. Sexual assault: nursing priorities

Emergency care must address immediate injuries while providing trauma-informed, patient-centered support.

First priorities: Stabilize physical emergencies, establish safety, and obtain informed consent for examination and treatment.

Nursing interventions

  • Provide privacy and emotional support.
  • Use nonjudgmental communication.
  • Avoid repeatedly asking the patient to recount the assault.
  • Explain each procedure and obtain consent.
  • Offer a Sexual Assault Nurse Examiner (SANE).
  • Assess for injuries, pregnancy risk, and sexually transmitted infections (STIs).
  • Offer STI prophylaxis, HIV post-exposure prophylaxis when indicated, and emergency contraception.
  • Explain forensic evidence collection options.
  • Coordinate safe discharge and follow-up.

Evidence preservation

  • Encourage avoiding bathing, changing clothes, or washing evidence before collection, if the patient wishes to preserve evidence .
  • Clothing should generally be placed in separate paper bags.
  • Document objective findings and patient statements accurately.
  • Maintain chain of custody.

Important: A patient who has already showered or changed clothes may still have valuable forensic evidence. Do not discourage evaluation.

Timing: The PPT states evidence collection can occur up to six days after assault. Actual collection windows depend on jurisdiction, evidence type, and protocol; patients should be offered evaluation even when outside a typical collection window.

NCLEX red flags

  • Do not force a forensic examination.
  • Do not require police reporting as a condition of receiving care.
  • Do not promise absolute confidentiality when mandatory reporting laws apply.
  • Allow the patient to make informed decisions whenever they have capacity.

20. Human trafficking

Trafficking may involve forced labor, sexual exploitation, coercion, threats, or manipulation.

Possible indicators

  • Companion insists on answering all questions.
  • Patient appears frightened or defers to companion.
  • Inconsistent history.
  • Repeated unexplained injuries.
  • Limited control over money, documents, or movement.
  • Signs of poor access to healthcare.
  • Fear of authorities or retaliation.

These are indicators, not proof of trafficking.

Nursing approach

  • Arrange a private conversation away from companions when safe.
  • Use a professional interpreter rather than the accompanying person.
  • Ask direct but nonjudgmental questions.
  • Assess immediate safety and urgent health needs.
  • Offer resources without pressuring disclosure.
  • Follow reporting laws and institutional protocols.

Examples of screening questions:

  • Are you able to leave your work or living situation when you choose?
  • Does anyone control your identification or money?
  • Do you feel safe where you live?
  • Has anyone threatened you or your family?

Resource: National Human Trafficking Hotline: 1-888-373-7888.

NCLEX: The priority is patient safety and autonomy. Do not confront a suspected trafficker or automatically separate the patient in a manner that increases danger.

23. Case #1: Mr. Raymond — Suspected acute pancreatitis

48-year-old male

History: Type 2 diabetes, inconsistent metformin use, daily alcohol consumption of at least six beers.

Findings:

  • Severe epigastric pain, 10/10.
  • Temperature 101.8°F.
  • HR 128, RR 26, BP 90/60.
  • Abdominal distention and periumbilical bruising.
  • Jaundice.
  • Poor oral intake.

Recognize and analyze cues

The presentation is highly concerning for acute pancreatitis, potentially severe or hemorrhagic , with hypovolemia or evolving shock. Alcohol-associated pancreatitis and gallstone-related pancreatitis are both considerations.

Cullen sign: Periumbilical ecchymosis, associated with intra-abdominal or retroperitoneal hemorrhage.

Grey Turner sign: Flank ecchymosis, also associated with retroperitoneal bleeding.

These findings are not specific to pancreatitis and warrant investigation for hemorrhage.

Expected diagnostics

  • Serum lipase (more specific than amylase for pancreatitis).
  • Complete blood count (CBC).
  • Comprehensive metabolic panel.
  • Blood glucose and calcium.
  • Liver enzymes and bilirubin.
  • Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine.
  • Lactate when shock is suspected.
  • Right upper quadrant ultrasound for biliary disease.
  • CT abdomen when diagnosis is unclear or complications are suspected.

Nursing interventions

  • Assess ABCs and perfusion.
  • Establish IV access.
  • Administer prescribed isotonic fluids with frequent reassessment.
  • Provide IV analgesics and antiemetics.
  • Monitor glucose, electrolytes, urine output, and respiratory status.
  • Maintain NPO during initial severe symptoms as ordered.
  • Prepare for early oral or enteral nutrition once clinically tolerated.

Clinical update: Prolonged NPO status is no longer routine for all pancreatitis patients. Early feeding is encouraged when tolerated, especially in mild disease.

Evaluate outcomes

Following the PowerPoint's IV normal saline, hydromorphone, ondansetron, and NPO orders, look for:

  • Improving blood pressure and heart rate.
  • Adequate urine output, generally at least 0.5 mL/kg/hr.
  • Decreased pain and nausea.
  • Improved perfusion and mental status.
  • Stable respiratory status.
  • Improving renal function and lactate when abnormal.

NCLEX priority: A BP of 90/60 with tachycardia and suspected hemorrhagic pancreatitis is more immediately concerning than the pain score alone.

24. Case #2: Mr. Grimes — Suspected major gastrointestinal hemorrhage

Patient history

  • Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).
  • Mechanical aortic valve.
  • Taking warfarin.
  • Found on floor with bloody emesis.

Assessment

  • HR 142/min.
  • BP 82/38 mmHg.
  • RR 32/min.
  • Pale, diaphoretic skin.
  • Weak, thready pulses.
  • Responds only to painful stimuli.
  • INR 6.6.

Reference: INR about 0.8–1.2 without anticoagulation. Mechanical-valve treatment targets depend on valve type and individual risks.

Priority hypothesis

Hemorrhagic hypovolemic shock from gastrointestinal bleeding, exacerbated by excessive warfarin anticoagulation.

The patient has profound circulatory instability and impaired consciousness.

Immediate interventions

  1. Activate emergency resuscitation response.
  1. Assess airway protection and prepare for intubation if indicated.
  1. Provide oxygen and suction as needed.
  1. Establish large-bore IV access.
  1. Obtain CBC, coagulation studies, type and crossmatch, electrolytes, and lactate.
  1. Initiate blood product resuscitation as ordered.
  1. Prepare for urgent endoscopy or other definitive bleeding control.
  1. Administer prescribed warfarin reversal therapy.

Warfarin reversal

Vitamin K: Restores synthesis of vitamin K-dependent clotting factors, but its effect is not immediate.

Four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (4F-PCC): Rapidly replenishes clotting factors for life-threatening warfarin- associated bleeding. IV vitamin K is typically given alongside it.

Fresh frozen plasma may be used in selected circumstances when PCC is unavailable.

What about his mechanical valve?

The patient requires long-term anticoagulation because of the mechanical valve, but life-threatening hemorrhage takes priority .

Anticoagulation resumption requires individualized specialist planning after bleeding control.

Evaluate outcomes

  • Blood pressure improves.
  • Heart rate decreases.
  • Mental status improves.
  • Active bleeding diminishes.
  • Hemoglobin stabilizes.
  • INR improves.
  • Urine output and perfusion recover.

Major NCLEX trap: The patient received 1,000 mL normal saline, but ongoing severe hemorrhage cannot be adequately corrected by crystalloid alone. Blood products and hemorrhage control are essential.

Disaster triage colors

  • RED — Immediate
  • Life-threatening but potentially survivable with prompt intervention
  • Severe hemorrhage that can be controlled; airway compromise that can be treated
  • YELLOW — Delayed

Blast injuries are classified by mechanism.

Blast lung

Pressure waves damage pulmonary tissue, producing hemorrhage, edema, and impaired gas exchange.

Manifestations:

  • Dyspnea.
  • Hypoxemia.
  • Chest pain.
  • Hemoptysis.
  • Possible pneumothorax or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

Nerve agents: cholinergic crisis

Nerve agents prevent breakdown of acetylcholine, causing excessive cholinergic stimulation.

DUMBBELSS mnemonic:

  • D — Diarrhea
  • U — Urination
  • M — Miosis
  • B — Bronchospasm
  • B — Bradycardia
  • E — Emesis
  • L — Lacrimation
  • S — Salivation
  • S — Sweating

Also watch for muscle fasciculations, weakness, seizures, and respiratory failure.

Antidotes

Atropine: Blocks muscarinic effects, particularly bronchorrhea, bronchospasm, and bradycardia.

Pralidoxime (2-PAM): Reactivates acetylcholinesterase when administered appropriately and early enough, helping reverse neuromuscular effects.

Benzodiazepines: Treat seizures.

NCLEX: In a nerve agent exposure, respiratory compromise from secretions, bronchospasm, and muscle paralysis is a leading cause of death.

Radiation protection

  • Time Distance Shielding
  • Minimize exposure duration Maximize separation Use suitable barriers

Decontamination priorities

  • Protect staff with appropriate PPE.
  • Remove contaminated clothing.
  • Bag and isolate clothing according to protocol.
  • Gently wash exposed skin with soap and lukewarm water.
  • Avoid aggressive scrubbing that damages skin.
  • Monitor for residual contamination.

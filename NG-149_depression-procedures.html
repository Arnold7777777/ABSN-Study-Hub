Depression: Procedures ⚡

ECT · TMS · Vagus Nerve Stimulation — non-drug options for treatment-resistant depression

NG-149 MENTAL HEALTH ADHD-friendly visual edition

When medication and therapy alone haven't worked, or a client needs a fast, safe response (severe suicidality, catatonia, psychotic depression, pregnancy), the next step is a brain-stimulation procedure. ECT is the most tested — think of it as a controlled, monitored "reset" that induces a brief, therapeutic seizure.

⚡ ECT = brief seizureElectrical stimulus induces a controlled, generalized seizure under anesthesia.

📋

BEFORE

STEP 1 · PREP & SCREEN

Same prep as any procedure under brief general anesthesia — plus a few psych-specific holds.

🚨 Screen the whole history — report to the HCP

ECT briefly raises intracranial pressure and stresses the cardiovascular system during the seizure, so the biggest contraindication-style concerns are:

  • 🧠 Cerebral neoplasm / space-occupying brain lesion — raised ICP risk
  • ❤️ Recent myocardial infarction — cardiac stress risk
  • 👁️ Recent stroke, retinal issues, or other high anesthesia risk — verify per facility screening
🧠 Same red flags as any general anesthesia case — a fresh MI or a brain lesion means the heart or the skull can't safely tolerate the stress of a seizure. Screen & report before scheduling.

📝 Standard pre-procedure checklist

  • ✅ Signed informed consent
  • ✅ NPO 6–8 hours before the procedure
  • ✅ Have the client void immediately before
  • ✅ Remove dentures and contact lenses
  • ✅ Baseline vital signs
🧠 Read this list and you're really reading a pre-op checklist — ECT is done under brief general anesthesia, so treat it like surgery prep.

🚫 Hold anticonvulsant mood stabilizers

Do not give anticonvulsant medications — e.g. valproic acid, carbamazepine — before ECT.

These drugs work by raising the seizure threshold, which fights the entire point of the treatment: ECT needs a seizure to be therapeutic.

🧠 Anticonvulsant = anti-seizure = anti-ECT. If the goal is to trigger a seizure, don't give the drug whose job is to prevent one.

🍽️ Nutrition tie-in

A client with depression often has poor nutritional intake going into the procedure — assess and correct this beforehand where possible: offer high-calorie fluids/snacks earlier in the day, and let the client help select menu items when eating is allowed.

🧠 Depression's appetite loss doesn't pause for procedure day — keep nutrition on the plan right up to the NPO window.
⚡

DURING

STEP 2 · THE PROCEDURE

A brief, monitored, medically induced seizure — not the uncontrolled event people picture.

⚡ What ECT actually does

ECT induces electrical activity across the scalp to trigger a generalized seizure — think of it as jump-starting the brain's chemistry. Because the client is under general anesthesia with a muscle relaxant, there is no violent outward convulsing.

  • Each seizure is brief — generally 15–60 seconds (exact duration varies by reference/response)
  • Given 2–3×/week for a course totaling roughly 6–12 treatments
🧠 “Jump-start, not lightning strike.” The client is asleep, paralyzed by the muscle relaxant, and monitored the entire time — it is a brief, controlled medical procedure, not the dramatic seizure people picture from old movies.

🧰 Equipment that must be at bedside

❤️Cardiac monitor
🛒Crash cart
🫁Oxygen
🧹Suction
🧠 “C-C-O-S” — Cardiac monitor · Crash cart · Oxygen · Suction. If it's on a code cart, it needs to be in the ECT room.

👥 The team & the setting

ECT is performed by a team including anesthesia provider + psychiatrist, typically in a monitored procedural setting (often same-day/outpatient once stable). The nurse's role during the procedure is continuous monitoring and immediate post-ictal recovery care.

🧠 Same team structure as any brief anesthesia procedure — anesthesia manages sedation, psychiatry manages the treatment itself.

AFTER

STEP 3 · RECOVER & TEACH

Expect brief confusion and short-term memory loss — reassure, reorient, and protect until it clears.

🧠 Expected side effect: temporary confusion & short-term memory loss

The most important nursing action when a client wakes up confused with memory gaps is to remind the client that the memory loss is temporary — reorient to person/place/time, stay calm and simple, and reassess after the full course.

🧠 Expected ≠ ignored. Confusion/memory loss is a known, temporary effect — but the nurse still reorients every time and documents severity.

🚗 Teaching: no driving during the course

  • No driving for the duration of the ECT treatment course — confusion/memory effects make it unsafe
  • Arrange transportation to and from every session
  • Avoid major decisions or signing important documents until memory clears
🧠 If confusion is expected after the treatment, driving is off the table for the whole course — not just treatment day.

🩺 Immediate post-procedure care

  • Recovery-room style monitoring: vitals, airway, LOC until fully awake
  • Reorient frequently — person, place, time
  • Reassess mood & suicidal ideation once alert — ECT does not remove the need for ongoing safety checks
  • Offer food/fluids once fully awake and gag reflex intact
🧠 Waking up from ECT is a lot like waking up from any anesthesia — airway, orientation, and safety come first, memory reassurance comes right after.

🧲 Non-drug alternatives: TMS & Vagus Nerve Stimulation

Feature⚡ ECT🧲 TMS (transcranial magnetic stimulation)
AnesthesiaYes — general anesthesia + muscle relaxantNo anesthesia needed
MechanismElectrical stimulus induces a generalized seizureRepeated magnetic pulses stimulate a targeted mood-regulation area of the brain — no seizure induced
Memory effectsTemporary short-term memory loss/confusion is expectedMinimal to no memory effects
SettingMonitored procedural/OR-style settingOutpatient office visits, client stays awake & alert
Used forSevere, treatment-resistant, or urgent depression (active suicidality, catatonia, psychosis)Treatment-resistant depression when a lower-intensity option is appropriate

Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) is a third, less-common option — a surgically implanted device delivers regular electrical pulses to the vagus nerve for treatment-resistant depression; it is a longer-term implanted therapy rather than a single scheduled procedure.

🧠 “ECT seizes, TMS pulses, VNS implants.” All three exist for the same client — depression that hasn't responded to medication — but they trade off invasiveness against intensity of effect.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: A client with severe depression and poor nutritional intake is scheduled for ECT. Which orders would the nurse expect (select all that apply)?
NPO 6–8 hours before the procedure, signed informed consent, remove dentures/contacts, screen for recent MI or cerebral neoplasm and report to the HCP, hold anticonvulsant medications (e.g. valproic acid, carbamazepine).
Q2: A client has temporary confusion and memory loss after ECT. What is the most important nursing action?
Remind the client that the memory loss is temporary, reorient frequently, and stay calm and simple.
Q3: Which side effect is most associated with ECT?
Short-term memory loss / confusion, which resolves over time.
Q4: What equipment should the nurse confirm is available before ECT?
Oxygen, suction equipment, a crash cart, and a cardiac monitor — the same as any procedure under general anesthesia.

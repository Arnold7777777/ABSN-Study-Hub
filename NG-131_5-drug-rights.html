5 Drug Rights ✅

The Rights of Safe Medication Administration

NG-131 Pharmacology ADHD-friendly visual edition

Every single medication pass, every time, boils down to 5 checks before the syringe or pill touches the client: right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, right time. Miss one and it's a medication error — this is the page every other drug page on this site quietly assumes you already know.

✅ The core 5 are non-negotiablePatient · Drug · Dose · Route · Time — check every one, every pass.
🪪 2 identifiers, alwaysName and DOB (or MRN) — never room number alone.
📈 Peak = highest, trough = lowestGive on time or the blood level drops below therapeutic.
📝 Document immediatelyChart time · route · site · response right after — never before.

THE CORE 5

FOUNDATION · MEMORIZE THESE

"Please Do Da Right Thing" — every safe medication pass runs through the same 5 checkpoints, in this order, every time.

🧾 The checklist every nurse silently runs

🧠 "Please Do Da Right Thing" — Patient · Drug · Dose · Route · Time. Say it before every single pass until it's automatic.

🪪 Right Patient — 2 identifiers, every time

  • Compare name + DOB (or MRN) on the ID band to the MAR
  • Never use room number as an identifier
  • Red/allergy band = stop and confirm before giving
🧠 "2 to confirm, 0 to assume." Ask the client to state their own name & DOB when they're able — don't just read the band and move on.

💊 Right Drug — read the label 3 times

  • Compare drug name on the MAR to the label at pick-up, pour, and bedside
  • EXAM TIP NCLEX tests generic names, not brand/trade names
  • Watch look-alike/sound-alike pairs — e.g. hydrALAZine vs hydrOXYZine
🧠 "3 looks before it goes in the mouth." Read the label when you grab it, when you pour it, and again at the bedside before it's given.

⚖️ Right Dose — verify the math

  • Confirm dose against the MAR and the prescriber's order
  • Never round or estimate a calculated dose
  • High-alert drugs (insulin, heparin, opioids) → independent double-check with a 2nd nurse
🧠 "When in doubt, recalculate — don't just double it, double-check it."

🚪 Right Route — as ordered, not as easiest

IV/IM = fastest absorption; oral is slowest and depends on GI transit.

🧠 If the order says PO and the client can't swallow — hold and call the HCP, don't just switch routes yourself.

⏱️ Right Time — on schedule, on purpose

  • Give within the facility's window, typically ±30–60 min of scheduled time
  • Time-critical meds (antibiotics, insulin, anticoagulants) → tighter window
  • Late antibiotic doses let blood levels dip below the therapeutic range
🧠 See the Timing section below — peak & trough exist because of this exact right.
🧭

THE MODERN RIGHTS

STEP 2 · BEYOND THE BIG 5

Practice varies — some programs teach 6 rights, some teach 9 or 10. These are the extra checkpoints you'll see added most often.

📝 Right Documentation

Chart time, route, site, and client response — immediately after giving, never before.

🧠 Never chart in advance — if you get pulled away, an undocumented-but-charted dose looks like a double-dose risk for the next nurse.

❓ Right Reason (Indication)

Know why this client is getting this drug — does the order make clinical sense for their diagnosis?

🧠 If a med doesn't match the diagnosis on the chart, question it before giving it — that's your last safety net.

📈 Right Response (Evaluation)

Reassess afterward — did the drug do what it was supposed to? Pain down? BP down? Blood glucose corrected?

🧠 Giving the drug isn't the end of the task — evaluating the response closes the loop.

🙅 Right to Refuse

A cognitively intact client can refuse any medication. Educate on the risk of refusing, then document & notify the HCP — you cannot force it.

🧠 Autonomy beats compliance. Coercing a competent adult into taking a med is a rights violation, not good nursing.

🎓 Right Education

It is your job as the nurse to teach — clear up confusion about why a drug is given, what to expect, and what to report. An informed client catches errors too.

🧠 "Un-teach the fear, teach the fact." A client who understands their meds is a second set of eyes on your safety checks.
⏱️

TIMING & VERIFICATION

STEP 3 · WHY TIME MATTERS

Right Time isn't just punctuality — it keeps drug levels inside the window where the drug actually works.

📈 Peak & trough — why "on time" is a safety right, not a courtesy

Peak = the highest blood concentration after a dose (checks for toxicity). Trough = the lowest level, drawn right before the next dose (checks the drug hasn't dropped too low to work). Antibiotics like vancomycin and gentamicin are commonly monitored this way.

🧠 "Peak pokes up, trough is tough (the lowest point it can go)." A late antibiotic dose lets the trough drop too far — the infection gets a window to regrow before the next dose arrives.

🚨 Example: Digoxin & the apical pulse

Before giving digoxin, count the apical heart rate for 1 full minute.

Hold the dose and notify the HCP if the apical pulse is below the facility's cutoff (commonly <60 bpm in adults) — a classic timing-and-assessment safety check tied to this right.

🧠 "Count a full 60 before the 60-second drug." No shortcuts on the apical count for digoxin.

✅ The 3 checks — med room, order, bedside

1
🏥 Med room / Pyxis — pull the drug, check label vs. MAR
2
📋 Order — recheck the MAR against the prescriber's order before pouring
3
🛏️ Bedside — final label check + 2 patient identifiers, right before giving
🧠 3 checks, 3 places — never skip the last one even if you're rushed. The bedside check is the one that catches wrong-room, wrong-patient errors.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What are the "core 5" rights of medication administration?
Right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, right time.
Q2: What two identifiers confirm right patient?
Name and date of birth (or medical record number) — never room number.
Q3: What's the difference between peak and trough?
Peak is the highest drug concentration after a dose; trough is the lowest, drawn right before the next dose.
Q4: A cognitively intact client refuses their medication — what do you do?
Educate on the risks of refusing, then document the refusal and notify the HCP. You cannot force a competent adult to take it.

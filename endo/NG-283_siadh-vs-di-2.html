SIADH vs DI 💧

Part 2 of 2 — the nursing management: fluid rules, sodium safety, desmopressin and monitoring

NG-283 ENDOCRINE · MANAGEMENT PAGE ADHD-friendly visual edition

Part 1 (NG-282) carries the core comparison: SIADH = too much ADH → water is retained → dilutional hyponatremia, concentrated urine, weight gain. DI = too little ADH (or kidneys that ignore it) → dilute water pours out → hypernatremia, huge pale urine output, dehydration. This page is what you DO about it — fluid restriction, hypertonic saline and the danger of correcting sodium too fast, daily weights, strict I&O, seizure precautions, desmopressin administration and teaching, and the numbers you watch.

THE TAP IN THE COLLECTING DUCT

STEP 1 · WHY IT ALL WORKS

Everything on this page happens in one tiny place: the wall of the renal collecting duct, where ADH decides whether water gets to leave the body.

🔬 Aquaporin cutaway — no ADH · normal · too much ADH

💧 Normal water-conservation loop

  1. Rising blood osmolality stimulates thirst and hypothalamic ADH signaling.
  2. Posterior pituitary releases ADH.
  3. Kidney V2 signaling inserts aquaporin-2 channels on the urine-facing membrane.
  4. Water moves from urine toward blood; urine becomes more concentrated.

Low effective circulating volume can also stimulate ADH. Central DI lacks adequate signal; nephrogenic DI has an impaired kidney response.

ADH aquaporin-2 water channel dilute urine concentrated urine
🧠 ADH = "Add water back to the blood." No ADH → the plug is out → DI. Too much ADH → the drain is welded shut → SIADH.

🧠 SIADH = Soaked Inside. DI = Dry Inside. Every management decision below flows from those four words.

⭐ Why the causes matter for management

  • DI — central: damage to the hypothalamus/posterior pituitary — tumor, trauma, neurosurgery, infection. Responds to desmopressin.
  • DI — nephrogenic: the kidney cannot respond to ADH (some medications, chronic kidney disease, electrolyte problems). Desmopressin will not fix it — management focuses on the cause, adequate water, and sometimes thiazide diuretics and a low-sodium diet.
  • SIADH: lung cancers (classically small-cell), CNS injury/infection, pneumonia, pain, nausea, surgery, and several drugs. Treating the cause is part of the treatment.
🚨 If desmopressin is not reducing the urine output, ask whether this is nephrogenic DI — the receptor problem is downstream of the hormone.
🧠 Central = no messenger. Nephrogenic = no mailbox. Only one of them can be fixed by sending more mail.

FLUID MANAGEMENT

STEP 2 · THE CORE INTERVENTION

SIADH: take water away. DI: give water back. And weigh them every single day.

⚖️ Daily weights — the best fluid-status measure there is

⚖️ Make weight trends meaningful

  1. Same scale and similar time each day.
  2. Use similar clothing and conditions.
  3. Interpret rapid changes with intake/output and examination.

A rapid 1 kg change can approximate 1 L of water, but weight is not the only measure of volume status.

🧠 A liter is a kilo is 2.2 pounds. If the weight moved and nothing else explains it, water moved.

🚱 SIADH — fluid RESTRICTION is the cornerstone

  • Restrict fluids — the prescribed volume is commonly in the region of 800–1000 mL/24 h, but it is always an individual order. Follow the written restriction.
  • Count everything liquid at room temperature — ice chips (about half their volume), gelatin, soup, ice cream, IV flushes and medication diluents.
  • Spread the allowance across the day and involve the patient in planning it; put the day's allowance in a labeled jug so it is visible.
  • Mouth care, sugar-free hard candy or gum, and lip balm for thirst — not extra fluid.
  • Post the restriction at the bedside and tell the family, or the well-meaning visitor with a soda undoes the plan.
  • Expect a rise in sodium and a fall in weight as it works; if urine output stays tiny and sodium keeps falling, escalate.

🥤 Count all prescribed fluid sources

Include
  • Drinks, IV fluids, tube-feed water, medication fluids and foods that melt to liquid.
Follow the order
  • Use the prescribed daily allowance and local counting method. Do not independently stop all fluids.
Reassess
  • New neurologic symptoms, falling sodium or an abrupt urine change require review.
🧠 In SIADH the problem is the water, not the salt. That is why the first move is taking water away rather than pouring sodium in.

🚰 DI — replace the water they are losing

  • Free access to water if the patient is awake, alert and able to drink — their thirst is a life-saving reflex. Do not restrict fluids in DI.
  • If they cannot drink enough or are obtunded, IV hypotonic fluid (commonly 0.45% NS or D5W) is used to replace free water — the exact fluid and rate are prescribed and guided by the sodium.
  • Match output with intake — hourly urine measurement in the acute phase; output can be enormous.
  • Watch for hypovolemia: falling BP, rising HR, poor turgor, dry mucous membranes, weak pulses, decreasing level of consciousness.
  • Safety: frequent urination and nocturia = fall risk. Call bell in reach, clear path to the bathroom, night light, bedside commode if needed.
  • Once desmopressin starts, the pendulum can swing — now the risk is too much water.
🧠 DI patients are drowning in thirst. Never take the glass away.

SODIUM SAFETY

STEP 3 · THE DANGEROUS PART

Brain cells sit in whatever sodium you give them. Change it too fast in either direction and you injure the brain.

🔬 What sodium does to a brain cell — and why speed matters

🧠 Water follows salt. Low sodium = swollen brain. High sodium = shrivelled brain. Fixing it fast is a second injury on top of the first.

🚨 Hypertonic saline (3% NaCl) — handle with respect

Reserved for severe, symptomatic hyponatremia — seizures, obtundation, coma — not for a mildly low number.

  • Given on an infusion pump, at a prescribed rate, usually in a monitored setting and often through a central line per policy.
  • Serum sodium is rechecked frequently during the infusion, on the schedule the protocol sets.
  • Correct slowly. There is a maximum rise permitted per 24 hours; going faster risks osmotic demyelination syndrome.
  • Monitor for fluid overload — crackles, dyspnea, jugular venous distension, rising BP — especially in heart failure. A loop diuretic is sometimes given alongside.
  • Neuro checks throughout: level of consciousness, orientation, speech, swallowing, motor strength.
🚨 Never infuse hypertonic saline by gravity, and never "catch up" a delayed infusion by speeding it up.
🧠 3% saline is a drug, not a fluid. Pump, monitor, recheck, slow.

🚨 Seizure precautions for severe hyponatremia

Answer first: sodium under about 120 mEq/L, or any sodium falling fast, is a seizure risk. Set the room up before it happens.

  • Bed in the lowest position, side rails padded and up per policy.
  • Suction and oxygen at the bedside, working, plus a bag-valve mask.
  • IV access maintained.
  • Reduce stimulation — quiet, dim, uninterrupted rest.
  • Never put anything in the mouth during a seizure; turn the patient to the side and protect the head.
  • Frequent neuro checks: level of consciousness, orientation, headache, nausea/vomiting, muscle twitching.
🧠 "Death by headache" for low sodium. Headache → nausea → confusion → seizure → coma. Catch it at the headache.

⚠️ The mirror problem in DI — hypernatremia

  • Sodium above 145 mEq/L with a high serum osmolality: thirst, dry sticky mucous membranes, fever, restlessness, irritability, lethargy, twitching, seizures, coma.
  • Rehydrate gradually. Dropping the sodium too quickly pulls water into brain cells → cerebral edema.
  • Monitor level of consciousness continuously during correction; a patient who becomes more confused while you are fixing the number is a red flag.
  • Watch simultaneously for hypovolemia: hypotension, tachycardia, poor perfusion, oliguria as the deficit deepens.
🧠 Both directions, same rule: the brain hates fast.

DESMOPRESSIN (DDAVP)

STEP 4 · THE DRUG

A synthetic copy of ADH. It reduces urine output. It is not a vasopressor.

💊 Desmopressin: replace the missing signal safely

Different formulations
  • Oral, nasal and injectable products are not dose-equivalent. Verify the specific order and administration instructions.
Watch for water retention
  • Headache, nausea, confusion, weight gain or seizures can signal hyponatremia. Seek timely assessment.
Avoid omissions
  • People dependent on desmopressin can deteriorate if doses or access to water are missed. Follow the individualized fluid and sick-day plan.

🚨 Say it correctly — the accuracy point that costs marks

Desmopressin is a synthetic antidiuretic hormone analog.

Its therapeutic job is to make the collecting duct reabsorb water, so urine output falls and urine becomes more concentrated. It is not a blood-pressure drug and it is not a vasopressor.

The confusion comes from the word "vasopressin" — the natural hormone's other name. Natural vasopressin does have vasoconstrictor activity at higher doses and is used in some shock protocols; desmopressin was deliberately modified to act on the water-handling receptors with minimal vasoconstriction. If an option says desmopressin is given "to raise the blood pressure," that option is wrong.

🚨 If blood pressure improves after desmopressin in DI, it is because you stopped the patient from urinating away their circulating volume — not because the drug squeezed their vessels.
🧠 Desmo-PRESS-in PRESSES the urine output DOWN. Not the blood pressure up.

💊 Routes, and what to teach for each

🖼️ Shown in the picture: Desmopressin: replace the missing signal safely ↑

🧠 Nose, mouth, needle. Same hormone, three doors — and the nose is the one a head cold can close.

🚨 Signs the dose is TOO MUCH — water intoxication

Too much desmopressin turns DI into SIADH. The patient stops excreting water and the sodium falls.

  • HEADACHE — the earliest and most important warning. A frequent headache on this drug is never "normal".
  • Nausea and vomiting, abdominal cramps
  • Weight gain and a sudden drop in urine output
  • Confusion, drowsiness, restlessness → seizures → coma

Action: hold and report per order, check weight, I&O and serum sodium, institute seizure precautions, and reassess the dose.

🧠 Headache → hold and report. Low sodium announces itself in the head first.

✅ Patient teaching for desmopressin

  • Weigh yourself DAILY — same time, same scale. NOT WEEKLY
  • Record intake and output and take the record to appointments.
  • Report headache, nausea, confusion, sudden weight gain or a big drop in urine output.
  • Avoid drinking excessively while on the drug — thirst is no longer a safe guide once the water is being held on to. Follow the fluid advice you were given.
  • Take it exactly as prescribed and do not run out; carry a supply when traveling.
  • Medical alert identification, and tell every provider you take it.
  • Report intercurrent illness — fever, vomiting, diarrhea and nasal congestion all change fluid balance or absorption.
🚨 Wrong statements to spot: "frequent headaches are normal" · "I'll weigh myself weekly" · "this treats my SIADH" (it would make SIADH worse) · "it's a blood-pressure medicine".
🧠 Weigh · record · report the headache. Three habits, one safe patient.

💊 On the SIADH side — the drug options

  • Fluid restriction first — it is the treatment, not a supporting measure.
  • Hypertonic saline for severe symptomatic hyponatremia, as above.
  • ADH receptor antagonists ("vaptans", e.g. tolvaptan, conivaptan) block ADH at the kidney and produce a water diuresis; they require close sodium monitoring and are typically started in a monitored setting.
  • Loop diuretics may be used in selected patients alongside sodium replacement.
  • Treat the cause: the tumor, the pneumonia, the head injury, or the offending medication.
  • Increased dietary sodium and protein are sometimes recommended — follow the prescribed plan.
🧠 SIADH: block the hormone or remove the water. DI: replace the hormone or replace the water. Perfect mirror.

MONITORING PARAMETERS

STEP 5 · WHAT YOU CHART

Six numbers tell you whether the plan is working — and which direction the patient is drifting.

🧪 Strict I&O and the specific gravity scale

📋 Fluid handover

Trend together
  • Hourly/shift urine volume as ordered.
  • Oral/IV intake; daily weight.
Pair with blood/urine tests
  • Serial sodium and serum osmolality.
  • Urine concentration interpreted with glucose and other solutes.
Escalate changes
  • New confusion or seizures.

🧾 Patient & family teaching to send home

  • Weigh daily and keep a simple log; report a sudden gain or loss.
  • Know your target: the fluid restriction volume (SIADH) or the need for free water access (DI).
  • Recognize the red flags: headache, confusion, unusual drowsiness, seizure, marked change in urine output.
  • Never stop or double the desmopressin without advice; keep a supply on hand.
  • Medical alert identification for both conditions.
  • Bring the weight and I&O log to every appointment; keep follow-up sodium checks.
🧠 The scale and the log are the home monitor. Teach them to trust the trend, not how they feel.
🆕 Newer guidance — side notes (your textbook answer stays the exam answer)
  • In DI the serum sodium can be normal if the patient can drink enough to keep up; it rises when intake cannot match the losses.
  • SIADH is usually clinically euvolemic — a rising blood pressure or visible puffiness is not typical, and edema suggests another cause.
  • SIADH urine is concentrated for the low serum osmolality; a specific gravity above 1.030 is not required.
  • 3% saline for severe symptoms may be given as protocol-directed measured boluses or a controlled infusion, never an improvised push.

🎯 Cover & check — 10 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What is the single best measure of fluid status in these patients?
Daily weight — same time, same scale, same clothing, after voiding. About 1 kg of weight change equals 1 liter of fluid. Weighing weekly is not adequate.
Q2: What is the cornerstone treatment of SIADH?
Fluid restriction, at the volume prescribed. Everything liquid at room temperature counts — including ice chips, gelatin, soup and IV diluents.
Q3: Why must hyponatremia be corrected slowly?
Raising the sodium too quickly causes osmotic demyelination syndrome (central pontine myelinolysis) — confusion, dysarthria, dysphagia and paralysis, often permanent. Follow the protocol's maximum rise per 24 hours and recheck sodium frequently.
Q4: When is hypertonic (3%) saline used, and what are the nursing rules?
Only for severe, symptomatic hyponatremia (seizures, obtundation). Give on an infusion pump at the prescribed rate, usually in a monitored setting, recheck sodium frequently, watch for fluid overload, and never speed it up to catch up.
Q5: Which serum sodium level should make you set up seizure precautions?
Severe hyponatremia — around 120 mEq/L or below, and any sodium that is falling rapidly. Set up suction, oxygen, padded rails, IV access and a quiet environment before the seizure happens.
Q6: What class of drug is desmopressin?
A synthetic antidiuretic hormone (ADH/vasopressin) analog. It increases water reabsorption in the collecting duct so urine output falls and urine concentrates. It is not a vasopressor and is not given to raise blood pressure.
Q7: Your patient on desmopressin reports frequent headaches. What does that mean?
Possible water intoxication with falling sodium — the dose may be too high. Headaches are never "normal" on this drug. Check weight, I&O and serum sodium, hold/report per order, and start seizure precautions if the sodium is low.
Q8: Would you restrict fluids in diabetes insipidus?
No. An alert patient with DI must have free access to water — thirst is protecting them from severe hypernatremia. Restricting fluids can be fatal. (Once on desmopressin, they are taught to avoid excessive intake, which is a different thing.)
Q9: Compare urine specific gravity in SIADH and DI.
SIADH: high, above about 1.030 — small volumes of dark concentrated urine. DI: low, around 1.005 or less — huge volumes of nearly clear urine. Normal band is roughly 1.005–1.030.
Q10: A patient with DI says, "I'm glad this medicine treats my SIADH." Correct or not?
Incorrect — needs teaching. Desmopressin treats diabetes insipidus by replacing ADH. Giving it in SIADH would make the water retention and hyponatremia worse.

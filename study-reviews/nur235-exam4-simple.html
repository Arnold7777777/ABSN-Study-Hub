Module 10 · Musculoskeletal and neuromusculoskeletal disorders 14 short topics

Module 10

Cast care

  • Recognize: Swelling, increasing pain, numbness, cool digits or poor capillary refill can indicate compromised circulation or pressure injury.
  • Act: Check neurovascular status regularly, keep the cast dry and protect edges and skin. Report increasing pain or sensory/circulatory changes promptly.
  • Remember: Never place objects inside the cast. A present pulse does not exclude compartment syndrome; escalating pain with passive stretch is a warning.

Module 10

Traction care

  • Recognize: Traction maintains alignment using continuous prescribed pull; disrupted alignment or ineffective weights can impair treatment.
  • Act: Maintain body alignment and countertraction, keep weights hanging freely, and assess skin, pin sites and distal neurovascular status as appropriate.
  • Remember: Do not remove or alter weights without an order. Prevent immobility complications and report infection signs, severe pain or altered sensation/circulation.

Module 10

Scoliosis

  • Recognize: Uneven shoulders or hips and a rib prominence on forward bending suggest a lateral spinal curve with rotation.
  • Act: Assess posture, function and ordered imaging; support prescribed bracing, skin care and body image. Report new neurologic symptoms.
  • Remember: Observation, bracing or surgery depends on curve size and remaining growth. After surgery, follow movement precautions and monitor neurologic status and complications.

Module 10

Developmental dysplasia of the hip

  • Recognize: Hip instability may cause limited abduction, unequal leg lengths or abnormal screening findings; assessment and imaging depend on age.
  • Act: Support prescribed harness/cast treatment and monitor skin, perfusion and movement. Teach caregivers not to adjust harness straps without instructions.
  • Remember: The goal is proper femoral-head positioning in the acetabulum. Use hip-safe swaddling that allows flexion and abduction; follow-up confirms correction.

Module 10

Torticollis

  • Recognize: A persistent head tilt or preference for one direction often reflects tight sternocleidomastoid muscle; head flattening may coexist.
  • Act: Assess neck movement and head shape; support early physical therapy and taught gentle positioning/stretching. Investigate sudden painful tilt with fever, trauma or neurologic change.
  • Remember: Encourage looking both ways and supervised awake tummy time. Do not force stretches or change safe supine sleep positioning.

Module 10

Plagiocephaly

  • Recognize: External pressure can flatten one area of the infant skull while sutures remain open; asymmetry may accompany torticollis.
  • Act: Assess head growth, neck motion and positioning. Encourage supervised awake tummy time and varied head position; arrange evaluation for progressive or atypical shape.
  • Remember: Keep sleep supine on a safe surface. Positional flattening differs from craniosynostosis, where sutures close too early.

Module 10

Duchenne muscular dystrophy

  • Recognize: Dystrophin deficiency causes progressive weakness: frequent falls, enlarged calves and Gowers maneuver, using hands to climb up the legs.
  • Act: Assess mobility, breathing, cardiac function and nutrition. Support stretching, assistive devices and specialist-directed treatment while preserving participation and independence.
  • Remember: Unlike cerebral palsy, muscle weakness progresses. Respiratory and cardiac monitoring remains essential even when mobility is the most visible concern.

Module 10

Legg-Calve-Perthes disease

  • Recognize: Loss of femoral-head blood supply causes bone necrosis/remodeling; a childhood limp, hip or referred knee pain and limited motion are clues.
  • Act: Assess pain, gait and range of motion; follow prescribed activity and weight-bearing limits. Support orthopedic imaging and long-term follow-up.
  • Remember: Treatment aims to preserve hip motion and contain the femoral head during healing. Bracing, casting or surgery depends on age and severity.

Module 10

Slipped capital femoral epiphysis

  • Recognize: An adolescent may report hip, groin or knee pain with a limp and reduced hip internal rotation.
  • Act: Stop weight bearing and arrange urgent orthopedic assessment; do not force hip movement. Prepare for prescribed surgical fixation and postoperative restrictions.
  • Remember: The complaint can be knee pain even though the problem is the hip growth plate. Report symptoms in the opposite hip or knee too.

Module 10

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis

  • Recognize: Persistent joint inflammation causes swelling, pain, morning stiffness and reduced function; some forms include fever or rash.
  • Act: Assess function, pain and medication effects; balance rest with range-of-motion and low-impact activity. Support prescribed anti-inflammatory or disease-modifying treatment.
  • Remember: Keep ophthalmology screening even without eye symptoms because uveitis can be silent. Fever while immunosuppressed needs clinical advice.

Module 10

Clubfoot: talipes

  • Recognize: The foot turns inward and downward, with an adducted/inverted forefoot and plantar-flexed ankle.
  • Act: Support serial casting and prescribed correction, assess toes and skin, and teach the brace schedule that maintains correction.
  • Remember: Cool or discolored toes, severe pain, swelling or a slipping cast needs prompt review. Early improvement does not remove the need for ongoing bracing.

Module 10

Craniosynostosis

  • Recognize: Premature skull-suture fusion produces an unusual head shape or suture ridge and may affect head growth.
  • Act: Measure growth and assess development, support craniofacial referral, and monitor after prescribed surgery. Escalate vomiting, lethargy or neurologic change.
  • Remember: This differs from positional plagiocephaly, where sutures remain open. Follow individualized wound, activity and helmet instructions; routine repositioning alone may not address fused sutures.

Module 10

Osteomyelitis

  • Recognize: Bone infection can cause localized pain, fever, swelling and refusal to bear weight or use the affected limb.
  • Act: Assess pain, temperature, perfusion and function; obtain ordered cultures/imaging and give prescribed antibiotics promptly. Support the affected area and monitor response.
  • Remember: A child may avoid using the limb rather than name the pain. Complete the treatment course and evaluate persistent symptoms or systemic deterioration.

Module 10

Compartment syndrome

  • Recognize: Pressure within a closed muscle compartment reduces perfusion: escalating pain, pain with passive stretch and paresthesia are early clues.
  • Act: Escalate immediately for surgical assessment; assess neurovascular status and keep the limb at heart level per emergency plan. Remove external constriction only as directed.
  • Remember: A pulse can remain present. Do not wait for pulselessness; urgent fasciotomy may be needed to prevent permanent muscle and nerve damage.

Module 11

Congenital hypothyroidism

  • Recognize: Low thyroid hormone from birth threatens brain development; newborn screening may detect it before poor feeding, prolonged jaundice, constipation or hypotonia appears.
  • Act: Ensure prompt confirmatory testing and prescribed levothyroxine; monitor growth, development and thyroid labs.
  • Remember: Do not wait for obvious symptoms. Give the exact dose consistently, not in a full bottle, and follow separation instructions for interfering foods or supplements.

Module 11

Hyperthyroidism

  • Recognize: Excess thyroid hormone may cause heat intolerance, tachycardia, tremor, weight loss and behavior or sleep changes.
  • Act: Assess pulse, temperature, weight and treatment response; give prescribed antithyroid therapy and monitor adverse effects. Escalate severe fever, tachycardia or altered mental status.
  • Remember: Beta blockers relieve adrenergic symptoms; they do not correct the underlying immune cause. Medication choice and monitoring require pediatric specialist guidance.

Module 11

Parathyroid disorders

  • Recognize: Low PTH usually lowers calcium and raises phosphate, producing tingling, cramps or tetany; excess PTH tends toward the opposite mineral pattern.
  • Act: Assess calcium, phosphate, ECG and neurologic/airway symptoms. Treat severe symptomatic hypocalcemia urgently with prescribed monitored therapy; follow cause-specific replacement or reduction plans.
  • Remember: Laryngospasm, seizure or dysrhythmia is an emergency. Think PTH controls calcium balance, not blood glucose.

Module 11

Growth hormone deficiency

  • Recognize: Slowing height velocity with proportionate short stature suggests inadequate growth hormone; a trend is more informative than one height.
  • Act: Track growth and development, support prescribed diagnostic testing and hormone therapy, and monitor treatment effects and adverse symptoms.
  • Remember: The medication product determines schedule and route. Family teaching includes consistent administration and follow-up; unexplained headache or hip/knee pain needs review.

Module 11

Type 1 diabetes mellitus

  • Recognize: Autoimmune beta-cell destruction causes insulin deficiency: polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss and fatigue; vomiting with ketones suggests DKA.
  • Act: Monitor glucose, ketones when indicated, hydration and insulin delivery. Support the prescribed insulin, meal and activity plan and teach hypoglycemia treatment.
  • Remember: Do not omit basal insulin during illness; follow the sick-day plan. School support, emergency supplies and medical identification help prevent crises.

Module 11

Diabetic ketoacidosis

  • Recognize: Insulin deficiency causes ketosis, acidosis and dehydration: vomiting, abdominal pain, fruity breath and deep rapid breathing are clues.
  • Act: Follow the pediatric protocol for IV fluids, insulin and electrolytes; monitor glucose, potassium, ECG, perfusion, urine and neurologic status.
  • Remember: Dextrose may be added while insulin clears ketosis. Worsening headache or reduced alertness during treatment needs urgent assessment for cerebral injury.

Module 11

Type 2 diabetes mellitus

  • Recognize: Insulin resistance and progressive beta-cell dysfunction raise glucose; acanthosis nigricans, polyuria or thirst may occur, but some children are asymptomatic.
  • Act: Monitor glucose, A1c, BP and comorbid risks; support family-centered nutrition/activity and prescribed age-appropriate medicines without blame.
  • Remember: Type 2 does not mean insulin is never needed. Vomiting, dehydration, deep breathing or confusion still requires urgent assessment for a hyperglycemic emergency.

Module 11

Phenylketonuria: PKU

  • Recognize: Impaired phenylalanine metabolism allows toxic accumulation; screening identifies risk before visible developmental injury.
  • Act: Ensure prompt specialist follow-up, prescribed controlled-phenylalanine feeding and medical formula. Monitor phenylalanine levels, nutrition, growth and development.
  • Remember: The plan is lifelong and is not a zero-protein diet. Avoid aspartame and follow the metabolic dietitian's instructions to protect brain development while meeting nutritional needs.

Module 11

Galactosemia

  • Recognize: After milk exposure, a metabolic enzyme defect can cause poor feeding, vomiting, jaundice, liver dysfunction or sepsis.
  • Act: Escalate an ill newborn, support screening confirmation and promptly provide the prescribed lactose/galactose-free feeding plan. Monitor glucose, liver status, growth and infection.
  • Remember: This differs from simple lactose intolerance and can be life-threatening. Follow the specific formula and food plan; do not eliminate all carbohydrates.

Module 12

Dehydration

  • Recognize: Reduced urine and tears, dry mouth, tachycardia, lethargy or delayed capillary refill suggest fluid deficit; weight change helps quantify loss.
  • Act: Assess perfusion, weight, intake/output and electrolytes; use oral rehydration for suitable mild/moderate cases and prescribed isotonic IV fluids for severe dehydration or shock.
  • Remember: Reassess after replacement. Small frequent oral rehydration amounts help tolerance; poor perfusion or inability to drink needs urgent treatment.

Module 12

Gastroesophageal reflux disease

  • Recognize: Reflux becomes concerning when associated with feeding distress, poor growth or respiratory problems rather than simple thriving-infant spit-up.
  • Act: Assess growth, intake and respiratory symptoms; use the prescribed feeding and positioning plan while awake. Monitor response and escalate bleeding, bilious vomiting or severe illness.
  • Remember: Keep infants supine on a firm, flat surface for sleep. Reflux does not justify prone sleeping or an inclined sleep device.

Module 12

Pyloric stenosis

  • Recognize: Thickened pyloric muscle obstructs gastric emptying: progressive projectile nonbilious vomiting, hunger after vomiting and dehydration are characteristic.
  • Act: Assess hydration, weight and electrolytes; keep NPO and correct prescribed fluid/electrolyte deficits before surgery. Prepare for pyloromyotomy.
  • Remember: Stabilize before repair. The classic vomiting is nonbilious; bilious emesis suggests obstruction beyond the stomach and needs urgent evaluation.

Module 12

Celiac disease

  • Recognize: An immune response to gluten damages intestinal villi, causing poor growth, bloating, altered stools or iron deficiency.
  • Act: Assess growth and nutrition, support testing while gluten is still being eaten unless directed otherwise, and provide dietitian-guided treatment after diagnosis.
  • Remember: A lifelong gluten-free diet avoids wheat, barley and rye. Check labels and cross-contact; removing gluten before testing may obscure diagnosis.

Module 12

Appendicitis

  • Recognize: Pain often migrates from the umbilicus to the right lower abdomen with anorexia, nausea or fever; rigidity or diffuse pain suggests perforation.
  • Act: Keep NPO, assess pain and perfusion, and prepare prescribed IV fluids, analgesia, antibiotics and surgical review.
  • Remember: Do not give laxatives, enemas or abdominal heat for suspected appendicitis. Prompt assessment matters because perforation can lead to peritonitis and sepsis.

Module 12

Cleft lip and palate

  • Recognize: Incomplete fusion can impair feeding seal, growth and later hearing or speech; airway or aspiration concerns require assessment.
  • Act: Use the prescribed specialty feeding method, upright feeding and frequent burping. Monitor growth and airway; protect the surgical repair according to the team's instructions.
  • Remember: Feeding and oral-device restrictions differ by repair and surgeon. Follow exact postoperative instructions and maintain hearing, dental and speech follow-up.

Module 12

Gastroenteritis

  • Recognize: Vomiting and diarrhea from infection can rapidly dehydrate children; assess fever, blood in stool, exposure and overall appearance.
  • Act: Monitor hydration, weight, urine and perfusion; use oral rehydration and continue appropriate nutrition when tolerated. Give IV fluids if needed and use infection precautions.
  • Remember: Antimicrobials are for selected causes, not routine diarrhea. Avoid unprescribed antidiarrheals; lethargy, poor perfusion or inability to drink needs urgent care.

Module 12

Intussusception

  • Recognize: One bowel segment telescopes into another: episodic severe pain, knees drawn up, vomiting or lethargy; bloody mucus stool can appear late.
  • Act: Assess perfusion, keep NPO and prepare prescribed IV fluids, ultrasound and reduction. Air/contrast enema may treat suitable cases; complications may require surgery.
  • Remember: Do not wait for currant-jelly stool. Peritonitis, shock or bilious vomiting requires urgent action; report recurrence after apparent improvement.

Module 12

Hirschsprung disease

  • Recognize: Absent intestinal ganglion cells prevent normal movement, producing delayed meconium passage, distention and constipation or obstruction.
  • Act: Assess stooling, abdominal findings, hydration and nutrition; prepare for prescribed diagnostic evaluation and surgery. Watch closely for enterocolitis.
  • Remember: Fever, increasing distention, explosive diarrhea or a very ill appearance needs urgent review. Definitive treatment removes or bypasses the nonfunctioning bowel segment.

Module 12

Constipation

  • Recognize: Hard painful stools and withholding can form a cycle; overflow soiling may occur around retained stool.
  • Act: Assess stool history, diet and red flags. Use prescribed disimpaction/maintenance therapy, age-appropriate fluids/fiber and relaxed toilet routines with foot support.
  • Remember: Do not punish soiling. Bilious vomiting, severe distention or symptoms beginning in the newborn period needs evaluation for an underlying disorder.

Module 12

Meconium plug

  • Recognize: A thick plug obstructs the newborn colon, causing delayed stool passage, distention and possible vomiting.
  • Act: Assess obstruction, hydration and feeding; follow NPO, fluid and neonatal/surgical evaluation orders. A specialist contrast enema may diagnose and relieve the plug.
  • Remember: Persistent or recurrent symptoms require investigation for an underlying disorder. Bilious vomiting or an ill newborn is urgent, even after some stool passes.

Module 12

Meconium ileus

  • Recognize: Thick meconium obstructs the distal ileum, causing failure to stool, distention and bilious vomiting; it is strongly associated with cystic fibrosis.
  • Act: Obtain urgent neonatal evaluation; keep NPO and provide prescribed decompression and fluids. Specialist enema or surgery depends on severity and complications.
  • Remember: Evaluate for cystic fibrosis. Unlike a colonic meconium plug, ileus involves the small bowel; perforation, peritonitis or shock requires emergency treatment.

Module 12

Imperforate anus

  • Recognize: An absent or abnormal anal opening, failure to pass stool or stool from an abnormal site suggests an anorectal malformation.
  • Act: Inspect the perineum, assess abdominal distention and associated abnormalities, and prepare for prescribed neonatal/surgical management. Keep NPO if obstruction is suspected.
  • Remember: Do not force a rectal thermometer or other instrument into an abnormal opening. Repair and bowel-management plans depend on the anatomy.

Every exam: priority + dose checks
  • Clinical judgment: Recognize cues → analyze → prioritize → plan → act → evaluate. Compare with baseline; reassess the response.
  • Dose safety: Verify patient, allergies, drug, dose, route and time. Use kilograms for weight-based doses; check the safe range and maximum.
  • Calculate: Ordered dose ÷ available dose × volume. Example: 250 mg ÷ 125 mg × 5 mL = 10 mL. Clarify an unsafe or unclear order before giving it.

Rules to remember

  • Pain that escalates under a cast is an urgent cue.
  • Deep, rapid breathing with vomiting in diabetes suggests acidosis.
  • Think obstruction when abdominal pain comes in waves.

See this → think this

A child reports worsening pain despite analgesia, especially with passive finger movement.

Think…
  • Pressure inside a muscle compartment can compromise circulation before pulses disappear.

A child with type 1 diabetes has vomiting, dehydration and deep breathing.

Think…
  • Consider diabetic ketoacidosis.
  • The breathing pattern helps compensate for metabolic acidosis.

A toddler has sudden spells of pain, pulls up the knees, then seems better between episodes.

Think…
  • Intussusception is one segment of bowel sliding into another.
  • Bloody mucus can occur late.

What do I do first?

Musculoskeletal

  1. First action
    • Perform a focused neurovascular check and urgently notify the team.
    • Assess color, warmth, movement and sensation.
  2. Look for a response
    • Track changes in pain, sensation and perfusion while urgent treatment is arranged.

Endocrine & Metabolic

  1. First action
    • Check glucose and ketones, assess circulation and initiate the pediatric DKA pathway with the team.
  2. Look for a response
    • Follow neurologic status, hydration, glucose and electrolytes throughout treatment.

Gastrointestinal

  1. First action
    • Keep the child NPO, assess perfusion and obtain urgent evaluation.
    • Prepare for ultrasound and ordered treatment.
  2. Look for a response
    • Monitor pain, perfusion and abdominal findings, including recurrence after treatment.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • For a weighed diaper, about 1 gram gained equals 1 mL of urine. Use measured output and the child's weight to follow hydration.
  • Pain out of proportion under a cast, especially with passive stretch, can precede loss of a pulse.

Practice and memory cues

In a telescoping bowel, pain can surge and then ease. A quiet interval does not rule out an urgent problem.

A 9-year-old had a long arm cast applied 6 hours ago. He now rates his pain 9 of 10, unrelieved by two doses of opioid, and his fingers are pale and tingling. Which action should the nurse take first?

  1. Administer an additional dose of analgesic
  2. Elevate the arm and reassess in one hour
  3. Perform a neurovascular check and notify the provider immediately
  4. Apply ice over the cast at the elbow
Check the answer and why

Perform a neurovascular check and notify the provider immediately

Pain that analgesia does not touch is compartment syndrome , not an inadequate dose, and pallor with paresthesia adds two more of the 6 Ps. This is time-critical, so waiting an hour or giving more medication both delay the only thing that saves the limb.

A 10-month-old has episodes of sudden inconsolable crying during which he draws his knees to his chest, and he appears completely normal between episodes. His diaper contains stool mixed with blood and mucus. Which action is the priority?

  1. Reassure the parents that this is colic
  2. Notify the provider immediately; the findings suggest intussusception
  3. Begin an oral rehydration solution and reassess in 2 hours
  4. Apply a warm pack to the abdomen for comfort
Check the answer and why

Notify the provider immediately; the findings suggest intussusception

Episodic severe pain with knees drawn up, currant jelly stool and a sausage-shaped mass is intussusception, which needs air or barium enema reduction. The pain-free intervals make the child look completely well — that is the trap , and it is why this gets mistaken for colic.

Musculoskeletal, endocrine and GI disorders

Use pain pattern, hydration and breathing to tell an expected finding from a threat to the limb, the circulation or the bowel.

Rules to remember

  • Pain that escalates under a cast is an urgent cue.
  • Deep, rapid breathing with vomiting in diabetes suggests acidosis.
  • Think obstruction when abdominal pain comes in waves.

See this → think this

A child reports worsening pain despite analgesia, especially with passive finger movement.

Think…
  • Pressure inside a muscle compartment can compromise circulation before pulses disappear.

A child with type 1 diabetes has vomiting, dehydration and deep breathing.

Think…
  • Consider diabetic ketoacidosis.
  • The breathing pattern helps compensate for metabolic acidosis.

A toddler has sudden spells of pain, pulls up the knees, then seems better between episodes.

Think…
  • Intussusception is one segment of bowel sliding into another.
  • Bloody mucus can occur late.

What do I do first?

Musculoskeletal

  1. First action
    • Perform a focused neurovascular check and urgently notify the team.
    • Assess color, warmth, movement and sensation.
  2. Look for a response
    • Track changes in pain, sensation and perfusion while urgent treatment is arranged.

Endocrine & Metabolic

  1. First action
    • Check glucose and ketones, assess circulation and initiate the pediatric DKA pathway with the team.
  2. Look for a response
    • Follow neurologic status, hydration, glucose and electrolytes throughout treatment.

Gastrointestinal

  1. First action
    • Keep the child NPO, assess perfusion and obtain urgent evaluation.
    • Prepare for ultrasound and ordered treatment.
  2. Look for a response
    • Monitor pain, perfusion and abdominal findings, including recurrence after treatment.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • For a weighed diaper, about 1 gram gained equals 1 mL of urine. Use measured output and the child's weight to follow hydration.
  • Pain out of proportion under a cast, especially with passive stretch, can precede loss of a pulse.

Practice and memory cues

In a telescoping bowel, pain can surge and then ease. A quiet interval does not rule out an urgent problem.

A 9-year-old had a long arm cast applied 6 hours ago. He now rates his pain 9 of 10, unrelieved by two doses of opioid, and his fingers are pale and tingling. Which action should the nurse take first?

  1. Administer an additional dose of analgesic
  2. Elevate the arm and reassess in one hour
  3. Perform a neurovascular check and notify the provider immediately
  4. Apply ice over the cast at the elbow
Check the answer and why

Perform a neurovascular check and notify the provider immediately

Pain that analgesia does not touch is compartment syndrome , not an inadequate dose, and pallor with paresthesia adds two more of the 6 Ps. This is time-critical, so waiting an hour or giving more medication both delay the only thing that saves the limb.

A 10-month-old has episodes of sudden inconsolable crying during which he draws his knees to his chest, and he appears completely normal between episodes. His diaper contains stool mixed with blood and mucus. Which action is the priority?

  1. Reassure the parents that this is colic
  2. Notify the provider immediately; the findings suggest intussusception
  3. Begin an oral rehydration solution and reassess in 2 hours
  4. Apply a warm pack to the abdomen for comfort
Check the answer and why

Notify the provider immediately; the findings suggest intussusception

Episodic severe pain with knees drawn up, currant jelly stool and a sausage-shaped mass is intussusception, which needs air or barium enema reduction. The pain-free intervals make the child look completely well — that is the trap , and it is why this gets mistaken for colic.

Module 11 · Postpartum family and newborn nutrition 13 short topics

Module 11

Puerperium retrogressive and progressive changes

  • Recognize: Retrogressive changes reverse pregnancy adaptations, including uterine/vaginal involution; progressive changes include lactation, return of reproductive function and the parenting role.
  • Act: Assess physical recovery, feeding and adjustment together; support rest, comfort, education and follow-up.
  • Remember: Different systems recover at different rates. Postpartum care includes both returning toward baseline and developing new functions, not merely watching bleeding.

Module 11

Attachment/bonding

  • Recognize: Attachment develops through interaction; pain, fatigue, depression, difficult birth or separation can interfere.
  • Act: Offer skin-to-skin, rooming-in, feeding/care participation and practical support; include partners and assess persistent withdrawal or distress.
  • Remember: Bonding may take time. Do not assume poor attachment from one interaction, but investigate sustained lack of interest and barriers to safe infant care.

Module 11

Disillusionment / postpartum blues

  • Recognize: Actual parenting or the baby's characteristics may differ from expectations; transient tearfulness and mood shifts can occur during early postpartum adjustment.
  • Act: Invite feelings without judgment, arrange rest/practical help and assess mood, functioning and safety.
  • Remember: Baby blues should resolve within about two weeks; persistent, worsening or disabling symptoms need assessment rather than reassurance alone.

Module 11

Uterine involution

  • Recognize: A normally involuting fundus is firm, midline and descends over time; a boggy or high/right-displaced fundus is concerning.
  • Act: For a boggy right-displaced uterus, empty the bladder first, then reassess/massage; for a boggy midline uterus, massage first.
  • Remember: Assess bleeding and perfusion and summon help for hemorrhage; a full bladder can prevent effective contraction.

Module 11

Lochia

  • Recognize: Lochia progresses rubra to serosa to alba and should generally lighten; heavier bleeding, large clots or foul odor requires assessment.
  • Act: Check color, amount and associated fundal/vital-sign findings; quantify concerning loss rather than relying on visual estimates alone.
  • Remember: Persistent or renewed heavy red bleeding is not ordinary progression. A firm fundus with continued bleeding raises concern for causes other than atony.

Module 11

Cervical/vaginal involution

  • Recognize: The cervix narrows/firms after birth; vaginal swelling resolves as tissue heals. After vaginal birth the cervical opening commonly remains slit-shaped.
  • Act: Assess pain, wound approximation, discharge and function; teach perineal care and discuss lactation-related dryness.
  • Remember: Healing takes time, but worsening pain, concerning drainage or persistent functional problems need review rather than being labeled inevitable recovery.

Module 11

Systemic changes during post-partum

  • Recognize: Postpartum fluid shifts alter cardiac workload; diuresis removes extra fluid, while retention can impair uterine contraction. Constipation and perineal discomfort are common.
  • Act: Assess perfusion, voiding, bowel function, mobility and healing; support fluids, comfort and activity as appropriate.
  • Remember: Dyspnea, chest pain, fever, severe headache or heavy bleeding is not routine recovery and requires urgent assessment.

Module 11

Preparation for discharge

  • Recognize: Discharge readiness includes physical recovery, safe self/infant care, support and a clear follow-up plan.
  • Act: Use teach-back for lochia/wound care, medications, feeding, activity, contraception and urgent symptoms; arrange indicated immunizations/Rh prophylaxis and support.
  • Remember: Ovulation can precede the first period. Heavy bleeding, chest pain, dyspnea, severe headache, fever or thoughts of harm require prompt help.

Module 11

Lactation / colostrum

  • Recognize: Colostrum is present early and supplies protein and antibodies; milk volume rises over the following days. Prolactin supports production and oxytocin milk ejection.
  • Act: Support skin-to-skin, early feeding, effective latch and frequent feeding cues; assess painful latch rather than simply advising longer feeds.
  • Remember: Small early colostrum volume does not mean there is no milk; assess transfer and newborn intake.

Module 11

Assessing the newborn for adequate nutrition/nutritional allowances

  • Recognize: Adequate intake is reflected by observed milk transfer, age-appropriate wet/stool output and weight trend; poor feeding, lethargy or reduced urine needs assessment.
  • Act: Observe latch and swallowing, review output/weights and arrange feeding help when needed.
  • Remember: Aim for 8–12 breastfeeds daily; by about day five, at least six wet diapers is reassuring. Do not judge intake from feeding duration alone.

Module 11

Benefits of breastfeeding (maternal/fetal)

  • Recognize: Human milk provides nutrition and immune protection; breastfeeding also supports bonding and oxytocin-mediated uterine involution.
  • Act: Explain benefits, assess the family's informed feeding preference and provide practical latch/feeding support without judgment.
  • Remember: Colostrum's antibodies matter even before milk volume increases. Maternal benefits include lower breast/ovarian cancer risk; feeding safety and adequate infant intake remain priorities for every family.

Module 11

Engorgement

  • Recognize: Engorgement causes fullness, swelling and discomfort, often when milk volume rises or feeding is ineffective/infrequent.
  • Act: Assess latch and transfer, support frequent effective feeds and use the course's comfort measures: warmth briefly before feeding and cold afterward/between feeds.
  • Remember: A focal red painful breast with fever or flu-like symptoms needs evaluation for mastitis rather than routine engorgement advice alone.

Module 11

Formula feeding

  • Recognize: Formula feeding requires correct dilution, hygienic preparation and responsive feeding; over-dilution reduces nutrition and can cause harm.
  • Act: Teach exact package preparation, safe storage and supported feeding; never prop bottles or microwave them, and discard leftovers one hour after feeding starts.
  • Remember: Follow newborn hunger/fullness cues and monitor output/weight. Do not stretch formula with extra water.

Module 12 · Postpartum complications 9 short topics

Module 12

Postpartum hemorrhage (tone/trauma/tissue/thrombin)

  • Recognize: PPH: cumulative loss of at least 1,000 mL after vaginal or cesarean birth, OR bleeding with hypovolemia within 24 hours. Four Ts: Tone, Trauma, Tissue, Thrombin.
  • Act: Call help; assess fundus/bladder, quantify loss, give oxygen by mask and support ordered IV fluids, uterotonics and blood.
  • Remember: Boggy/right-displaced: empty bladder first, then massage; boggy/midline: massage first. Firm but bleeding: consider trauma, retained tissue or coagulopathy. Do not wait for a volume threshold to act.

Module 12

Subinvolution

  • Recognize: Subinvolution is delayed return of the uterus toward its prepregnant state; persistent red lochia and an unexpectedly enlarged fundus raise concern.
  • Act: Assess bleeding, uterine findings, infection signs and vital trends; report abnormalities and prepare evaluation for retained tissue or infection.
  • Remember: This is delayed involution, not expected afterpains. Management targets the cause and ongoing hemorrhage risk.

Module 12

Puerperal infection

  • Recognize: Endometritis can cause fever, uterine tenderness, pelvic pain and foul/purulent lochia; wounds or urinary infection can also cause postpartum illness.
  • Act: Assess vitals, pain, fundus, lochia, incisions and breasts; obtain ordered cultures and start prescribed antibiotics promptly.
  • Remember: Prolonged membrane rupture, cesarean birth, repeated examinations and retained fragments raise risk. Worsening perfusion or mental status requires sepsis escalation.

Module 12

Mastitis

  • Recognize: Mastitis may cause a focal red, hot, painful breast with fever and flu-like symptoms; milk stasis and nipple trauma contribute.
  • Act: Assess feeding technique and symptoms, arrange treatment and prescribed antibiotics when indicated, and support ongoing breastfeeding or expression as tolerated.
  • Remember: Abruptly stopping milk removal can worsen stasis; persistent focal swelling or failure to improve needs further evaluation.

Module 12

Thrombophlebitis

  • Recognize: Unilateral leg warmth, pain, swelling or redness suggests thrombophlebitis; sudden chest pain/dyspnea suggests pulmonary embolism.
  • Act: Report findings, compare leg measurements, anticipate prescribed anticoagulation and use ordered elevation/warm compresses; never massage the affected limb.
  • Remember: Prevention includes appropriate early mobility; management of a suspected or confirmed clot follows the individualized treatment plan rather than unsupervised exercise.

Module 12

GU system disorders

  • Recognize: Anesthesia, edema or trauma may cause retention/overflow leakage; dysuria, fever or flank pain raises infection concern.
  • Act: Assess bladder distention, ability to void and urine output; follow ordered bladder-emptying and infection evaluation/treatment plans.
  • Remember: A full bladder can impair uterine contraction and increase bleeding risk. Persistent incontinence, pain or difficulty voiding warrants follow-up rather than dismissal as normal childbirth recovery.

Module 12

Postpartum depression / psychosis

  • Recognize: Depression causes persistent sadness, anxiety or impaired functioning; psychosis may involve delusions, hallucinations, confusion or paranoia.
  • Act: Screen and ask directly about self/infant-harm thoughts; arrange treatment/support. For psychosis or immediate danger, protect parent/infant, stay with the client and obtain emergency help.
  • Remember: Baby blues are brief and self-limited; loss of reality or safety concerns is an emergency, not ordinary adjustment.

Module 12

Infant with illness/physical challenge

  • Recognize: Infant illness or physical differences may bring fear, guilt, grief and loss of control, while separation can complicate bonding.
  • Act: Explain the condition plainly, check understanding and invite safe participation in comfort/care; coordinate NICU teaching, specialists, social work and peer support.
  • Remember: Family involvement should match the infant's condition and the family's readiness, without forcing a particular emotional response.

Neonatal death

  • Recognize: Neonatal death creates bereavement while the parent still needs postpartum physical recovery and follow-up. Families differ in cultural, spiritual and memory-making preferences.
  • Act: Offer privacy, honest compassionate explanations and choices about time with the infant; coordinate bereavement resources and continued care.
  • Remember: Do not impose a grief timetable or pressure the family to accept or decline contact and keepsakes.

Module 13 · Normal and high-risk newborn 19 short topics

Module 13

Vital sign measurement in the newborn

  • Recognize: Assess newborn heart rate, respirations, temperature, color and effort. Usual course ranges are heart rate 110–160/min, respirations 30–60/min and axillary temperature 36.5–37.5°C.
  • Act: Count carefully, trend findings and evaluate abnormal breathing, temperature instability or poor perfusion promptly.
  • Remember: Irregular breathing can occur, but grunting, flaring, retractions or central cyanosis is not a routine normal finding.

Module 13

Systemic physiologic adaptation (cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, neuromuscular)

  • Recognize: Lung expansion begins air breathing and lowers pulmonary resistance; loss of placental circulation raises systemic resistance and changes fetal shunt flow.
  • Act: Assess respiratory effort, oxygenation/perfusion, feeding, stool passage, flexed tone and symmetric movement during transition.
  • Remember: Transition involves several systems together; weak feeding or poor tone can accompany illness even when one vital sign appears reassuring.

Module 13

Newborn reflexes

  • Recognize: Rooting/sucking support feeding; Moro, grasp, stepping, tonic-neck and Babinski responses help assess neurologic function.
  • Act: Check expected responses and symmetry while considering gestational age and infant state; assess coordinated suck-swallow-breathe before oral feeding.
  • Remember: An absent/asymmetric response may suggest injury or neurologic problems; interpret the whole examination rather than diagnosing from one sleepy response.

Module 13

APGAR scoring

  • Recognize: Apgar scores appearance, pulse, grimace, activity and respirations, each zero to two, at one and five minutes.
  • Act: Assess the newborn's condition immediately and provide needed support without waiting for the score; document and repeat scoring as indicated.
  • Remember: Total seven to ten is reassuring; a low score signals concern. Apgar describes transition and never delays resuscitation.

Module 13

Hyperbilirubinemia/jaundice

  • Recognize: Jaundice in the first 24 hours needs prompt evaluation; bilirubin risk depends on age in hours, gestation and other factors.
  • Act: Obtain ordered bilirubin assessment, support feeding and prescribed phototherapy; protect eyes and monitor temperature, hydration and output during treatment.
  • Remember: Severe unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia can injure the brain. Do not label all jaundice after day one harmless or treat by appearance alone.

Module 13

Vernix/lanugo/milia/erythema toxicum

  • Recognize: Vernix is a protective white coating; lanugo is fine hair; milia are small white facial bumps; erythema toxicum is a common transient rash.
  • Act: Describe appearance/distribution, provide gentle skin care and assess accompanying symptoms or unusual changes.
  • Remember: Familiar benign findings differ from central cyanosis, infection signs or an ill-appearing newborn; the skin finding must fit the complete assessment.

Module 13

Fontanelles/sutures/molding/caput/cephalhematoma

  • Recognize: Molding reshapes the skull after birth; caput is scalp edema that crosses sutures, while cephalhematoma is blood beneath periosteum and does not.
  • Act: Assess head shape, sutures/fontanelles and swelling; report concerning progression and monitor jaundice risk with cephalhematoma.
  • Remember: A bulging fontanelle can suggest raised pressure; a sunken one can suggest dehydration. Interpret findings with infant state and clinical status.

Module 13

Newborn medications (required/recommended)

  • Recognize: Newborn prophylaxis includes vitamin K for bleeding prevention, erythromycin eye ointment for gonococcal infection prevention and indicated hepatitis B immunization.
  • Act: Verify maternal infection status, orders, consent requirements and administration details; explain each purpose and document care.
  • Remember: Vitamin K is not a vaccine. Eye prophylaxis does not replace assessment or treatment of an infant with infection signs.

Module 13

Metabolic screening

  • Recognize: Metabolic screening identifies selected inherited/metabolic/endocrine conditions before symptoms may appear; an infant can look well and still need testing.
  • Act: Obtain the required specimen at the prescribed age, document feeding/collection circumstances and ensure results, recalls or repeat specimens receive follow-up.
  • Remember: Screening flags risk rather than confirming every diagnosis; early discharge or early collection may require repeat testing under the screening program.

Module 13

Circumcision

  • Recognize: Circumcision requires informed consent, analgesia and suitability assessment; hypospadias/epispadias may require preserving foreskin for repair.
  • Act: Monitor bleeding, pain and voiding; teach gentle device-specific care and signs needing help.

Module 13

Initiating and maintaining newborn respirations

  • Recognize: At birth assess breathing, tone and color; apnea, gasping, grunting, retractions or central cyanosis requires immediate attention.
  • Act: Dry/warm, position the airway and promptly support breathing with the neonatal team according to the resuscitation protocol.
  • Remember: Do not wait for the one-minute Apgar to help a newborn who is not breathing; ventilation takes priority over completing routine measurements.

Module 13

Methods of heat loss

  • Recognize: Evaporation loses heat from wet skin; conduction transfers heat to a cold surface; convection involves moving air; radiation involves nearby cold objects.
  • Act: Dry promptly, use warm surfaces, reduce drafts and keep away from cold windows; use skin-to-skin/warming appropriately.
  • Remember: Cold stress increases oxygen and glucose use, raising hypoxia, hypoglycemia and acidosis risk.

Module 13

Altered gestational age or weight

  • Recognize: Prematurity concerns organ maturity; small/large for gestational age concerns weight relative to age. Both affect feeding, temperature and glucose risk.
  • Act: Assess gestational age and growth category, monitor warmth/glucose/respirations and verify feeding coordination; prepare additional support as indicated.
  • Remember: Size does not establish maturity: a large infant may have immature lungs; a small term infant is not automatically premature. Postterm means at least 42 weeks.

Module 13

Respiratory distress syndrome/surfactant

  • Recognize: Surfactant deficiency makes immature lungs difficult to expand, producing tachypnea, grunting, flaring, retractions or cyanosis.
  • Act: Obtain neonatal help, support prescribed oxygen/ventilation, temperature and glucose care, and prepare surfactant therapy when ordered.
  • Remember: Antenatal corticosteroids promote lung maturation; surfactant replacement treats deficiency after birth. Birth weight alone does not establish lung maturity.

Module 13

Meconium aspiration syndrome

  • Recognize: Meconium exposure can accompany respiratory distress from aspiration; assess the newborn rather than assuming stained fluid alone proves aspiration syndrome.
  • Act: Alert the neonatal team, assess breathing/heart rate and support ventilation according to the resuscitation plan; monitor oxygenation and ongoing distress.
  • Remember: Meconium-stained fluid is a risk cue. Respiratory assessment determines urgency, and necessary breathing support must not be delayed.

Module 13

Apnea / apparent life threatening event (ALE)

  • Recognize: Apnea is a pause of at least 20 seconds, or a shorter pause with concerning bradycardia/color change; preterm infants are vulnerable.
  • Act: Assess breathing, heart rate and oxygenation immediately and provide indicated neonatal support; investigate the cause.
  • Remember: ALTE/ALE is older terminology. BRUE describes a resolved unexplained episode after assessment; a newborn does not meet the lower-risk age criterion.

Module 13

Sudden infant death syndrome

  • Recognize: SIDS is an unexplained infant death after evaluation; sleep-environment risk reduction is central to prevention teaching.
  • Act: Teach supine sleep on a firm, flat separate surface without pillows, loose bedding or soft objects; promote room-sharing without bed-sharing and avoid smoke exposure.
  • Remember: Supervised awake tummy time is different from sleep positioning; prone sleep is not the safe-sleep default.

Module 13

Newborns at risk for maternal infections

  • Recognize: Maternal infection, prolonged membrane rupture, prematurity or inadequate GBS prophylaxis can increase newborn infection risk. Signs may include temperature instability, poor feeding, apnea or lethargy.
  • Act: Review exposure history, assess subtle changes and promptly escalate for ordered cultures, antibiotics and support.
  • Remember: Newborn sepsis may cause low or unstable temperature rather than obvious fever; apparent sleepiness can be illness.

Module 13

Neonatal drug withdrawal / Fetal alcohol syndrome

  • Recognize: Withdrawal may cause tremors, high-pitched cry, irritability, increased tone and feeding problems. Fetal alcohol syndrome involves growth, facial and neurodevelopmental effects.
  • Act: Use structured assessment, reduce overstimulation, support feeding/family care and administer prescribed withdrawal treatment when indicated.
  • Remember: Withdrawal is a post-exposure adaptation; alcohol-related developmental injury is a different process. There is no known safe amount of alcohol in pregnancy.

Every exam: priority + dose checks
  • Clinical judgment: Recognize cues → analyze → prioritize → plan → act → evaluate. Compare with baseline; reassess the response.
  • Dose safety: Verify patient, allergies, drug, dose, route and time. Use kilograms for weight-based doses; check the safe range and maximum.
  • Calculate: Ordered dose ÷ available dose × volume. Example: 250 mg ÷ 125 mg × 5 mL = 10 mL. Clarify an unsafe or unclear order before giving it.

Rules to remember

  • Check both uterine tone and position when bleeding increases.
  • Judge feeding by transfer and output.
  • A newborn loses heat quickly through wet skin.
  • Link prematurity to the work of breathing.

What do I do first?

Postpartum Assessment & Hemorrhage

  1. First action
    • Activate the hemorrhage response for heavy bleeding.
    • For this course cue, empty the bladder first, then massage the fundus.
    • Give ordered uterotonics and oxygen by mask per the course pathway.
  2. Look for a response
    • Recheck fundal firmness and position, quantified blood loss and perfusion.

Newborn Feeding

  1. First action
    • Observe a complete feed, check latch, and review diaper output and weight trend with the team.
  2. Look for a response
    • Look for audible swallowing and improving age-appropriate output and weight trajectory.

The Normal Newborn & Thermoregulation

  1. First action
    • Dry promptly, replace damp linens, and use skin-to-skin or a warmer as appropriate.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess temperature, breathing and color.

The High-Risk Newborn & Course Review

  1. First action
    • Get neonatal assistance, assess oxygenation and support temperature and breathing.
  2. Look for a response
    • Recheck work of breathing, oxygenation and response to ordered support.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • Check the Apgar score at 1 and 5 minutes. A newborn who needs ventilation needs immediate support; care begins before the score is completed.
  • A boggy uterus suggests atony. Persistent bright bleeding with a firm uterus points toward trauma. Escalate heavy bleeding in either case.

Practice and memory cues

A wet infant loses heat by evaporation. A cold scale causes conduction. A draft causes convection. A cold nearby window causes radiation.

A client 3 hours postpartum has a firm midline fundus at the umbilicus, yet a steady trickle of bright red blood without clots continues. Which action does the nurse take?

  1. Massage the fundus vigorously
  2. Inspect the perineum, vagina and cervix for a laceration
  3. Encourage her to void and reassess
  4. Document the finding as normal lochia rubra
Check the answer and why

Inspect the perineum, vagina and cervix for a laceration

A firm fundus with continued bleeding means the uterus is not the source , so look for a laceration or hematoma, classically a bright red continuous trickle without clots. Massage will not help a uterus that is already contracted, and this is not normal lochia.

A newborn has a scalp swelling that crosses the suture lines and was present at birth. How does the nurse document this finding?

  1. Cephalohematoma, which raises the risk of jaundice
  2. Caput succedaneum, which resolves in a few days
  3. Molding, which requires no documentation
  4. A subgaleal hemorrhage requiring immediate surgery
Check the answer and why

Caput succedaneum, which resolves in a few days

Caput succedaneum crosses suture lines and resolves within days; a cephalohematoma is bounded by sutures, does not cross them, and carries a higher jaundice risk as the blood breaks down. Whether it crosses the suture line is the entire discrimination.

A newborn born at 28 weeks is receiving gavage feedings. On day 6 the abdomen is distended, a feeding is returned undigested, and the diaper contains bloody stool. Which action does the nurse take first?

  1. Stop all feedings and report the findings
  2. Give the next feeding more slowly over a longer time
  3. Place the newborn prone and reassess the abdomen in 2 hours
  4. Change the newborn from gavage feedings to nipple feedings

Stop all feedings and report the findings

A distended abdomen, bloody stool and feeding intolerance point to NEC , so all feeds are stopped immediately and reported — feeding a hypoxic gut makes the ischemia worse. Slowing the feeding or changing the route is still feeding, so it does not protect the gut.

Module 5

Blood transfusions

  • Recognize: Fever, chills, back pain, rash, dyspnea or hypotension during a transfusion may signal a reaction.
  • Act: Stop the transfusion, assess airway and circulation, maintain IV access with saline and new tubing per protocol, and notify the provider/blood bank.
  • Remember: Verify identity and product before starting; monitor closely. Do not restart suspected reaction blood. Hemolysis and allergy are different reaction mechanisms.

Module 5

Epistaxis

  • Recognize: Nosebleeds often follow irritated nasal mucosa, dry air, trauma or nose picking; recurrent bleeding can reflect a clotting disorder.
  • Act: Sit upright, lean forward and apply continuous pressure to the soft nose as instructed. Assess blood loss and persistent bleeding.
  • Remember: Do not tilt the head back: swallowing blood hides the loss and may cause vomiting. Persistent or severe bleeding needs further evaluation.

Module 5

Sickle cell anemia and crisis

  • Recognize: Sickled cells obstruct flow and hemolyze: pain, pallor and jaundice occur. Fever, chest symptoms or neurologic changes are urgent.
  • Act: Assess oxygenation and perfusion; provide rapid prescribed analgesia and appropriate hydration. Escalate fever or suspected acute chest syndrome immediately.
  • Remember: Avoid overhydration. Give oxygen for hypoxemia; an uncomplicated pain crisis does not routinely require transfusion. Teach trigger prevention and infection precautions.

Module 5

Iron deficiency anemia

  • Recognize: Pallor, fatigue, irritability and pica suggest reduced hemoglobin; premature infants and toddlers with excessive milk intake are vulnerable.
  • Act: Assess diet and CBC/iron studies; give prescribed iron and iron-rich foods. Teach safe storage because iron overdose is dangerous.
  • Remember: Milk can displace iron-rich meals. Liquid iron may stain teeth; use the taught technique. Dark stools can occur; adherence and follow-up confirm response.

Module 5

Hemophilia

  • Recognize: Factor VIII or IX deficiency causes prolonged bleeding and deep joint or muscle bleeding; a painful swollen joint suggests hemarthrosis.
  • Act: Assess bleeding and neurovascular status; give prescribed clotting-factor replacement promptly. Protect injured joints and seek urgent care for head injury or internal bleeding signs.
  • Remember: Avoid unnecessary invasive procedures and medicines that worsen bleeding. Do not assume a small external wound excludes serious internal bleeding.

Module 5

Leukemia

  • Recognize: Malignant immature cells crowd normal marrow: anemia causes fatigue, thrombocytopenia causes bruising and neutropenia increases infection risk.
  • Act: Monitor CBC, fever, bleeding, pain and treatment effects. Use infection and bleeding precautions; promptly report fever during neutropenia.
  • Remember: A high total WBC does not mean effective immune protection. Treatment also risks tumor lysis, so monitor ordered electrolytes, urine output and hydration.

Module 5

Nephroblastoma: Wilms tumor

  • Recognize: A painless abdominal mass in a young child may be accompanied by hematuria or hypertension.
  • Act: Avoid palpating or pressing on the abdomen when Wilms tumor is suspected. Monitor BP, urine and abdominal findings; prepare for specialist imaging and treatment.
  • Remember: Protect the tumor from rupture. Care commonly includes surgery and chemotherapy; monitor the remaining kidney and provide family support.

Module 5

Brain cancer

  • Recognize: Morning headache, vomiting, gait change, visual disturbance or altered behavior can reflect a tumor and increased intracranial pressure.
  • Act: Trend neurologic status, pupils, vital signs and seizure activity; promptly escalate deterioration. Prepare for imaging and prescribed tumor-directed care.
  • Remember: Tumor location affects the clues. Persistent vomiting plus neurologic change should not be treated as a simple stomach illness.

Module 6

Allergic response

  • Recognize: Anaphylaxis can rapidly cause airway swelling, wheezing, vomiting or poor circulation after exposure; skin findings may be absent.
  • Act: Recognize the emergency, summon help and give prescribed IM epinephrine promptly in the anterolateral thigh. Support airway, oxygenation and circulation; monitor response.
  • Remember: Antihistamines do not replace epinephrine. Teach avoidance, the emergency plan and correct auto-injector use.

Module 6

HIV

  • Recognize: HIV damages CD4 immune cells; recurrent infection, poor growth and oral problems can occur.
  • Act: Support consistent combination antiretroviral therapy, monitor growth and ordered labs, and promptly assess infection symptoms. Use standard precautions and individualized vaccine guidance.
  • Remember: Treatment is long term and adherence matters. Ordinary social contact does not transmit HIV; avoid isolation or stigma while teaching actual transmission risks.

Module 6

Burns

  • Recognize: Skin injury causes fluid loss, infection risk and pain; facial burns, soot or respiratory change raise airway concern.
  • Act: Stop exposure, assess airway and perfusion, prevent hypothermia and provide prescribed fluids and pain care. Cover appropriately and monitor urine output and circulation.
  • Remember: Never apply ice to a major burn. Healing also needs nutrition, infection surveillance and movement/positioning to prevent contractures.

Module 6

Atopic and contact dermatitis

  • Recognize: Atopic dermatitis is recurrent itchy inflammation; contact dermatitis follows an irritant or allergen at the exposed skin.
  • Act: Remove triggers, protect skin, use gentle bathing and prescribed topical therapy. Keep nails short, moisturize as directed and watch for infection from scratching.
  • Remember: Control the itch-scratch cycle. Eczema is not contagious; harsh scrubbing and fragranced products can worsen irritation.

Module 6

Acne

  • Recognize: Inflamed pilosebaceous follicles cause comedones and inflammatory lesions; severity can affect adolescent self-image.
  • Act: Use gentle cleansing and prescribed therapy consistently. Discuss adherence, skin irritation and psychosocial impact without blame.
  • Remember: Scrubbing or squeezing is not a cure and can worsen injury. Improvement takes time; review medication-specific precautions rather than stopping treatment after a few days.

Module 6

Pertussis

  • Recognize: Paroxysmal coughing, posttussive vomiting or a whoop suggests pertussis; young infants may have apnea without a typical whoop.
  • Act: Assess breathing and hydration; use droplet precautions and prescribed antibiotics. Escalate apnea or distress and follow guidance for exposed contacts.
  • Remember: Vaccination reduces risk. An infant's apnea can be the key clue; do not wait for the classic sound before seeking care.

Module 6

Impetigo

  • Recognize: Superficial bacterial lesions rupture and leave honey-colored crusts; scratching spreads infection to nearby skin or contacts.
  • Act: Use gentle cleansing and prescribed topical or oral antibiotics. Encourage hand hygiene, short nails and separate personal items.
  • Remember: Limit scratching and follow school-return guidance. Watch for worsening redness, fever or deeper infection; the honey crust is a recognition clue, not a reason to skip assessment.

Module 6

Scarlet fever

  • Recognize: Group A strep can produce fever, sore throat, a sandpaper-like rash and strawberry tongue.
  • Act: Assess hydration, swallowing and illness severity; obtain prescribed testing and administer the full prescribed antibiotic course. Use appropriate infection precautions.
  • Remember: The rash accompanies a streptococcal infection. Completing treatment and follow-up matters; new cardiac or joint symptoms require assessment.

Module 6

Pediculosis capitis

  • Recognize: Scalp itching and nits attached to hair suggest head lice; the infestation spreads through close contact and shared items.
  • Act: Use the prescribed or recommended pediculicide correctly, remove nits as directed and assess close contacts. Clean relevant clothing and bedding according to instructions.
  • Remember: Lice are not evidence of poor character or hygiene. Do not use unsafe pesticides or share combs and hats.

Module 6

Pinworms

  • Recognize: Nighttime perianal itching and disturbed sleep are typical; eggs are spread from hands to mouth.
  • Act: Collect the ordered morning tape test before bathing/toileting. Give prescribed antiparasitic therapy and reinforce handwashing, short nails and laundering of bedding.
  • Remember: Follow instructions about repeat dosing and household treatment. Treating the child without reducing egg spread can allow reinfection.

Module 6

Mononucleosis: EBV

  • Recognize: Fatigue, fever, sore throat and lymphadenopathy suggest EBV; splenic enlargement creates injury risk.
  • Act: Support hydration and rest, assess severe swallowing or breathing difficulty, and teach activity restrictions until cleared.
  • Remember: Avoid contact sports or abdominal trauma when the spleen may be enlarged. Antibiotics do not treat EBV itself; fatigue can persist beyond the acute illness.

Module 6

Measles

  • Recognize: Fever, cough, coryza and conjunctivitis precede a spreading rash; Koplik spots can appear in the mouth.
  • Act: Isolate using airborne precautions, assess hydration and breathing, and notify the appropriate clinical/public-health team. Provide prescribed supportive treatment.
  • Remember: Measles is highly contagious and vaccine preventable. Identify exposure risk promptly; a routine waiting room can expose vulnerable people.

Module 6

Rubella

  • Recognize: A mild fever, rash and lymph-node swelling may occur; fetal harm is the major concern when a pregnant person is infected.
  • Act: Use appropriate precautions and assess exposure history; avoid exposure of pregnant contacts and involve public-health/clinical guidance.
  • Remember: MMR prevents rubella, but live vaccine is not administered during pregnancy. A mild illness in the child can still carry serious pregnancy consequences.

Module 6

Varicella

  • Recognize: Itchy lesions appear in crops, so macules, vesicles and crusts coexist; fever may accompany the rash.
  • Act: Use airborne and contact precautions, protect skin from scratching, support hydration and give prescribed treatment for the child's risk level.
  • Remember: Avoid aspirin in children with viral illness. Keep susceptible pregnant or immunocompromised contacts away; follow isolation guidance until lesions have crusted.

Module 6

Fifth disease

  • Recognize: Parvovirus B19 often causes slapped-cheek redness followed by a lacy rash; joint symptoms may occur.
  • Act: Provide supportive care and assess exposure risks. Promptly involve the provider for a child with hemolytic disease or immune compromise and for pregnant exposed contacts.
  • Remember: Infection can suppress red-cell production. The familiar rash may appear after the most contagious phase, so exposure history matters.

Module 6

Conjunctivitis

  • Recognize: Red eyes with irritation and discharge may be viral, bacterial or allergic; symptoms alone do not always establish the cause.
  • Act: Clean drainage gently, use hand hygiene and avoid shared towels. Give prescribed treatment and reassess for worsening symptoms.
  • Remember: Eye pain, photophobia or visual change needs prompt evaluation. Do not automatically use antibiotics for every red eye or reuse another person's eye drops.

Module 6

Fungal skin infections

  • Recognize: Tinea can produce scaly circular skin lesions or scalp involvement; the location changes the treatment plan.
  • Act: Keep affected skin clean and dry, give prescribed antifungal therapy and avoid sharing combs, hats or towels. Assess contacts or pets when advised.
  • Remember: Scalp infection often needs systemic treatment; topical cream alone may be inadequate. Complete therapy even if the visible rash improves.

Module 7

Ventriculoperitoneal shunt

  • Recognize: A shunt drains excess CSF; headache, vomiting, irritability, lethargy or a bulging fontanel can suggest malfunction or infection.
  • Act: Trend neurologic status, head growth in infants, temperature and the shunt tract. Escalate new neurologic changes or infection signs promptly.
  • Remember: Do not pump or manipulate the shunt unless specifically ordered. Families need a clear plan for recognizing obstruction and obtaining urgent review.

Module 7

Increased intracranial pressure

  • Recognize: Changing level of consciousness, headache, vomiting or pupil changes suggest rising pressure; infants may show a bulging fontanel or increasing head size.
  • Act: Assess airway and neurologic status urgently; keep head/neck aligned and follow positioning and ICP-management orders. Minimize avoidable strain and monitor trends.
  • Remember: Bradycardia with widened pulse pressure and irregular respirations is a late, dangerous pattern. Do not wait for the full triad.

Module 7

Head injury

  • Recognize: Confusion, worsening headache, repeated vomiting, unequal pupils or seizure after trauma may indicate serious brain injury.
  • Act: Maintain airway and spinal precautions when indicated; perform serial neurologic checks and escalate deterioration. Follow concussion return-to-school and activity guidance.
  • Remember: A child who initially appears well can worsen. After concussion, use a gradual supported return; no same-day return to risky sport.

Module 7

Seizures

  • Recognize: Abnormal electrical activity may cause loss of awareness, staring or convulsive movements; prolonged events threaten breathing and safety.
  • Act: Time the seizure, protect the head, clear hazards and position to maintain the airway. Give prescribed rescue treatment and seek emergency help for a prolonged seizure.
  • Remember: Do not restrain or place anything in the mouth. Reassess breathing and neurologic status afterward; document the event and recovery.

Module 7

Bacterial meningitis

  • Recognize: Infants may have poor feeding, irritability or a bulging fontanel; older children may have fever, headache and neck stiffness.
  • Act: Treat as an emergency: assess airway/perfusion, institute appropriate isolation, obtain ordered cultures without delaying antibiotics and perform frequent neurologic checks.
  • Remember: Lumbar puncture requires safety assessment when increased ICP is suspected. Follow-up may include hearing testing; contact prophylaxis depends on the organism.

Module 7

Hydrocephalus

  • Recognize: Excess CSF enlarges ventricles; infants may show rapid head growth, prominent veins, bulging fontanel or downward-looking eyes.
  • Act: Measure and trend head circumference and neurologic findings; assess feeding and signs of raised ICP. Prepare for ordered imaging and CSF-diversion treatment.
  • Remember: Early detection protects brain function. After shunt placement, vomiting, lethargy or recurring pressure signs may indicate obstruction or infection.

Module 7

Myelomeningocele

  • Recognize: An open spinal defect contains neural tissue; motor, bowel and bladder deficits and hydrocephalus may coexist.
  • Act: Protect the sac with the ordered sterile moist nonadherent dressing, position to avoid pressure, prevent contamination and latex exposure, and prepare for repair.
  • Remember: Never leave the sac dry or compressed. Ongoing care monitors mobility, skin, bladder/bowel function and signs of increasing intracranial pressure.

Module 7

Cerebral palsy

  • Recognize: A nonprogressive brain injury affects movement and posture; spasticity, feeding problems, communication difficulties or seizures may coexist.
  • Act: Assess the child's abilities and aspiration risk; support safe feeding, positioning, mobility, communication and therapy goals. Encourage independence and family participation.
  • Remember: The brain injury is nonprogressive, but care needs change with growth. Motor impairment does not automatically imply intellectual disability.

Module 7

ADHD

  • Recognize: Persistent inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity interferes with functioning across settings; assess sleep, learning and other possible contributors.
  • Act: Use structured routines, short instructions, positive reinforcement and coordinated home/school support. Monitor prescribed medication effects, appetite, growth and sleep.
  • Remember: Medication is one part of care. Describe behavior without blame and verify that symptoms cause impairment rather than labeling ordinary activity alone.

Module 7

Reye syndrome

  • Recognize: Vomiting and acute neurologic deterioration after a viral illness can indicate hepatic dysfunction and cerebral edema; aspirin exposure is a key association.
  • Act: Escalate urgently, monitor airway, glucose and neurologic status, and prepare for intensive supportive care.
  • Remember: Avoid aspirin in children with viral illness unless a specialist directs it for a specific condition. Check combination products rather than relying only on brand names.

Every exam: priority + dose checks
  • Clinical judgment: Recognize cues → analyze → prioritize → plan → act → evaluate. Compare with baseline; reassess the response.
  • Dose safety: Verify patient, allergies, drug, dose, route and time. Use kilograms for weight-based doses; check the safe range and maximum.
  • Calculate: Ordered dose ÷ available dose × volume. Example: 250 mg ÷ 125 mg × 5 mL = 10 mL. Clarify an unsafe or unclear order before giving it.

Rules to remember

  • Recognize fever as urgent during cancer treatment.
  • An airway or circulation change makes an allergic reaction an emergency.
  • Protect the child and time the seizure.

What do I do first?

Hematology & Childhood Neoplasms

  1. First action
    • Notify the oncology team immediately and follow the febrile-neutropenia pathway.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess perfusion, mental status, temperature and response to timely treatment.

Immune, Infectious Disease, Skin & Burns

  1. First action
    • Call for help and give intramuscular epinephrine per the emergency protocol.
    • Support breathing and circulation.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess airway, respiratory effort, perfusion and recurrence of symptoms.

Neurologic & Neuromuscular

  1. First action
    • Protect the head, clear hazards, time the seizure and turn to the side when safe.
    • Keep objects out of the mouth.
  2. Look for a response
    • Afterward assess breathing, responsiveness and return toward baseline.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • Time a seizure from onset. A convulsive seizure reaching 5 minutes, or repeated seizures without recovery, needs an emergency response.
  • Compare behavior and responsiveness to the child's baseline. An infant may show irritability or a bulging fontanelle rather than an adult pattern.

Practice and memory cues

Red cells carry oxygen, platelets help stop bleeding and neutrophils fight infection. Decide which function is failing before choosing a precaution.

A nurse receives report on four children. Which child should the nurse assess first?

  1. An 8-year-old with sickle cell disease who has sudden chest pain, fever and dyspnea
  2. A 3-year-old with iron deficiency anemia who is pale and tired
  3. A 6-year-old with hemophilia who has mild knee swelling after bumping a table
  4. A 15-year-old with mononucleosis who reports a sore throat
Check the answer and why

An 8-year-old with sickle cell disease who has sudden chest pain, fever and dyspnea

Chest pain with fever and dyspnea in sickle cell disease is acute chest syndrome , a life-threatening emergency. The other three are expected findings for their diagnoses — pallor in iron deficiency, a minor bleed in hemophilia, and pharyngitis in mono.

A 7-year-old at a school event develops hives, lip swelling and audible stridor minutes after eating a cookie containing peanuts. Which action should the school nurse take first?

  1. Administer oral diphenhydramine
  2. Administer intramuscular epinephrine
  3. Apply high-flow oxygen by mask
  4. Call the parents for permission to treat
Check the answer and why

Administer intramuscular epinephrine

IM epinephrine is the first action in anaphylaxis — it is the only agent that reverses airway edema and vasodilation. Antihistamines are adjuncts, oxygen supports but does not treat the cause, and calling for permission delays a life-saving drug.

A 5-year-old is being observed after a fall from a playground structure. Which finding should the nurse report as the priority?

  1. The child reports a headache
  2. The child, previously alert and chatty, is now confused and difficult to arouse
  3. The child vomited once shortly after the fall
  4. The heart rate has drifted from 96 to 88
Check the answer and why

The child, previously alert and chatty, is now confused and difficult to arouse

A change in level of consciousness is the earliest sign of neurologic deterioration and outranks the others. Cushing's triad — rising BP, falling HR, falling RR — is a late finding, so waiting for the heart rate to drop meaningfully means waiting too long.

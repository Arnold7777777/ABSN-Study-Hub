Module 8

Bacterial epiglottitis

  • Recognize: Drooling, painful swallowing, tripod posture, stridor and a very ill appearance suggest a threatened upper airway.
  • Act: Keep the child calm and upright, summon emergency airway support, and prepare prescribed oxygen and treatment without provoking distress.
  • Remember: Do not inspect the throat with a tongue blade or force the child supine. Airway management comes before routine testing.

Module 8

Asthma

  • Recognize: Wheeze, cough, prolonged expiration and chest tightness reflect variable obstruction. Exhaustion, confusion or a silent chest with distress is dangerous.
  • Act: Assess air entry, work of breathing and oxygenation; sit upright, give prescribed rescue therapy and reassess. Escalate severe or worsening symptoms.
  • Remember: Rescue bronchodilators relieve bronchospasm; inhaled corticosteroids control inflammation. Teach spacer technique, trigger management and the action plan.

Module 8

Nasopharyngitis: common cold

  • Recognize: Runny or blocked nose, sneezing and cough usually indicate a viral upper respiratory infection; poor feeding can worsen dehydration in infants.
  • Act: Assess breathing, intake and wet diapers. Use saline and gentle nasal suction before feeds, with appropriate fluids and comfort measures.
  • Remember: Antibiotics do not treat the cold virus. Check before using cough/cold products in children; worsening breathing or hydration needs review.

Module 8

Bronchiolitis: RSV

  • Recognize: Cold symptoms progress to fast breathing, wheeze/crackles and feeding difficulty; young infants may present with apnea.
  • Act: Monitor work of breathing, oxygenation, apnea and hydration. Use gentle nasal suction and prescribed oxygen or fluids when indicated; escalate exhaustion or inability to feed.
  • Remember: Care is mainly supportive. Bronchodilators, steroids and antibiotics are not routine treatment for typical bronchiolitis.

Module 8

Tonsillitis and tonsillectomy

  • Recognize: Tonsillitis can impair swallowing and intake. After surgery, repeated swallowing, fresh blood or bloody emesis may indicate hemorrhage.
  • Act: Assess airway, bleeding, pain and hydration. Report postoperative fresh bleeding immediately and follow emergency instructions; provide prescribed analgesia and permitted fluids.
  • Remember: Expected throat pain is different from hemorrhage. Diet and activity follow the surgeon's plan; caregivers need clear bleeding return precautions.

Module 8

Laryngotracheobronchitis: croup

  • Recognize: A barking cough, hoarseness and inspiratory stridor suggest viral swelling below the vocal cords; stridor at rest indicates greater obstruction.
  • Act: Keep the child calm, assess breathing and oxygenation, and give prescribed corticosteroid; significant obstruction may require nebulized epinephrine and observation afterward.
  • Remember: Croup's barking cough differs from epiglottitis with drooling and toxic appearance. Cyanosis, exhaustion or reduced air entry requires emergency escalation.

Module 8

Viral and bacterial pharyngitis

  • Recognize: Sore throat with cough or runny nose favors a virus; fever and tender neck nodes can occur with group A strep.
  • Act: Assess swallowing, hydration and airway; obtain prescribed strep testing. Provide pain relief and fluids, with antibiotics for confirmed bacterial disease as directed.
  • Remember: Appearance alone cannot reliably confirm strep. Complete prescribed antibiotics; drooling, muffled voice or respiratory difficulty needs urgent evaluation.

Module 8

Pneumonia

  • Recognize: Fever, cough, tachypnea and crackles reflect inflamed alveoli; hypoxemia, severe retractions or exhaustion signals deterioration.
  • Act: Assess respiratory effort, oxygenation and hydration; give prescribed oxygen, fluids and cause-appropriate treatment. Monitor response and increasing oxygen needs.
  • Remember: Antibiotics treat bacterial pneumonia, not every viral respiratory illness. Escalate cyanosis, poor perfusion or altered alertness rather than focusing only on fever.

Module 8

Cystic fibrosis

  • Recognize: CFTR dysfunction causes thick secretions, recurrent respiratory infection, poor growth and greasy stools when pancreatic function is impaired.
  • Act: Support airway clearance, infection treatment and nutrition; assess growth, stools and respiratory status. Give prescribed pancreatic enzymes with meals and snacks.
  • Remember: Do not crush enzyme beads. Calorie/protein support and prescribed fat-soluble vitamins help growth; worsening breathing, hemoptysis or dehydration needs review.

Module 8

Influenza

  • Recognize: Abrupt fever, aches, fatigue and cough suggest influenza; respiratory distress or improvement followed by marked worsening raises concern for complications.
  • Act: Assess breathing, hydration and risk factors; use appropriate respiratory precautions and prescribed supportive or antiviral treatment. Early assessment matters for higher-risk children.
  • Remember: Annual vaccination reduces risk. Avoid aspirin during pediatric viral illness unless specifically directed for another condition; monitor intake and deterioration.

COVID-19

  • Recognize: Fever, cough, sore throat, fatigue or GI symptoms may occur; severity ranges from mild illness to respiratory compromise.
  • Act: Assess breathing, oxygenation and hydration; follow current testing and infection-control guidance. Give prescribed treatment based on risk and severity.
  • Remember: Breathing difficulty, chest pain, marked lethargy or dehydration needs urgent care. Persistent fever after infection warrants assessment; follow current prevention guidance.

Module 8

Allergic rhinitis

  • Recognize: Itchy nose or eyes, sneezing and clear drainage usually occur without fever; symptoms can interfere with sleep and feeding.
  • Act: Identify triggers, assess associated asthma symptoms, and use prescribed antihistamine or intranasal therapy. Teach nasal-spray technique and practical allergen reduction.
  • Remember: Allergic inflammation is different from bacterial infection; antibiotics are not the routine answer. Wheeze or breathing difficulty needs separate assessment.

Module 8

Otitis media

  • Recognize: Ear pain, fever or irritability after a respiratory infection suggests middle-ear inflammation; otoscopy establishes the findings.
  • Act: Prioritize pain control and assess feeding, hydration and hearing concerns. Observation versus antibiotics depends on age, severity and clinical evaluation.
  • Remember: Finish antibiotics when prescribed; reduce smoke exposure. Swelling behind the ear or a very ill child needs prompt review for complications.

Module 9

Cardiac catheterization

  • Recognize: Catheter insertion creates bleeding and distal-perfusion risks; baseline pulses and limb findings support comparison after the procedure.
  • Act: Monitor vital signs, insertion site, distal pulses, color, warmth and capillary refill. Keep the limb positioned and activity limited as ordered; report bleeding or a cool pulseless extremity.
  • Remember: Use the prescribed pressure and escalation protocol for bleeding. Assess contrast-related risks, hydration and urine output as ordered.

Module 9

Common congenital heart defects: flow patterns

  • Recognize: Defects may increase pulmonary flow, obstruct outflow or mix blood. Tachypnea, feeding fatigue, poor growth, cyanosis and poor perfusion are key clues.
  • Act: Trace where blood goes; assess oxygenation, pulses, feeding endurance and growth. Support the prescribed cardiac and nutrition plan and escalate deterioration.
  • Remember: Left-to-right shunts often cause pulmonary overcirculation; duct-dependent defects may require keeping the ductus open rather than closing it.

Module 9

Patent ductus arteriosus

  • Recognize: A persistent aorta-to-pulmonary artery connection usually produces left-to-right flow; a machinery-like murmur, feeding fatigue and poor growth may occur.
  • Act: Assess breathing, feeding, weight and perfusion; monitor prescribed closure treatment and fluid balance when closure is appropriate.
  • Remember: A symptomatic PDA may be closed, but a duct-dependent congenital defect may need prostaglandin to keep that same connection open.

Module 9

Atrial septal defect

  • Recognize: An opening between atria usually permits left-to-right flow; the child may be asymptomatic, with a murmur or fixed split second heart sound.
  • Act: Monitor growth, respiratory symptoms and activity tolerance; support echocardiography and cardiology follow-up. Significant defects may require catheter or surgical closure.
  • Remember: A small defect may be observed. Feeling well does not eliminate the need for follow-up; activity guidance is individualized.

Module 9

Ventricular septal defect

  • Recognize: An opening between ventricles commonly increases pulmonary blood flow; larger defects cause tachypnea, sweating with feeds and poor growth.
  • Act: Assess feeding endurance, weight, breathing and fluid balance. Support prescribed heart-failure therapy, nutrition and repair when indicated.
  • Remember: Small defects may close spontaneously; large shunts can cause heart failure. Escalate worsening feeding or breathing even when a murmur is already known.

Module 9

Coarctation of the aorta

  • Recognize: A narrowed aortic segment causes higher arm pressures, weak femoral pulses and cooler legs; critical newborns may develop shock.
  • Act: Compare upper- and lower-limb pulses and pressures; monitor urine, perfusion and feeding. Escalate poor systemic perfusion and prepare prescribed prostaglandin or repair.
  • Remember: The arm-leg difference is the key exam clue. Duct-dependent newborns need urgent support, and long-term BP/cardiology follow-up remains important after repair.

Module 9

Aortic stenosis

  • Recognize: Narrowed left-ventricular outflow increases workload; exertional chest pain or syncope is concerning, and critical infants may have shock.
  • Act: Assess pulses, BP, perfusion and exercise symptoms; promptly report syncope or poor perfusion. Follow individualized activity guidance and prepare for prescribed intervention.
  • Remember: Severity determines observation, catheter treatment or surgery. Critical neonatal disease may require prostaglandin; do not dismiss exertional fainting as ordinary fatigue.

Module 9

Pulmonary stenosis

  • Recognize: Narrowed right-ventricular outflow limits blood reaching the lungs; severe disease can cause cyanosis or exercise intolerance.
  • Act: Assess oxygenation, perfusion and activity tolerance; escalate severe neonatal cyanosis. Monitor prescribed therapy and circulation after intervention.
  • Remember: Balloon valvuloplasty or surgery may relieve obstruction. Critical newborns may need prostaglandin when pulmonary flow depends on the ductus.

Module 9

Tetralogy of Fallot

  • Recognize: PROVe: pulmonary outflow obstruction, right-ventricular hypertrophy, overriding aorta and VSD. A hypercyanotic spell causes abruptly worsening cyanosis.
  • Act: Calm the child, use knee-chest positioning, summon urgent help and give prescribed oxygen and therapy. Monitor saturation and perfusion.
  • Remember: Knee-chest positioning improves systemic resistance and helps reduce right-to-left shunting. A prolonged or worsening spell is an emergency; definitive treatment is surgical repair.

Module 9

Transposition of the great arteries

  • Recognize: The aorta leaves the right ventricle and pulmonary artery leaves the left, creating parallel circulations and severe neonatal cyanosis.
  • Act: Arrange emergency specialist care, monitor oxygenation/perfusion and prepare prescribed prostaglandin and mixing procedures such as atrial septostomy.
  • Remember: Survival before repair requires blood mixing through a connection between circuits. Arterial-switch surgery corrects the circulation; oxygen alone cannot correct the anatomy.

Module 9

Tricuspid atresia

  • Recognize: The tricuspid opening is absent, preventing direct right-atrium-to-right-ventricle flow; cyanosis depends on mixing and pulmonary blood supply.
  • Act: Monitor oxygenation, perfusion and feeding; escalate worsening cyanosis or lethargy. Give prescribed prostaglandin when flow is duct dependent and watch for apnea/hypotension.
  • Remember: Blood needs an alternate route between chambers. Management often uses staged surgical palliation with long-term congenital-cardiology follow-up.

Module 9

Kawasaki disease

  • Recognize: Persistent fever with red eyes, oral changes, rash, extremity changes or cervical lymphadenopathy suggests vasculitis; incomplete presentations occur.
  • Act: Arrange prompt evaluation, monitor cardiac status and hydration, and support echocardiography plus prescribed IVIG and aspirin under specialist direction.
  • Remember: Coronary artery injury is the major concern. Keep cardiac follow-up and check live-vaccine timing after IVIG; aspirin here is specialist-directed.

Module 9

Rheumatic fever

  • Recognize: An immune response after group A strep can cause migratory joint pain, carditis, chorea, rash or nodules.
  • Act: Assess cardiac symptoms, perfusion and joint pain; support prescribed strep eradication, anti-inflammatory treatment and activity limits.
  • Remember: Diagnosis uses the overall criteria and evidence of preceding infection. Ongoing antibiotic prophylaxis may prevent recurrence and further valve injury; adherence matters after symptoms improve.

Every exam: priority + dose checks
  • Clinical judgment: Recognize cues → analyze → prioritize → plan → act → evaluate. Compare with baseline; reassess the response.
  • Dose safety: Verify patient, allergies, drug, dose, route and time. Use kilograms for weight-based doses; check the safe range and maximum.
  • Calculate: Ordered dose ÷ available dose × volume. Example: 250 mg ÷ 125 mg × 5 mL = 10 mL. Clarify an unsafe or unclear order before giving it.

Rules to remember

  • Watch effort and alertness as well as breath sounds.
  • A sudden cyanotic spell in tetralogy of Fallot needs prompt support.

See this → think this

A child with stridor is drooling and leaning forward, and appears frightened.

Think…
  • Possible epiglottitis.
  • Agitation or a throat examination can worsen airway obstruction.

A child with tetralogy becomes intensely blue and distressed during crying.

Think…
  • Reduced pulmonary blood flow produces a hypercyanotic spell.

What do I do first?

Respiratory

  1. First action
    • Keep the child calm in the position of comfort and call the airway team.
    • Avoid examining the throat with a tongue depressor.
  2. Look for a response
    • Watch air movement, color, alertness and readiness for a controlled airway.

Cardiac

  1. First action
    • Calm the child, bring knees toward the chest and provide oxygen and emergency support per protocol.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess color, oxygenation and level of alertness.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • A peak-flow plan compares today's value with the child's personal best. The usual green zone is at least 80%, yellow 50–79% and red below 50%.
  • A quiet chest in a tiring child with severe asthma can signal very poor airflow. Reassess work of breathing and alertness immediately.

Practice and memory cues

Stridor points toward the upper airway. Wheeze often reflects narrowed lower airways. A blue spell in tetralogy of Fallot suggests reduced lung blood flow.

A 3-year-old arrives with a temperature of 39.5 C, drooling, a muffled voice, and is sitting forward on his hands refusing to lie back. He has no cough. Which action should the nurse take first?

  1. Inspect the throat with a tongue blade for exudate
  2. Obtain a throat culture
  3. Keep the child upright with the parent and ensure emergency airway equipment is at the bedside
  4. Position the child supine for a chest x-ray
Check the answer and why

Keep the child upright with the parent and ensure emergency airway equipment is at the bedside

High fever, drooling, muffled voice, tripod posture and no cough is epiglottitis — never use a tongue blade or inspect the throat, because it can trigger complete airway obstruction. Croup is the look-alike, but croup brings a barking cough with a low-grade fever.

An infant with tetralogy of Fallot has a hypercyanotic (tet) spell. What is the nurse's first action?

  1. Place the infant in a knee-chest position
  2. Lay the infant flat and stimulate vigorously
  3. Give a rapid IV fluid bolus first
  4. Withhold oxygen to avoid closing the duct
Check the answer and why

Place the infant in a knee-chest position

Knee-chest raises systemic vascular resistance, which pushes blood back through the pulmonary circulation instead of shunting right to left. Older children squat instinctively for the same reason. Then calm the child, give oxygen and morphine as prescribed.

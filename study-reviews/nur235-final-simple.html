Module 1 · Pediatric assessment, comfort and medication safety 6 short topics

Module 1

Medication administration and techniques

  • Recognize: Immature kidneys and liver change drug handling; the correct adult dose cannot simply be scaled down.
  • Act: Check allergies, weight in kilograms, safe dose, route and measuring device. Give oral medicines in a small approved volume, never a full bottle.
  • Remember: Offer a choice of how, not whether; never call medicine candy. Reassess effect and adverse reactions.

Module 1

Therapeutic communication

  • Recognize: Developmental ability determines how a child understands illness, time and instructions; caregiver anxiety also affects the child.
  • Act: Use short, honest explanations, medical play and limited real choices. Speak directly to the child and include the caregiver.
  • Remember: Demonstrate unfamiliar procedures; never promise that a painful procedure will not hurt. Adolescents need privacy and participation.

Diagnostic procedures and specimen collection

  • Recognize: Fear and movement can interfere with testing; contaminated specimens can produce misleading results.
  • Act: Explain at the child's level, use comfort positioning and collect the correct specimen with ordered technique. Label promptly and check preparation requirements.
  • Remember: Use least invasive steps first when appropriate. A urine collection bag carries contamination risk; a positive bag result needs proper confirmation.

Hospitalization and perception of illness

  • Recognize: Separation, unfamiliar routines and loss of control may cause crying, withdrawal or regression; reaction varies with developmental stage.
  • Act: Encourage caregiver presence, familiar comfort objects and home routines. Preserve age-appropriate independence and therapeutic play.

Pain assessment and management

  • Recognize: Use self-report when the child can reliably communicate; otherwise use a validated behavioral scale such as FLACC.
  • Act: Combine prescribed analgesia with comfort positioning, distraction and caregiver support. Reassess with the same pain tool after intervention.
  • Remember: FLACC scores face, legs, activity, cry and consolability. Lack of crying does not prove comfort; monitor sedation and breathing with opioids.

Weight-based dosage calculations

  • Recognize: A calculation may be mathematically correct yet unsafe if pounds, kilograms, daily dose and dose frequency are confused.
  • Act: Convert weight to kilograms; find the safe range, compare the order and calculate the volume from concentration. Clarify an unsafe order before giving it.
  • Remember: mg/kg/day is divided across scheduled doses; mg/kg/dose is per administration. Check units, maximum dose and decimal placement.

Module 2

Infant assessment: expected and unexpected findings

  • Recognize: Observe breathing, color, interaction, feeding and hydration before disturbing the infant; compare findings with age-specific expectations.
  • Act: Keep the caregiver close, use a warm environment, and auscultate while quiet. Trend weight, length and head circumference rather than judging one measurement.
  • Remember: Poor feeding, lethargy, respiratory distress or abnormal growth needs assessment; a newborn may show subtle illness signs.

Module 2

Infant development: Erikson and Piaget

  • Recognize: Erikson: trust versus mistrust. Piaget: sensorimotor learning through movement and the senses; object permanence develops during infancy.
  • Act: Respond consistently to hunger and comfort cues. Provide safe sensory exploration, caregiver presence and simple play such as peekaboo.
  • Remember: Consistent care builds trust; separation or stranger anxiety reflects development. Match teaching to what the infant can experience, not verbal reasoning.

Infant milestones and growth

  • Recognize: Growth is rapid; birth weight generally doubles around 4–6 months and triples by one year. Motor control develops from head downward.
  • Act: Trend growth and motor, language and social progress. Ask caregivers about new skills and any lost skills; arrange follow-up for concerning delays.

Toddler and preschool assessment

  • Recognize: Stranger fear, resistance and short attention span can limit assessment; expected findings differ from adult norms.
  • Act: Let the child stay near the caregiver, demonstrate on a doll, and use least threatening steps first. Offer two acceptable choices and save uncomfortable procedures for last when possible.
  • Remember: Observe play and behavior as assessment data. Approach according to developmental ability, not age alone.

Toddler and preschool development: Erikson and Piaget

  • Act: Give limited real choices, maintain routines and use simple concrete explanations or medical play. Correct the belief that thoughts or misbehavior caused illness.

School-age and adolescent assessment

Module 5

Blood transfusions

  • Recognize: Fever, chills, back pain, rash, dyspnea or hypotension during a transfusion may signal a reaction.
  • Act: Stop the transfusion, assess airway and circulation, maintain IV access with saline and new tubing per protocol, and notify the provider/blood bank.
  • Remember: Verify identity and product before starting; monitor closely. Do not restart suspected reaction blood. Hemolysis and allergy are different reaction mechanisms.

Module 5

Epistaxis

  • Recognize: Nosebleeds often follow irritated nasal mucosa, dry air, trauma or nose picking; recurrent bleeding can reflect a clotting disorder.
  • Act: Sit upright, lean forward and apply continuous pressure to the soft nose as instructed. Assess blood loss and persistent bleeding.
  • Remember: Do not tilt the head back: swallowing blood hides the loss and may cause vomiting. Persistent or severe bleeding needs further evaluation.

Sickle cell anemia and crisis

  • Recognize: Sickled cells obstruct flow and hemolyze: pain, pallor and jaundice occur. Fever, chest symptoms or neurologic changes are urgent.
  • Act: Assess oxygenation and perfusion; provide rapid prescribed analgesia and appropriate hydration. Escalate fever or suspected acute chest syndrome immediately.
  • Remember: Avoid overhydration. Give oxygen for hypoxemia; an uncomplicated pain crisis does not routinely require transfusion. Teach trigger prevention and infection precautions.

Iron deficiency anemia

  • Recognize: Pallor, fatigue, irritability and pica suggest reduced hemoglobin; premature infants and toddlers with excessive milk intake are vulnerable.
  • Act: Assess diet and CBC/iron studies; give prescribed iron and iron-rich foods. Teach safe storage because iron overdose is dangerous.
  • Remember: Milk can displace iron-rich meals. Liquid iron may stain teeth; use the taught technique. Dark stools can occur; adherence and follow-up confirm response.

Hemophilia

  • Recognize: Factor VIII or IX deficiency causes prolonged bleeding and deep joint or muscle bleeding; a painful swollen joint suggests hemarthrosis.
  • Act: Assess bleeding and neurovascular status; give prescribed clotting-factor replacement promptly. Protect injured joints and seek urgent care for head injury or internal bleeding signs.
  • Remember: Avoid unnecessary invasive procedures and medicines that worsen bleeding. Do not assume a small external wound excludes serious internal bleeding.

Leukemia

  • Recognize: Malignant immature cells crowd normal marrow: anemia causes fatigue, thrombocytopenia causes bruising and neutropenia increases infection risk.

  • Remember: A high total WBC does not mean effective immune protection. Treatment also risks tumor lysis, so monitor ordered electrolytes, urine output and hydration.

Allergic response

  • Recognize: Anaphylaxis can rapidly cause airway swelling, wheezing, vomiting or poor circulation after exposure; skin findings may be absent.
  • Act: Recognize the emergency, summon help and give prescribed IM epinephrine promptly in the anterolateral thigh. Support airway, oxygenation and circulation; monitor response.
  • Remember: Antihistamines do not replace epinephrine. Teach avoidance, the emergency plan and correct auto-injector use.

HIV

  • Act: Use gentle cleansing and prescribed topical or oral antibiotics. Encourage hand hygiene, short nails and separate personal items.
  • Remember: Limit scratching and follow school-return guidance. Watch for worsening redness, fever or deeper infection; the honey crust is a recognition clue, not a reason to skip assessment.

  • Act: Assess hydration, swallowing and illness severity; obtain prescribed testing and administer the full prescribed antibiotic course. Use appropriate infection precautions.

  • Act: Use the prescribed or recommended pediculicide correctly, remove nits as directed and assess close contacts. Clean relevant clothing and bedding according to instructions.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • For weight-based medication calculations use kilograms. Convert pounds to kilograms by dividing by 2.2; check the prescribed dose against the safe range and maximum.
  • Follow urine output, daily weight, blood pressure and edema together when kidney function changes.

Practice and memory cues

Distinguish nephrotic protein loss from nephritic inflammation. Use the earlier exams to rehearse airway priorities and age-appropriate assessment.

A child with nephrotic syndrome is admitted. Which findings does the nurse expect? Select all that apply.

  1. Massive proteinuria
  2. Generalized edema including periorbital swelling
  3. Hypoalbuminemia
  4. Grossly bloody urine
  5. Hyperlipidemia
Check the answer and why

Massive proteinuria Generalized edema including periorbital swelling Hypoalbuminemia Hyperlipidemia

Nephrotic syndrome leaks protein : heavy proteinuria, low albumin, edema as oncotic pressure falls, and a compensatory rise in lipids. Gross hematuria points to glomerulonephritis instead - that is the pair these questions separate. Periorbital swelling on waking is often the first thing a parent notices.

A child is admitted with a suspected Wilms tumor. Which nursing action is essential?

  1. Palpate the abdominal mass each shift to track its size
  2. Post a sign at the bedside not to palpate the abdomen
  3. Encourage vigorous activity
  4. Place the child prone for comfort
Check the answer and why

Post a sign at the bedside not to palpate the abdomen

Do not palpate the abdomen. Handling a Wilms tumor can rupture the capsule and seed malignant cells across the abdomen. A sign goes above the bed so no one does it out of habit - one of the few nursing interventions that is entirely about not doing something.

An infant with tetralogy of Fallot has a hypercyanotic (tet) spell. What is the nurse's first action?

Check the answer and why

Knee-chest raises systemic vascular resistance, which pushes blood back through the pulmonary circulation instead of shunting right to left. Older children squat instinctively for the same reason. Then calm the child, give oxygen and morphine as prescribed.

Airway and breathing outrank everything, and accessory muscle use at rest is active respiratory distress . The croup child is calm, post-op pain of 5 is expected, and a fontanel that bulges only with crying is normal — bulging at rest is what suggests raised ICP.

Rules to remember

  • Distinguish protein loss from glomerular inflammation.
  • Choose a pain tool the child can use.
  • Adjust infant care to development and sleep safety.
  • Offer a toddler two acceptable choices.
  • Match explanations to the child's stage of thinking.
  • Recognize fever as urgent during cancer treatment.
  • An airway or circulation change makes an allergic reaction an emergency.
  • Protect the child and time the seizure.
  • Watch effort and alertness as well as breath sounds.
  • A sudden cyanotic spell in tetralogy of Fallot needs prompt support.
  • Pain that escalates under a cast is an urgent cue.
  • Deep, rapid breathing with vomiting in diabetes suggests acidosis.
  • Think obstruction when abdominal pain comes in waves.

A child has puffy eyelids, generalized edema and heavy protein in the urine.

Think…
  • Nephrotic syndrome causes urinary protein loss and edema.
  • Tea-colored urine and hypertension suggest a nephritic pattern.

A preverbal child grimaces, draws up the legs and is difficult to console.

Think…
  • FLACC uses observable behaviors when reliable self-report is unavailable.

A caregiver asks how to position a healthy infant for sleep.

Think…
  • Back sleeping on a firm, flat, separate sleep surface lowers sleep-related risk.

A hospitalized toddler says no to every part of the assessment.

Think…
  • Seeking control is expected.
  • Parallel play means playing beside another child.

A school-age child wants to know exactly how a dressing change will work.

Think…
  • Concrete explanations, demonstration and participation support mastery.

A child on chemotherapy has a new fever even though other symptoms are mild.

Think…
  • Neutropenia can mask signs of serious infection.

Hives appear with wheezing and faintness soon after a known allergen.

Think…
  • Anaphylaxis can narrow the airway and cause circulatory collapse.

A child suddenly stiffens and has rhythmic jerking movements.

Think…
  • During a seizure the immediate risks include injury and impaired breathing.

A child with stridor is drooling and leaning forward, and appears frightened.

Think…
  • Possible epiglottitis.
  • Agitation or a throat examination can worsen airway obstruction.

A child with tetralogy becomes intensely blue and distressed during crying.

Think…
  • Reduced pulmonary blood flow produces a hypercyanotic spell.

A child reports worsening pain despite analgesia, especially with passive finger movement.

Think…
  • Pressure inside a muscle compartment can compromise circulation before pulses disappear.

A child with type 1 diabetes has vomiting, dehydration and deep breathing.

Think…
  • Consider diabetic ketoacidosis.
  • The breathing pattern helps compensate for metabolic acidosis.

A toddler has sudden spells of pain, pulls up the knees, then seems better between episodes.

Think…
  • Intussusception is one segment of bowel sliding into another.
  • Bloody mucus can occur late.

What do I do first?

Renal & Genitourinary

  1. First action
    • Assess blood pressure, urine output, daily weight and edema.
  2. Look for a response
    • Compare weight, fluid balance and blood pressure with baseline.

Intro to Pediatric Nursing

  1. First action
    • Score face, legs, activity, cry and consolability.
    • Provide appropriate pain relief.
  2. Look for a response
    • Repeat the same scale after the intervention and compare with caregiver observations.

The Infant (0–1 year)

  1. First action
    • Teach safe sleep using an empty crib and ask the caregiver to demonstrate.
  2. Look for a response
    • The caregiver shows the sleep setup without loose blankets, pillows or toys.

Toddler & Preschooler

  1. First action
    • Offer a small choice such as which arm to check and keep the caregiver nearby.
  2. Look for a response
    • The child participates with less distress and the needed assessment is completed.

School-Age & Adolescent

  1. First action
    • Show the supplies, explain the steps and give the child a manageable job.
  2. Look for a response
    • The child can describe the sequence and identify a coping strategy.

Hematology & Childhood Neoplasms

  1. First action
    • Notify the oncology team immediately and follow the febrile-neutropenia pathway.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess perfusion, mental status, temperature and response to timely treatment.

Immune, Infectious Disease, Skin & Burns

  1. First action
    • Call for help and give intramuscular epinephrine per the emergency protocol.
    • Support breathing and circulation.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess airway, respiratory effort, perfusion and recurrence of symptoms.

Neurologic & Neuromuscular

  1. First action
    • Protect the head, clear hazards, time the seizure and turn to the side when safe.
    • Keep objects out of the mouth.
  2. Look for a response
    • Afterward assess breathing, responsiveness and return toward baseline.

Respiratory

  1. First action
    • Keep the child calm in the position of comfort and call the airway team.
    • Avoid examining the throat with a tongue depressor.
  2. Look for a response
    • Watch air movement, color, alertness and readiness for a controlled airway.

Cardiac

  1. First action
    • Calm the child, bring knees toward the chest and provide oxygen and emergency support per protocol.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess color, oxygenation and level of alertness.

Musculoskeletal

  1. First action
    • Perform a focused neurovascular check and urgently notify the team.
    • Assess color, warmth, movement and sensation.
  2. Look for a response
    • Track changes in pain, sensation and perfusion while urgent treatment is arranged.

Endocrine & Metabolic

  1. First action
    • Check glucose and ketones, assess circulation and initiate the pediatric DKA pathway with the team.
  2. Look for a response
    • Follow neurologic status, hydration, glucose and electrolytes throughout treatment.

Gastrointestinal

  1. First action
    • Keep the child NPO, assess perfusion and obtain urgent evaluation.
    • Prepare for ultrasound and ordered treatment.
  2. Look for a response
    • Monitor pain, perfusion and abdominal findings, including recurrence after treatment.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • For weight-based medication calculations use kilograms. Convert pounds to kilograms by dividing by 2.2; check the prescribed dose against the safe range and maximum.
  • Follow urine output, daily weight, blood pressure and edema together when kidney function changes.

Practice and memory cues

Distinguish nephrotic protein loss from nephritic inflammation. Use the earlier exams to rehearse airway priorities and age-appropriate assessment.

A child with nephrotic syndrome is admitted. Which findings does the nurse expect? Select all that apply.

  1. Massive proteinuria
  2. Generalized edema including periorbital swelling
  3. Hypoalbuminemia
  4. Grossly bloody urine
  5. Hyperlipidemia
Check the answer and why

Massive proteinuria Generalized edema including periorbital swelling Hypoalbuminemia Hyperlipidemia

Nephrotic syndrome leaks protein : heavy proteinuria, low albumin, edema as oncotic pressure falls, and a compensatory rise in lipids. Gross hematuria points to glomerulonephritis instead - that is the pair these questions separate. Periorbital swelling on waking is often the first thing a parent notices.

A child is admitted with a suspected Wilms tumor. Which nursing action is essential?

  1. Palpate the abdominal mass each shift to track its size
  2. Post a sign at the bedside not to palpate the abdomen
  3. Encourage vigorous activity
  4. Place the child prone for comfort
Check the answer and why

Post a sign at the bedside not to palpate the abdomen

Do not palpate the abdomen. Handling a Wilms tumor can rupture the capsule and seed malignant cells across the abdomen. A sign goes above the bed so no one does it out of habit - one of the few nursing interventions that is entirely about not doing something.

An infant with tetralogy of Fallot has a hypercyanotic (tet) spell. What is the nurse's first action?

  1. Place the infant in a knee-chest position
  2. Lay the infant flat and stimulate vigorously
  3. Give a rapid IV fluid bolus first
  4. Withhold oxygen to avoid closing the duct
Check the answer and why

Place the infant in a knee-chest position

Knee-chest raises systemic vascular resistance, which pushes blood back through the pulmonary circulation instead of shunting right to left. Older children squat instinctively for the same reason. Then calm the child, give oxygen and morphine as prescribed.

A nurse receives report on four children. Which child should the nurse assess first?

  1. A 3-year-old with croup who has a barking cough and is resting calmly on a parent's lap
  2. A 6-year-old with asthma who has audible wheezing and accessory muscle use at rest
  3. A 10-year-old who is 1 day post appendectomy and rates incisional pain 5 of 10
  4. A 4-month-old whose anterior fontanel bulges while she is crying hard
Check the answer and why

A 6-year-old with asthma who has audible wheezing and accessory muscle use at rest

Airway and breathing outrank everything, and accessory muscle use at rest is active respiratory distress . The croup child is calm, post-op pain of 5 is expected, and a fontanel that bulges only with crying is normal — bulging at rest is what suggests raised ICP.

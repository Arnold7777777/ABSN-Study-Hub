Module 1

Eye and ear tests

  • Recognize: Eyes: acuity/fields, tonometry and retinal examination identify different problems. Ears: otoscopy, audiometry and Weber/Rinne distinguish obstruction, conduction and nerve loss.
  • Act: Explain the test, compare both sides and report sudden vision or hearing changes promptly.
  • Remember: Match the test to function: pressure is not acuity; air/bone conduction helps classify hearing loss.

Impaired vision

Glaucoma

  • Recognize: Open-angle disease gradually damages peripheral vision. Acute angle closure causes severe eye pain, halos, blurred vision and nausea.
  • Act: Treat sudden painful visual changes as an emergency; reinforce prescribed pressure-lowering drops and follow-up.
  • Remember: Glaucoma damages the optic nerve; established loss is irreversible. Chronic disease can be silent before substantial loss occurs.

Cataracts

Immune assessment and CBC

  • Recognize: Age, nutrition, chronic illness, medications and exposures affect immune defense. Leukopenia is low WBC; leukocytosis is high WBC; neutropenia specifically reduces neutrophils.
  • Act: Interpret the differential/ANC and clinical trend, assess infection sites and urgently report fever with impaired defenses.
  • Remember: A high WBC alone does not establish bacterial infection; a low neutrophil count can blunt expected inflammatory findings.

Module 3

Four hypersensitivity mechanisms

  • Recognize: Type I is immediate IgE-mediated allergy; type II targets cells; type III involves immune complexes; type IV is delayed T-cell activity.
  • Act: Identify the trigger and severity; rapidly treat airway or circulatory compromise rather than waiting to classify the mechanism.
  • Remember: ACID: Anaphylactic, Cytotoxic, Immune complex, Delayed. Timing and organ involvement distinguish mechanisms; not every rash is anaphylaxis.

Anaphylaxis

  • Recognize: Sudden airway swelling, wheeze, hypotension or multisystem symptoms after exposure indicate a potentially fatal reaction.

Module 4

Burn mechanisms and deep injury

  • Recognize: Thermal injury damages tissue; electrical injury can hide deep muscle damage, dysrhythmia and myoglobin-related kidney injury. Chemicals continue damaging tissue while present.
  • Act: Ensure scene safety, remove the exposure appropriately and monitor ECG, urine and limb perfusion. Deep compartment pressure may require fasciotomy.
  • Remember: Visible skin injury can underestimate electrical damage. Fasciotomy releases fascia; escharotomy releases constricting burn eschar.

Module 4

Emergent burn resuscitation

  • Recognize: Capillary leak shifts fluid into tissues, causing edema and intravascular depletion; severe burns also affect perfusion and GI function.
  • Act: Prioritize airway, ordered fluids and hourly perfusion/urine reassessment; consider escharotomy for constricting circumferential burns.
  • Remember: Course Parkland: 4 mL × kg × %TBSA over 24 hours, half within 8 hours of injury, not arrival. Actual resuscitation follows current orders and response rather than formula alone.

Burn depth, appearance and severity

  • Recognize: Superficial burns are red and painful; partial-thickness burns may blister; full-thickness burns can appear leathery and have reduced sensation. Extent, depth, location and inhalation injury determine severity.
  • Act: Assess cleaned wound depth, perfusion and sensation, prevent hypothermia and consider critical location, age and inhalation injury when judging severity.

Module 5

Addison disease and adrenal crisis

  • Recognize: Low cortisol causes weakness, hypotension and hypoglycemia; primary adrenal failure also loses aldosterone, causing sodium loss and potassium retention. Crisis can progress to shock.
  • Act: Urgently support circulation with prescribed fluids and IV glucocorticoids; monitor glucose, electrolytes, ECG and urine output.
  • Remember: Primary disease can cause hyperpigmentation. Teach lifelong replacement, the prescribed stress-dose plan and never abruptly stopping chronic steroids.

Cushing syndrome and disease

  • Recognize: Excess cortisol causes central weight gain, thin skin/bruising, muscle weakness, hyperglycemia and infection risk.
  • Act: Monitor glucose, blood pressure, skin and infection; protect fragile tissues and follow the steroid taper or endocrine treatment plan.

Module 13

Four shock mechanisms

  • Recognize: Hypovolemic loses volume; cardiogenic loses pump function; distributive loses vascular tone; obstructive blocks blood flow. Risks include bleeding, infarction, infection/anaphylaxis and mechanical obstruction.
  • Act: Recognize poor perfusion and rapidly identify the mechanism while supporting ABCs.
  • Remember: A low blood pressure is not the only clue: altered mentation, reduced urine and abnormal perfusion can appear earlier. Different mechanisms need different fluid and definitive treatment plans.

Module 13

Cardiogenic shock

  • Recognize: Pump failure produces low output, often with pulmonary congestion; myocardial infarction is an important trigger.
  • Act: Support oxygenation, ECG/hemodynamic monitoring and urgent cause-directed therapy; give ordered vasoactive or mechanical support.
  • Remember: The tank may already be full. Large indiscriminate fluid boluses can worsen congestion; treatment follows volume assessment and the failing pump's cause.

Module 13

Septic and anaphylactic shock

  • Recognize: Distributive shock involves vasodilation and maldistributed flow. Sepsis adds infection-related organ dysfunction; anaphylaxis can add airway swelling/bronchospasm.
  • Act: Sepsis: urgent assessment, prescribed cultures/antibiotics, fluids and vasopressors. Anaphylaxis: prompt IM epinephrine, emergency response and airway/circulation support.
  • Remember: Early septic shock can have warm skin. Similar hypotension does not make these conditions interchangeable; treat the underlying mechanism immediately.

Module 13

Hypovolemic shock

  • Recognize: Blood or fluid loss lowers preload and output, causing tachycardia, cool skin, reduced urine and worsening mental status.
  • Act: Control the source of loss and administer prescribed volume replacement; use blood products for significant hemorrhage as indicated.
  • Remember: Trend perfusion rather than relying on one BP. Replacing volume without stopping ongoing bleeding or fluid loss does not resolve the cause.

Obstructive shock

  • Recognize: Mechanical blockage prevents filling or forward flow; examples include pulmonary embolism, cardiac tamponade and tension pneumothorax.
  • Act: Support ABCs and arrange urgent cause-specific relief through the emergency team.
  • Remember: The pump may be intrinsically functional but cannot move blood past the obstruction. Fluids or pressors alone cannot definitively fix every mechanical cause.

Module 13

Stages and progression of shock

  • Recognize: Inadequate cellular oxygen delivery leads to anaerobic metabolism and organ dysfunction. Compensation can preserve BP initially; progressive shock causes hypotension, oliguria and worsening mental status; refractory shock has severe irreversible injury.
  • Act: Track perfusion, lactate trends, urine output and response while treating the cause.
  • Remember: Do not wait for hypotension to recognize shock. Stages describe a continuum rather than a reason to postpone intervention.

Shock diagnostics and treatment

  • Recognize: Examination, ECG, lactate, blood gas, CBC/chemistry, cultures or imaging help identify impaired perfusion and its cause.
  • Act: Monitor response to prescribed fluids, oxygen/ventilation and cause-directed treatment; titrate vasoactive/inotropic therapy to ordered targets with close perfusion and access-site checks.

Rules to remember

  • Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion, even before hypotension is obvious.
  • A new curtain across vision needs urgent eye evaluation.
  • Match precautions to how the organism spreads.
  • Treat breathing or circulation problems after allergen exposure immediately.
  • Suspected inhalation injury can progress before visible swelling appears.
  • Match fluid output with the sodium trend.
  • Check whether the patient can swallow before treating hypoglycemia.
  • Sudden focal changes start the stroke clock.
  • Hypertension and headache after a high spinal cord injury are urgent.
  • Stop a transfusion when new symptoms suggest a reaction.
  • Let the patient's goal guide comfort care.
  • After prostate surgery, watch catheter drainage and bladder distention together.
  • Mass casualty triage priorities change with resources and reassessment.

What do I do first?

Shock & MODS

  1. First action
    • Call for help, assess airway and circulation, address the cause and initiate ordered resuscitation.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess mentation, perfusion, pressure, urine output and response to treatment.

Sensory Disorders — Eye & Ear

  1. First action
    • Arrange urgent ophthalmologic assessment and clarify onset and vision changes.
  2. Look for a response
    • Document baseline vision and ensure prompt specialist evaluation.

Infectious Diseases & HIV

  1. First action
    • Use the designated airborne infection isolation room and appropriate respirator.
    • Mask the patient for necessary transport.
  2. Look for a response
    • Verify precautions are maintained while the diagnostic workup proceeds.

Allergic, Inflammatory & Immunologic Disorders

  1. First action
    • Stop the suspected trigger, call for emergency help and give IM epinephrine per protocol.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess breathing and blood pressure promptly and monitor for recurrent symptoms.

Burns

  1. First action
    • Get urgent airway support, administer high-concentration oxygen per protocol and prepare for airway management.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess voice, air movement, work of breathing and perfusion.

Endocrine Disorders

  1. First action
    • Measure output, assess volume status and report findings for ordered fluid and hormone therapy.
  2. Look for a response
    • Trend intake, output, sodium and neurologic status.

Diabetes

  1. First action
    • Check glucose.
    • If below 70 mg/dL, give fast-acting carbohydrate per protocol.
    • If unable to swallow, use protocol-directed IV dextrose or glucagon.
  2. Look for a response
    • Recheck glucose after 15 minutes for oral treatment and repeat per protocol until corrected.

Neurologic Dysfunction & Cerebrovascular Disorders

  1. First action
    • Activate the stroke pathway, establish last known well and check glucose.
    • Prepare for urgent brain imaging.
  2. Look for a response
    • Repeat neurologic assessments and keep the patient NPO until swallowing safety is assessed.

Traumatic, Infectious, Oncologic & Degenerative Neuro

  1. First action
    • Sit upright, loosen constrictive items, recheck BP frequently and inspect the catheter or bladder first.
    • Follow the emergency protocol.
  2. Look for a response
    • Confirm blood pressure moves back toward the patient's baseline as the trigger is relieved.

Hematologic Disorders

  1. First action
    • Stop the transfusion, assess ABCs, maintain IV access with normal saline using new tubing per policy and notify the team and blood bank.
  2. Look for a response
    • Trend vital signs, oxygenation and urine output, and complete the ordered workup.

Oncologic Disorders & End-of-Life Care

  1. First action
    • Assess the symptom and preferences, adjust positioning and give prescribed comfort medication as indicated.
  2. Look for a response
    • Ask whether breathing feels easier and whether the plan still fits the patient's wishes.

Reproductive Disorders

  1. First action
    • Check for kinks and dependent loops, assess the bladder and follow the prescribed catheter protocol.
    • Escalate persistent obstruction.
  2. Look for a response
    • Confirm free drainage and subtract irrigant infused from total output when calculating urine output.

Disaster, Mass Casualty & Emergency Nursing

  1. First action
    • Ensure scene safety and apply the assigned triage system.
    • Reassess because categories can change.
  2. Look for a response
    • Check whether the next intervention or transfer still matches each person's condition.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • For adults with septic shock on vasopressors, an initial MAP target of 65 mmHg is common. Assess mentation, urine output, skin perfusion and trends alongside pressure.
  • Match the mechanism: volume loss empties the tank, cardiogenic shock weakens the pump, distributive shock changes vascular tone and obstructive shock blocks forward flow.

Practice and memory cues

Compare response with the patient's own baseline. A transiently improved pressure alone does not prove adequate organ perfusion.

A foot blister is painless in a person with neuropathy. What does that imply?

  1. It still needs prompt attention
  2. It is safe to ignore
  3. A heating pad will speed healing
Check the answer and why

It still needs prompt attention

Reduced sensation can hide injury. Prompt assessment helps prevent worsening wounds.

A client with a T4 spinal cord injury has BP 208/112, HR 48, a pounding headache and flushing above the injury. What does the nurse do first?

  1. Administer the prescribed antihypertensive
  2. Sit the client upright
  3. Check for a fecal impaction
  4. Lay the client flat and elevate the legs
Check the answer and why

Sit the client upright

In autonomic dysreflexia the first action is to sit her up , which uses gravity to drop the blood pressure immediately, then find and remove the trigger - check the bladder before the bowel. Antihypertensives are an adjunct, not the first move, and lying flat worsens the hypertension.

Fifteen minutes into a transfusion a client has chills, fever and flank pain. What does the nurse do first?

  1. Notify the provider
  2. Stop the transfusion
  3. Slow the rate and reassess in 15 minutes
  4. Administer the prescribed antipyretic
Check the answer and why

Stop the transfusion

For any suspected reaction the sequence is stop the transfusion, keep the line open with 0.9 percent NS on new tubing, stay with the client, then notify . Slowing the rate keeps incompatible blood going in, and notifying first delays the one action that stops the exposure.

A drip is ordered at 5 mcg/kg/min for a client weighing 154 lb. The bag contains 400 mg in 250 mL. What rate does the nurse program?

  1. 8.2 mL/hr
  2. 13.1 mL/hr
  3. 21 mL/hr
  4. 26.3 mL/hr
Check the answer and why

13.1 mL/hr

154 divided by 2.2 = 70 kg; 70 x 5 = 350 mcg/min = 21,000 mcg/hr = 21 mg/hr; the bag is 1.6 mg/mL, so 21 divided by 1.6 = 13.1 mL/hr . Answering 21 means stopping at mg/hr and never converting to volume - round only at the very end.

Rules to remember

  • Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion, even before hypotension is obvious.
  • A new curtain across vision needs urgent eye evaluation.
  • Match precautions to how the organism spreads.
  • Treat breathing or circulation problems after allergen exposure immediately.
  • Suspected inhalation injury can progress before visible swelling appears.
  • Match fluid output with the sodium trend.
  • Check whether the patient can swallow before treating hypoglycemia.
  • Sudden focal changes start the stroke clock.
  • Hypertension and headache after a high spinal cord injury are urgent.
  • Stop a transfusion when new symptoms suggest a reaction.
  • Let the patient's goal guide comfort care.
  • After prostate surgery, watch catheter drainage and bladder distention together.
  • Mass casualty triage priorities change with resources and reassessment.

A patient becomes confused with cool skin, tachycardia and falling urine output after fluid loss.

Think…
  • Hypovolemia reduces circulating volume.
  • Other shock types can impair the pump, vascular tone or blood flow.

A patient reports flashes, new floaters and a painless dark curtain in one eye.

Think…
  • Think retinal detachment.
  • Delay can threaten vision.

A patient with a persistent cough, weight loss and night sweats is being evaluated for pulmonary TB.

Think…
  • Suspected pulmonary tuberculosis requires airborne precautions.

A patient develops wheezing, throat tightness and hypotension after an infusion.

Think…
  • Anaphylaxis is a systemic reaction that can rapidly threaten the airway and circulation.

After an enclosed-space fire, a patient has hoarseness, soot around the mouth and increasing work of breathing.

Think…
  • Think inhalation injury.
  • Airway edema can progress quickly.

After pituitary surgery a patient has very large amounts of dilute urine, marked thirst and high sodium.

Think…
  • Diabetes insipidus causes loss of free water.
  • SIADH usually causes water retention and low sodium.

An insulin-treated patient is shaky and sweaty but awake and able to swallow safely.

Think…
  • Low glucose can impair brain function.
  • Oral carbohydrate is appropriate only when swallowing is safe.

A patient suddenly has facial droop, arm weakness and difficulty speaking.

Think…
  • Suspect acute stroke.
  • Symptoms alone cannot tell ischemic from hemorrhagic stroke.

A person with a T4 injury has a pounding headache, sweating above the lesion and a sudden rise in blood pressure.

Think…
  • Think autonomic dysreflexia.
  • Bladder distention is a common trigger.

During transfusion the patient develops chills, dyspnea or back pain.

Think…
  • A transfusion reaction can worsen while more product enters the circulation.

A patient with advanced cancer reports breathlessness and wants relief while family is present.

Think…
  • Palliative care treats distress and can accompany disease-directed treatment.

While continuous bladder irrigation is running, drainage falls and the patient has bladder pressure.

Think…
  • Tubing obstruction or clots can prevent outflow and distend the bladder.

Several patients arrive at once. One can walk and another has serious breathing or perfusion compromise.

Think…
  • Use the designated disaster triage algorithm.
  • In adult START, red means immediate and green means minor.

What do I do first?

Shock & MODS

  1. First action
    • Call for help, assess airway and circulation, address the cause and initiate ordered resuscitation.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess mentation, perfusion, pressure, urine output and response to treatment.

Sensory Disorders — Eye & Ear

  1. First action
    • Arrange urgent ophthalmologic assessment and clarify onset and vision changes.
  2. Look for a response
    • Document baseline vision and ensure prompt specialist evaluation.

Infectious Diseases & HIV

  1. First action
    • Use the designated airborne infection isolation room and appropriate respirator.
    • Mask the patient for necessary transport.
  2. Look for a response
    • Verify precautions are maintained while the diagnostic workup proceeds.

Allergic, Inflammatory & Immunologic Disorders

  1. First action
    • Stop the suspected trigger, call for emergency help and give IM epinephrine per protocol.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess breathing and blood pressure promptly and monitor for recurrent symptoms.

Burns

  1. First action
    • Get urgent airway support, administer high-concentration oxygen per protocol and prepare for airway management.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess voice, air movement, work of breathing and perfusion.

Endocrine Disorders

  1. First action
    • Measure output, assess volume status and report findings for ordered fluid and hormone therapy.
  2. Look for a response
    • Trend intake, output, sodium and neurologic status.

Diabetes

  1. First action
    • Check glucose.
    • If below 70 mg/dL, give fast-acting carbohydrate per protocol.
    • If unable to swallow, use protocol-directed IV dextrose or glucagon.
  2. Look for a response
    • Recheck glucose after 15 minutes for oral treatment and repeat per protocol until corrected.

Neurologic Dysfunction & Cerebrovascular Disorders

  1. First action
    • Activate the stroke pathway, establish last known well and check glucose.
    • Prepare for urgent brain imaging.
  2. Look for a response
    • Repeat neurologic assessments and keep the patient NPO until swallowing safety is assessed.

Traumatic, Infectious, Oncologic & Degenerative Neuro

  1. First action
    • Sit upright, loosen constrictive items, recheck BP frequently and inspect the catheter or bladder first.
    • Follow the emergency protocol.
  2. Look for a response
    • Confirm blood pressure moves back toward the patient's baseline as the trigger is relieved.

Hematologic Disorders

  1. First action
    • Stop the transfusion, assess ABCs, maintain IV access with normal saline using new tubing per policy and notify the team and blood bank.
  2. Look for a response
    • Trend vital signs, oxygenation and urine output, and complete the ordered workup.

Oncologic Disorders & End-of-Life Care

  1. First action
    • Assess the symptom and preferences, adjust positioning and give prescribed comfort medication as indicated.
  2. Look for a response
    • Ask whether breathing feels easier and whether the plan still fits the patient's wishes.

Reproductive Disorders

  1. First action
    • Check for kinks and dependent loops, assess the bladder and follow the prescribed catheter protocol.
    • Escalate persistent obstruction.
  2. Look for a response
    • Confirm free drainage and subtract irrigant infused from total output when calculating urine output.

Disaster, Mass Casualty & Emergency Nursing

  1. First action
    • Ensure scene safety and apply the assigned triage system.
    • Reassess because categories can change.
  2. Look for a response
    • Check whether the next intervention or transfer still matches each person's condition.

Compare the neurologic cues

Fluctuating double vision, drooping lids or weakness that worsens with use
  • Think myasthenia gravis.
  • A weak cough, difficulty swallowing or shallow breathing calls for urgent respiratory assessment.
Episodes of neurologic deficits separated in time
  • Consider multiple sclerosis.
  • Compare function with baseline and review infection or overheating when symptoms worsen.
Slowness, rigidity and a resting tremor
  • Think Parkinson disease.
  • Assess fall and swallowing risks and keep prescribed medications on schedule.
New confusion, pupillary change or a falling Glasgow Coma Scale score after head trauma
  • Escalate immediately, support airway and oxygenation, and protect spinal alignment.
  • Reassess the individual eye, verbal and motor responses.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • For adults with septic shock on vasopressors, an initial MAP target of 65 mmHg is common. Assess mentation, urine output, skin perfusion and trends alongside pressure.
  • Match the mechanism: volume loss empties the tank, cardiogenic shock weakens the pump, distributive shock changes vascular tone and obstructive shock blocks forward flow.

Practice and memory cues

Compare response with the patient's own baseline. A transiently improved pressure alone does not prove adequate organ perfusion.

A foot blister is painless in a person with neuropathy. What does that imply?

  1. It still needs prompt attention
  2. It is safe to ignore
  3. A heating pad will speed healing
Check the answer and why

It still needs prompt attention

Reduced sensation can hide injury. Prompt assessment helps prevent worsening wounds.

A client with a T4 spinal cord injury has BP 208/112, HR 48, a pounding headache and flushing above the injury. What does the nurse do first?

  1. Administer the prescribed antihypertensive
  2. Sit the client upright
  3. Check for a fecal impaction
  4. Lay the client flat and elevate the legs
Check the answer and why

Sit the client upright

In autonomic dysreflexia the first action is to sit her up , which uses gravity to drop the blood pressure immediately, then find and remove the trigger - check the bladder before the bowel. Antihypertensives are an adjunct, not the first move, and lying flat worsens the hypertension.

Fifteen minutes into a transfusion a client has chills, fever and flank pain. What does the nurse do first?

  1. Notify the provider
  2. Stop the transfusion
  3. Slow the rate and reassess in 15 minutes
  4. Administer the prescribed antipyretic
Check the answer and why

Stop the transfusion

For any suspected reaction the sequence is stop the transfusion, keep the line open with 0.9 percent NS on new tubing, stay with the client, then notify . Slowing the rate keeps incompatible blood going in, and notifying first delays the one action that stops the exposure.

A drip is ordered at 5 mcg/kg/min for a client weighing 154 lb. The bag contains 400 mg in 250 mL. What rate does the nurse program?

  1. 8.2 mL/hr
  2. 13.1 mL/hr
  3. 21 mL/hr
  4. 26.3 mL/hr
Check the answer and why

13.1 mL/hr

154 divided by 2.2 = 70 kg; 70 x 5 = 350 mcg/min = 21,000 mcg/hr = 21 mg/hr; the bag is 1.6 mg/mL, so 21 divided by 1.6 = 13.1 mL/hr . Answering 21 means stopping at mg/hr and never converting to volume - round only at the very end.

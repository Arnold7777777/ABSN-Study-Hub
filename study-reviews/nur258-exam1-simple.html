Eye, ear, infection and immunity

Rules to remember

  • A new curtain across vision needs urgent eye evaluation.
  • Match precautions to how the organism spreads.
  • Treat breathing or circulation problems after allergen exposure immediately.

See this → think this

A patient reports flashes, new floaters and a painless dark curtain in one eye.

Think…
Think retinal detachment. Delay can threaten vision.

A patient with a persistent cough, weight loss and night sweats is being evaluated for pulmonary TB.

Think…
Suspected pulmonary tuberculosis requires airborne precautions.

A patient develops wheezing, throat tightness and hypotension after an infusion.

Think…
Anaphylaxis is a systemic reaction that can rapidly threaten the airway and circulation.

What do I do first?

Sensory Disorders — Eye & Ear

First action
Arrange urgent ophthalmologic assessment and clarify onset and vision changes.
Look for a response
Document baseline vision and ensure prompt specialist evaluation.

Infectious Diseases & HIV

First action
Use the designated airborne infection isolation room and appropriate respirator. Mask the patient for necessary transport.
Look for a response
Verify precautions are maintained while the diagnostic workup proceeds.

Allergic, Inflammatory & Immunologic Disorders

First action
Stop the suspected trigger, call for emergency help and give IM epinephrine per protocol.
Look for a response
Reassess breathing and blood pressure promptly and monitor for recurrent symptoms.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

For a visual-acuity result such as 20/40, the first number is the testing distance. The second describes what a person with standard vision can read at that distance.

A normal-looking eye does not exclude retinal detachment when new flashes and a curtain-like field loss are reported.

Practice and memory cues

Separate a local rash from systemic allergy: airway symptoms, breathing difficulty or poor perfusion change the urgency.

Four clients arrive in the eye clinic. Which does the nurse see first?

  1. A client with gradual painless loss of side vision over 2 years
  2. A client with sudden severe eye pain, halos, vomiting, a fixed dilated pupil and a hazy cornea
  3. A client with a clouded lens and worsening night glare
  4. A client with wavy lines on an Amsler grid at home
Check the answer and why

A client with sudden severe eye pain, halos, vomiting, a fixed dilated pupil and a hazy cornea

Acute closed-angle glaucoma presents as fixed dilated pupil, hazy cornea and pain with vomiting, and it is a surgical emergency - pressure must come down or vision is lost. The gradual painless peripheral loss is chronic open-angle glaucoma: serious, but managed with lifelong drops, not tonight.

A nurse sustains a needlestick from a client who is HIV positive. What does the nurse do first?

  1. Report the exposure to the charge nurse
  2. Wash the site with soap and water
  3. Draw a baseline HIV antibody test
  4. Begin post-exposure prophylaxis
Check the answer and why

Wash the site with soap and water

The sequence taught is wash with soap and water first , then report, start PEP and draw baseline labs. Rushing to PEP feels more urgent, but the first act reduces the inoculum still on the skin.

Module 1 · Sensory Disorders 15 short topics

Module 1

Eye and ear tests

  • Recognize: Eyes: acuity/fields, tonometry and retinal examination identify different problems. Ears: otoscopy, audiometry and Weber/Rinne distinguish obstruction, conduction and nerve loss.
  • Act: Explain the test, compare both sides and report sudden vision or hearing changes promptly.
  • Remember: Match the test to function: pressure is not acuity; air/bone conduction helps classify hearing loss.

Module 1

Impaired vision

  • Recognize: Reduced functional vision can persist despite correction; assess what the patient can actually see and do.
  • Act: Orient to the room, announce your presence, maintain consistent placement and offer assistance with mobility and self-care.
  • Remember: Speak directly to the patient. Support independence rather than assuming blindness means total loss of vision.

Module 1

Glaucoma

  • Recognize: Open-angle disease gradually damages peripheral vision. Acute angle closure causes severe eye pain, halos, blurred vision and nausea.
  • Act: Treat sudden painful visual changes as an emergency; reinforce prescribed pressure-lowering drops and follow-up.
  • Remember: Glaucoma damages the optic nerve; established loss is irreversible. Chronic disease can be silent before substantial loss occurs.

Module 1

Cataracts

  • Recognize: A cloudy lens causes painless progressive blur, glare and difficulty seeing at night.
  • Act: After surgery, use ordered drops and protection; avoid pressure or straining and report severe pain or worsening vision.
  • Remember: Cataracts affect the lens, unlike glaucoma's optic-nerve injury. Surgical replacement improves vision; postoperative pain should not simply be dismissed.

Module 1

Retinal detachment

  • Recognize: Sudden flashes, new floaters or a curtain over vision suggest the retina is separating.
  • Act: Arrange urgent ophthalmic evaluation; follow procedure-specific positioning and restrictions after repair.
  • Remember: Usually painless does not mean harmless. With an intraocular gas bubble, air travel and nitrous oxide can dangerously expand the bubble until cleared.

Module 1

Macular degeneration

  • Recognize: Central vision becomes distorted or blurred while peripheral vision may remain; a straight-line grid can appear wavy.
  • Act: Report new distortion promptly, support low-vision aids and reinforce specialist monitoring and prescribed treatment.
  • Remember: Dry disease is degenerative; wet disease involves abnormal leaking vessels and can deteriorate rapidly. Central loss interferes with reading and recognizing faces.

Module 1

Diabetic retinopathy

  • Recognize: Retinal microvascular injury may be asymptomatic initially, later causing blur, floaters or visual loss.
  • Act: Reinforce dilated retinal screening and glucose, blood-pressure and lipid management; urgently report sudden changes.
  • Remember: Absence of symptoms does not exclude damage. Laser, injections or surgery depend on retinal findings; diabetes care and eye surveillance work together.

Module 1

Conjunctivitis

  • Recognize: Redness and discharge suggest conjunctival inflammation; itching favors allergy, while infectious patterns vary.
  • Act: Use hand hygiene, separate towels, avoid sharing eye products and follow prescribed therapy; remove contact lenses until cleared.
  • Remember: Significant pain, photophobia or reduced vision needs evaluation beyond routine conjunctivitis. Antibiotics do not treat viral or allergic causes.

Module 1

Hearing loss

  • Recognize: Conductive loss disrupts sound transmission; sensorineural loss involves the cochlea or nerve. Assess onset, laterality and communication needs.
  • Act: Face the patient, reduce background noise, speak clearly and verify hearing-aid function; report sudden loss urgently.
  • Remember: Shouting can distort speech. Use written or visual supports and teach-back rather than treating a missed question as confusion.

Module 1

Cerumen impaction

  • Recognize: Wax can cause conductive hearing loss, fullness or discomfort; inspect the canal and tympanic membrane.
  • Act: Use appropriate removal methods; avoid irrigation with suspected perforation, tubes, relevant ear surgery or significant infection unless cleared.
  • Remember: Do not push cotton swabs into the canal. Distinguish wax blockage from sudden sensorineural hearing loss requiring urgent assessment.

Module 1

Otitis externa

  • Recognize: External-canal inflammation causes pain, often increased by pulling the pinna or pressing the tragus; moisture and trauma contribute.
  • Act: Keep the ear dry, administer prescribed topical therapy correctly and avoid inserting objects.
  • Remember: Diabetes or immunosuppression plus severe persistent pain warrants prompt evaluation for invasive infection. This is a canal problem, not middle-ear fluid.

Module 1

Tympanic membrane perforation

  • Recognize: Trauma, pressure changes or infection can perforate the eardrum, causing sudden pain, drainage and reduced hearing.
  • Act: Keep the ear dry and avoid unapproved drops, irrigation and forceful pressure changes; arrange evaluation.
  • Remember: Some perforations heal spontaneously; persistent defects may need repair. Sudden severe vertigo or major hearing loss needs urgent assessment.

Module 1

Otitis media and middle-ear surgery

  • Recognize: Acute middle-ear infection causes pain and an abnormal tympanic membrane; chronic disease can produce persistent drainage and hearing loss.
  • Act: Give prescribed treatment and protect the repaired ear after surgery; monitor facial movement, hearing, drainage and worsening vertigo.
  • Remember: Follow procedure-specific positioning, water and pressure restrictions. Persistent drainage or cholesteatoma can damage middle-ear structures and may require surgery.

Module 1

Ménière disease

  • Recognize: Episodic vertigo, fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus and ear fullness reflect excess inner-ear fluid.
  • Act: During attacks, prevent falls and reduce movement/stimulation; administer prescribed symptom treatment and reinforce the individualized diet/medication plan.
  • Remember: Vertigo is episodic and hearing can progressively decline. Teach safe mobility and avoidance of hazardous activity while symptomatic.

Module 1

Vertigo, tinnitus, BPPV and ototoxicity

  • Recognize: Vertigo is a false movement sensation; tinnitus is perceived sound. Brief position-triggered vertigo suggests BPPV, while ototoxic drugs can impair hearing or balance.
  • Act: Prevent falls, review medications and report new hearing changes; use prescribed repositioning maneuvers for BPPV.
  • Remember: BPPV involves displaced inner-ear crystals. Persistent neurologic findings or severe new symptoms require evaluation beyond a benign ear diagnosis.

Module 2

Immune assessment and CBC

  • Recognize: Age, nutrition, chronic illness, medications and exposures affect immune defense. Leukopenia is low WBC; leukocytosis is high WBC; neutropenia specifically reduces neutrophils.
  • Act: Interpret the differential/ANC and clinical trend, assess infection sites and urgently report fever with impaired defenses.
  • Remember: A high WBC alone does not establish bacterial infection; a low neutrophil count can blunt expected inflammatory findings.

Module 2

Isolation, neutropenia and device infections

  • Recognize: Neutropenia increases infection risk; central lines, urinary catheters and ventilation create device-associated infection routes.
  • Act: Use appropriate transmission precautions, meticulous hand hygiene and device care. Review necessity daily: sterile line care, closed urinary drainage and ventilator oral care/head elevation.
  • Remember: CLABSI: protect insertion/hub sites. CAUTI: keep flow unobstructed and bag below bladder. VAP: oral hygiene, mobility and liberation protocols. Routine chlorhexidine oral care is not recommended here.

Module 2

HIV diagnosis and AIDS

  • Recognize: HIV progressively depletes CD4 cells; opportunistic infections and certain malignancies signal advanced disease. AIDS can be defined by CD4 criteria or an AIDS-defining condition.
  • Act: Support testing, antiretroviral adherence and prompt infection evaluation.
  • Remember: ELISA/Western blot appears in course terminology, but current testing uses antigen/antibody screening, differentiation testing and nucleic-acid testing when indicated; use the actual laboratory algorithm.

Module 2

HIV transmission, stages and monitoring

  • Recognize: HIV transmits through specific body fluids, not casual contact. Acute infection may resemble a viral illness; chronic infection may remain silent before advanced immunodeficiency.
  • Act: Teach prevention, sustained ART and urgent evaluation after exposure; PEP is time-sensitive and must start within 72 hours when indicated.
  • Remember: Viral load measures replication/treatment response; CD4 count measures immune status. Undetectable sexual transmission risk depends on sustained viral suppression.

Module 2

Infectious-disease priorities

  • Recognize: Look for exposure, susceptible host, local infection and systemic deterioration; immunocompromised clients may show subtle signs.
  • Act: Assess ABCs/perfusion, apply appropriate precautions and obtain ordered cultures without delaying urgent treatment.
  • Remember: Identify the organism and transmission route instead of assuming every fever needs the same isolation or antibiotic. Reassess response, hydration and organ function.

Module 2

Chain of infection

  • Recognize: The chain requires an infectious agent, reservoir, exit route, transmission method, entry route and susceptible host.
  • Act: Break links with hand hygiene, PPE, cleaning, safe sharps, respiratory hygiene, vaccination and appropriate device care.
  • Remember: Choose an intervention that interrupts the relevant link. Standard precautions apply to everyone; contact, droplet or airborne measures depend on suspected transmission.

Module 2

C. difficile

  • Recognize: Antibiotic exposure can permit toxin-producing C. difficile to cause watery diarrhea, abdominal pain and severe colitis.
  • Act: Use contact precautions, appropriate handwashing and sporicidal cleaning; monitor fluids and give prescribed targeted therapy.
  • Remember: Alcohol sanitizer does not reliably kill spores. Avoid unnecessary antibiotics and report distention, hypotension or worsening illness promptly.

Module 2

MRSA and VRE

  • Recognize: MRSA resists common beta-lactams; VRE resists vancomycin. Colonization and active infection are different findings.
  • Act: Follow culture-directed therapy and facility contact precautions, protect wounds and devices, and perform hand/environmental hygiene.
  • Remember: An organism's resistance pattern determines effective therapy; colonization alone does not automatically mean antibiotics are needed. Communicate precautions during transfers.

Module 2

Immunizations, diarrhea and emerging infections

  • Recognize: Vaccination history, travel, food/water and outbreak exposures guide risk assessment. Diarrhea can rapidly compromise fluids and electrolytes.
  • Act: Use current vaccine guidance, restore hydration, monitor severity and apply exposure-specific testing/precautions; report suspected outbreaks through the required channels.
  • Remember: Immune status affects vaccine selection. New or emerging infections require updated public-health guidance rather than assumptions based on older transmission rules.

Module 3 · Allergic, Inflammatory, & Immunological (Rheumatic) Disorders 7 short topics

Module 3

Four hypersensitivity mechanisms

  • Recognize: Type I is immediate IgE-mediated allergy; type II targets cells; type III involves immune complexes; type IV is delayed T-cell activity.
  • Act: Identify the trigger and severity; rapidly treat airway or circulatory compromise rather than waiting to classify the mechanism.
  • Remember: ACID: Anaphylactic, Cytotoxic, Immune complex, Delayed. Timing and organ involvement distinguish mechanisms; not every rash is anaphylaxis.

Module 3

Anaphylaxis

  • Recognize: Sudden airway swelling, wheeze, hypotension or multisystem symptoms after exposure indicate a potentially fatal reaction.
  • Act: Give IM epinephrine promptly per emergency protocol, activate help, support airway/oxygenation and circulation, and monitor for recurrence.
  • Remember: Antihistamines are adjuncts, not substitutes for epinephrine. A skin rash may be absent; teach trigger avoidance and correct auto-injector use.

Module 3

Urticaria and food allergy

  • Recognize: Urticaria causes transient itchy wheals; food allergy may progress to breathing difficulty, swelling, vomiting or circulatory compromise.
  • Act: Assess airway and systemic involvement first; use prescribed symptom therapy for isolated hives and emergency epinephrine for anaphylaxis.
  • Remember: Teach label reading, avoiding known allergens and carrying the emergency plan. Do not equate every food intolerance with immune-mediated allergy.

Module 3

Latex allergy

  • Recognize: Latex exposure may cause local dermatitis or immediate systemic allergy; repeated healthcare exposure increases risk.
  • Act: Identify and remove latex sources, use latex-free supplies and document the allergy; urgently treat airway or circulatory symptoms.
  • Remember: Delayed contact dermatitis differs from immediate IgE-mediated anaphylaxis. Some food cross-reactions can occur, but assess actual history rather than assuming all related foods are unsafe.

Module 3

Rheumatic disease

  • Recognize: Inflammation or autoimmune activity can affect joints and other organs; evaluate pain, stiffness, fatigue, function and systemic findings.
  • Act: Balance activity and rest, preserve mobility, monitor medication effects and screen for infection or organ involvement.
  • Remember: A rheumatic diagnosis is not just joint pain. Connect disease activity, treatment-related immune suppression and functional limitations to individualized care.

Module 3

Systemic lupus erythematosus

  • Recognize: Autoimmune inflammation may cause fatigue, joint pain, photosensitive rash and renal or other organ involvement.
  • Act: Monitor kidney findings and flare symptoms; reinforce sun protection, medication adherence and infection precautions appropriate to therapy.
  • Remember: Distinguish infection from a flare before attributing fever to lupus. Organ involvement determines urgency and treatment; avoid abrupt steroid withdrawal.

Module 3

Gout and fibromyalgia

  • Recognize: Gout causes acute inflammatory joint pain from urate crystals; fibromyalgia causes widespread pain, fatigue and sleep problems without inflammatory joint destruction.
  • Act: For gout, use prescribed anti-inflammatory therapy and long-term urate management when indicated. For fibromyalgia, support graded activity, sleep and multimodal symptom control.
  • Remember: Do not treat fibromyalgia as crystal arthritis; acute gout relief and prevention use different strategies.

Every exam: priority + dose checks
  • Clinical judgment: Recognize cues → analyze → prioritize → plan → act → evaluate. Compare with baseline; reassess the response.
  • Dose safety: Verify patient, allergies, drug, dose, route and time. Use kilograms for weight-based doses; check the safe range and maximum.
  • Calculate: Ordered dose ÷ available dose × volume. Example: 250 mg ÷ 125 mg × 5 mL = 10 mL. Clarify an unsafe or unclear order before giving it.

Rules to remember

  • A new curtain across vision needs urgent eye evaluation.
  • Match precautions to how the organism spreads.
  • Treat breathing or circulation problems after allergen exposure immediately.

  1. First action
    • Arrange urgent ophthalmologic assessment and clarify onset and vision changes.
  2. Look for a response
    • Document baseline vision and ensure prompt specialist evaluation.

Infectious Diseases & HIV

  1. First action
    • Use the designated airborne infection isolation room and appropriate respirator.
    • Mask the patient for necessary transport.
  2. Look for a response
    • Verify precautions are maintained while the diagnostic workup proceeds.

    • Stop the suspected trigger, call for emergency help and give IM epinephrine per protocol.

    • Reassess breathing and blood pressure promptly and monitor for recurrent symptoms.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • For a visual-acuity result such as 20/40, the first number is the testing distance. The second describes what a person with standard vision can read at that distance.
  • A normal-looking eye does not exclude retinal detachment when new flashes and a curtain-like field loss are reported.

Practice and memory cues

Separate a local rash from systemic allergy: airway symptoms, breathing difficulty or poor perfusion change the urgency.

Four clients arrive in the eye clinic. Which does the nurse see first?

  1. A client with gradual painless loss of side vision over 2 years
  2. A client with sudden severe eye pain, halos, vomiting, a fixed dilated pupil and a hazy cornea
  3. A client with a clouded lens and worsening night glare
  4. A client with wavy lines on an Amsler grid at home
Check the answer and why

A client with sudden severe eye pain, halos, vomiting, a fixed dilated pupil and a hazy cornea

Acute closed-angle glaucoma presents as fixed dilated pupil, hazy cornea and pain with vomiting, and it is a surgical emergency - pressure must come down or vision is lost. The gradual painless peripheral loss is chronic open-angle glaucoma: serious, but managed with lifelong drops, not tonight.

A nurse sustains a needlestick from a client who is HIV positive. What does the nurse do first?

  1. Report the exposure to the charge nurse
  2. Wash the site with soap and water
  3. Draw a baseline HIV antibody test
  4. Begin post-exposure prophylaxis
Check the answer and why

Wash the site with soap and water

The sequence taught is wash with soap and water first , then report, start PEP and draw baseline labs. Rushing to PEP feels more urgent, but the first act reduces the inoculum still on the skin.

Burns, endocrine disorders and diabetes

Check airway risk in burns, then use urine, sodium, glucose and mental status to distinguish endocrine and diabetes emergencies.

Rules to remember

  • Suspected inhalation injury can progress before visible swelling appears.
  • Match fluid output with the sodium trend.
  • Check whether the patient can swallow before treating hypoglycemia.

See this → think this

After an enclosed-space fire, a patient has hoarseness, soot around the mouth and increasing work of breathing.

Think…
Think inhalation injury. Airway edema can progress quickly.

After pituitary surgery a patient has very large amounts of dilute urine, marked thirst and high sodium.

Think…
Diabetes insipidus causes loss of free water. SIADH usually causes water retention and low sodium.

An insulin-treated patient is shaky and sweaty but awake and able to swallow safely.

Think…
Low glucose can impair brain function. Oral carbohydrate is appropriate only when swallowing is safe.

What do I do first?

Burns

First action
Get urgent airway support, administer high-concentration oxygen per protocol and prepare for airway management.
Look for a response
Reassess voice, air movement, work of breathing and perfusion.

Endocrine Disorders

First action
Measure output, assess volume status and report findings for ordered fluid and hormone therapy.
Look for a response
Trend intake, output, sodium and neurologic status.

Diabetes

First action
Check glucose. If below 70 mg/dL, give fast-acting carbohydrate per protocol. If unable to swallow, use protocol-directed IV dextrose or glucagon.
Look for a response
Recheck glucose after 15 minutes for oral treatment and repeat per protocol until corrected.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

Typical adult serum sodium reference range is 135–145 mEq/L. DI tends toward high sodium with dilute urine. SIADH tends toward low sodium with concentrated urine.

For an awake adult able to swallow with glucose below 70 mg/dL, the usual 15–15 rule is 15 g of fast carbohydrate and a glucose recheck in 15 minutes. Typical fasting glucose is 70–99 mg/dL.

Practice and memory cues

Clear lungs do not clear a burn airway. Likewise, a glucose number alone does not distinguish DKA from HHS: look for ketones, acidosis, dehydration and mental-status change.

A client with burns to the face and anterior neck is hoarse and has singed nasal hair and carbonaceous sputum. What is the priority action?

  1. Calculate the TBSA using the rule of nines
  2. Obtain intubation equipment now
  3. Start lactated Ringer's by the Parkland formula
  4. Apply silver sulfadiazine to the facial burns
Check the answer and why

Obtain intubation equipment now

Inhalation cues mean the airway will swell shut within 1 to 2 hours , so intubation equipment comes before TBSA estimation or fluids. Face, neck or chest burns make airway the priority; only waist-down burns put fluids first.

A client has 6 L of pale urine in 24 hours, urine specific gravity 1.002, serum sodium 152 and BP 88/54. Which condition does the nurse suspect?

Typical adult references: serum sodium 135–145 mEq/L; urine specific gravity about 1.005–1.030.

  1. Diabetes insipidus
  2. SIADH
  3. Cushing's syndrome
  4. Thyroid storm
Check the answer and why

Diabetes insipidus

Dilute high-volume urine with low specific gravity and high serum sodium is DI - dry inside, too little ADH. SIADH is the mirror: scant concentrated urine, high specific gravity, overload and low sodium.

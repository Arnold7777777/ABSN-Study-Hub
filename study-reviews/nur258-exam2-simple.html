Module 4 · Burns 8 short topics

Module 4

Burn mechanisms and deep injury

  • Recognize: Thermal injury damages tissue; electrical injury can hide deep muscle damage, dysrhythmia and myoglobin-related kidney injury. Chemicals continue damaging tissue while present.
  • Act: Ensure scene safety, remove the exposure appropriately and monitor ECG, urine and limb perfusion. Deep compartment pressure may require fasciotomy.
  • Remember: Visible skin injury can underestimate electrical damage. Fasciotomy releases fascia; escharotomy releases constricting burn eschar.

Module 4

Emergent burn resuscitation

  • Recognize: Capillary leak shifts fluid into tissues, causing edema and intravascular depletion; severe burns also affect perfusion and GI function.
  • Act: Prioritize airway, ordered fluids and hourly perfusion/urine reassessment; consider escharotomy for constricting circumferential burns.
  • Remember: Course Parkland: 4 mL × kg × %TBSA over 24 hours, half within 8 hours of injury, not arrival. Actual resuscitation follows current orders and response rather than formula alone.

Module 4

Burn depth, appearance and severity

  • Recognize: Superficial burns are red and painful; partial-thickness burns may blister; full-thickness burns can appear leathery and have reduced sensation. Extent, depth, location and inhalation injury determine severity.
  • Act: Assess cleaned wound depth, perfusion and sensation, prevent hypothermia and consider critical location, age and inhalation injury when judging severity.
  • Remember: Do not count superficial erythema in resuscitation TBSA. Deep burns can be less painful.

Module 4

Inhalation injury

  • Recognize: Enclosed-space exposure, facial burns, soot, hoarseness or stridor raise airway concern; swelling may progress after the initial assessment.
  • Act: Give indicated high-concentration oxygen, obtain urgent airway evaluation and prepare for early airway protection when needed.
  • Remember: A reassuring conventional pulse-oximeter reading does not exclude carbon-monoxide poisoning. Airway injury and toxic inhalation can occur with little visible skin burn.

Module 4

Burn phases and changing priorities

  • Recognize: Emergent: capillary leak and shock. Acute: fluid return, wound management and infection risk. Rehabilitation: function, scarring and adaptation.
  • Act: Reassess respiratory, cardiovascular, renal, GI, temperature and mobility effects as the phase changes.
  • Remember: A plan appropriate during fluid loss may need revision when fluid returns to circulation. Nursing priorities follow physiology rather than a fixed calendar alone.

Module 4

Acute burn care and rehabilitation

  • Recognize: After stabilization, open wounds, pain, infection, hypermetabolism, stiffness and psychosocial distress dominate.
  • Act: Premedicate for wound care; support high-protein/calorie nutrition, aseptic dressings, debridement and ordered topical therapy. Protect grafts initially, then advance prescribed range of motion, splinting and mobility.
  • Remember: Pressure garments/scar care follow healing and specialist orders. Teach wound changes, infection warning signs, home exercise and coping support; grafting covers wounds while debridement removes devitalized tissue.

Module 4

Burn wound care choices

  • Recognize: Wound depth, perfusion, contamination, drainage and healing determine the dressing and treatment plan.
  • Act: Clean and debride as ordered; give analgesia before procedures, use prescribed topical agents and protect viable tissue/grafts.
  • Remember: No single topical agent or dressing fits every burn. Document wound appearance and response, monitor systemic infection and teach safe home care with the burn team's instructions.

Module 4

TBSA: rule of nines and palmar estimate

  • Recognize: Adult rule of nines: head/neck and each arm 9%; each leg, anterior trunk and posterior trunk 18%; perineum 1%. The patient’s palm including fingers is approximately 1% TBSA.
  • Act: Use the adult body map for larger areas and palmar estimation for small scattered burns.
  • Remember: Count partial/full-thickness burns, not superficial redness, for resuscitation TBSA. Children need age-adjusted assessment.

Module 5

Addison disease and adrenal crisis

  • Recognize: Low cortisol causes weakness, hypotension and hypoglycemia; primary adrenal failure also loses aldosterone, causing sodium loss and potassium retention. Crisis can progress to shock.
  • Act: Urgently support circulation with prescribed fluids and IV glucocorticoids; monitor glucose, electrolytes, ECG and urine output.
  • Remember: Primary disease can cause hyperpigmentation. Teach lifelong replacement, the prescribed stress-dose plan and never abruptly stopping chronic steroids.

Module 5

Cushing syndrome and disease

  • Recognize: Excess cortisol causes central weight gain, thin skin/bruising, muscle weakness, hyperglycemia and infection risk.
  • Act: Monitor glucose, blood pressure, skin and infection; protect fragile tissues and follow the steroid taper or endocrine treatment plan.
  • Remember: Cushing syndrome means excess cortisol from any cause; Cushing disease specifically means a pituitary ACTH source. Exogenous steroids are a common cause.

Module 5

Pheochromocytoma

  • Recognize: A catecholamine-secreting tumor can cause episodic severe hypertension, headache, sweating and palpitations.
  • Act: Monitor hemodynamics and follow specialist preparation for surgery; avoid provoking catecholamine release.
  • Remember: When ordered, alpha blockade precedes beta blockade; isolated beta blockade can worsen hypertension. Plasma or urine metanephrine testing helps evaluate the suspected tumor.

Module 5

Hypothyroidism and myxedema coma

  • Recognize: Low thyroid activity causes fatigue, cold intolerance, constipation and slowed heart rate. Myxedema coma adds altered consciousness, hypothermia and hypoventilation.
  • Act: Support airway/ventilation and give prescribed thyroid replacement and emergency treatment; monitor cardiac response.
  • Remember: Primary disease generally has high TSH with low thyroid hormone; secondary disease reflects pituitary dysfunction. Severe hypothyroidism can be life-threatening even without literal coma.

Module 5

Hyperthyroidism and thyroid storm

  • Recognize: Heat intolerance, weight loss, tremor and tachycardia suggest excess thyroid hormone; fever, severe tachycardia and altered mental status suggest storm.
  • Act: Escalate urgently, support ABCs and administer ordered antithyroid, rate-control, cooling and supportive treatment.
  • Remember: Thyroid storm is a clinical emergency. Follow medication sequencing; iodine is given after antithyroid medication when prescribed, not as a stand-alone first step.

Module 5

DI versus SIADH

  • Recognize: DI loses large amounts of dilute urine, risking dehydration and hypernatremia. SIADH retains water, causing dilutional hyponatremia with concentrated urine.
  • Act: Trend sodium, neurologic status, intake/output and daily weight; follow prescribed fluid and hormone treatment.
  • Remember: DI: water escapes. SIADH: water stays. Severe symptomatic hyponatremia needs urgent controlled correction; rapid sodium shifts can cause neurologic injury.

Module 6

Type 1 and type2 diabetes

  • Recognize: Type 1 usually involves autoimmune beta-cell destruction and severe insulin deficiency. Type 2 combines insulin resistance with progressive loss of beta-cell function.
  • Act: Monitor glucose and symptoms, teach the individualized medication/meal/activity plan and confirm safe self-management.
  • Remember: Type 1 requires insulin replacement. Age and body size alone cannot distinguish the type; either type can develop acute hyperglycemic emergencies.

Module 6

Insulin timing and safety

  • Recognize: Insulin formulations differ in onset, peak and duration; strongest activity increases hypoglycemia risk.
  • Act: Verify product, concentration, dose and route. Coordinate meal-related insulin with food and check glucose when symptoms occur.
  • Remember: Rapid, regular, intermediate and basal products have different profiles. Mixing, storage and meal rules are product-specific; long-acting products do not share a universal peak or snack time.

Module 6

Oral diabetes medications

  • Recognize: Metformin lowers liver glucose output; sulfonylureas/meglitinides increase insulin release; TZDs improve sensitivity; alpha-glucosidase inhibitors slow digestion; DPP-4 drugs enhance incretins; SGLT2 drugs increase urinary glucose.
  • Act: Watch for metformin GI/renal concerns, secretagogue hypoglycemia, TZD edema/heart failure, digestive effects, persistent abdominal pain and SGLT2 genital infection/ketoacidosis.
  • Remember: Match teaching to the exact drug and kidney/heart status, not a single rule for every diabetes tablet.

Module 6

DKA versus HHS

  • Recognize: DKA combines ketones and acidosis; HHS causes marked hyperosmolar dehydration without significant ketosis. Both can alter consciousness; overlap and euglycemic DKA occur.
  • Act: Begin prescribed fluids, check potassium before insulin and repeatedly monitor glucose, electrolytes, perfusion and mental status.
  • Remember: Correct low potassium before starting insulin per prescribed protocol; total-body stores may be depleted even when serum potassium is high.

Module 6

Diabetes sick-day plan

  • Recognize: Illness can raise glucose, while poor intake or some medicines cause lows; vomiting and dehydration increase risk.
  • Act: Check glucose more often, check ketones as directed, maintain fluids and follow medication/contact instructions. Type 1 patients still need basal insulin.
  • Remember: Breathing difficulty, confusion, persistent vomiting or inability to retain fluids needs urgent help. Use an individualized written plan, not a blanket rule to stop all medicines.

Module 6

Hypoglycemia

  • Recognize: Glucose below 70 mg/dL can cause sweating, shaking, hunger, palpitations, confusion or reduced consciousness.
  • Act: If awake and able to swallow, give 15 g rapid carbohydrate and recheck in 15 minutes; repeat if still low. If swallowing is unsafe, obtain emergency help and use prescribed glucagon or IV glucose.
  • Remember: Never give oral food/drink to an unconscious patient. Treat symptoms promptly rather than waiting for a predicted insulin peak.

Module 6

Long-term diabetes complications

  • Recognize: Chronic vascular and nerve injury affects eyes, kidneys, heart, brain and feet; neuropathy can hide injury.
  • Act: Reinforce retinal and kidney screening, cardiovascular risk control and daily foot checks. Protect feet with fitted shoes and report wounds or color changes.
  • Remember: Avoid barefoot walking, heating pads and self-treatment of corns. Lack of pain does not mean an ulcer or infection is harmless.

Every exam: priority + dose checks
  • Clinical judgment: Recognize cues → analyze → prioritize → plan → act → evaluate. Compare with baseline; reassess the response.
  • Dose safety: Verify patient, allergies, drug, dose, route and time. Use kilograms for weight-based doses; check the safe range and maximum.
  • Calculate: Ordered dose ÷ available dose × volume. Example: 250 mg ÷ 125 mg × 5 mL = 10 mL. Clarify an unsafe or unclear order before giving it.

Rules to remember

  • Suspected inhalation injury can progress before visible swelling appears.
  • Match fluid output with the sodium trend.
  • Check whether the patient can swallow before treating hypoglycemia.

See this → think this

After an enclosed-space fire, a patient has hoarseness, soot around the mouth and increasing work of breathing.

Think…
  • Think inhalation injury.
  • Airway edema can progress quickly.

After pituitary surgery a patient has very large amounts of dilute urine, marked thirst and high sodium.

Think…
  • Diabetes insipidus causes loss of free water.
  • SIADH usually causes water retention and low sodium.

An insulin-treated patient is shaky and sweaty but awake and able to swallow safely.

Think…
  • Low glucose can impair brain function.
  • Oral carbohydrate is appropriate only when swallowing is safe.

What do I do first?

Burns

  1. First action
    • Get urgent airway support, administer high-concentration oxygen per protocol and prepare for airway management.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess voice, air movement, work of breathing and perfusion.

Endocrine Disorders

  1. First action
    • Measure output, assess volume status and report findings for ordered fluid and hormone therapy.
  2. Look for a response
    • Trend intake, output, sodium and neurologic status.

Diabetes

  1. First action
    • Check glucose.
    • If below 70 mg/dL, give fast-acting carbohydrate per protocol.
    • If unable to swallow, use protocol-directed IV dextrose or glucagon.
  2. Look for a response
    • Recheck glucose after 15 minutes for oral treatment and repeat per protocol until corrected.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • Typical adult serum sodium reference range is 135–145 mEq/L. DI tends toward high sodium with dilute urine. SIADH tends toward low sodium with concentrated urine.
  • For an awake adult able to swallow with glucose below 70 mg/dL, the usual 15–15 rule is 15 g of fast carbohydrate and a glucose recheck in 15 minutes. Typical fasting glucose is 70–99 mg/dL.

Practice and memory cues

Clear lungs do not clear a burn airway. Likewise, a glucose number alone does not distinguish DKA from HHS: look for ketones, acidosis, dehydration and mental-status change.

A client with burns to the face and anterior neck is hoarse and has singed nasal hair and carbonaceous sputum. What is the priority action?

  1. Calculate the TBSA using the rule of nines
  2. Obtain intubation equipment now
  3. Start lactated Ringer's by the Parkland formula
  4. Apply silver sulfadiazine to the facial burns
Check the answer and why

Obtain intubation equipment now

Inhalation cues mean the airway will swell shut within 1 to 2 hours , so intubation equipment comes before TBSA estimation or fluids. Face, neck or chest burns make airway the priority; only waist-down burns put fluids first.

A client has 6 L of pale urine in 24 hours, urine specific gravity 1.002, serum sodium 152 and BP 88/54. Which condition does the nurse suspect?

Typical adult references: serum sodium 135–145 mEq/L; urine specific gravity about 1.005–1.030.

  1. Diabetes insipidus
  2. SIADH
  3. Cushing's syndrome
  4. Thyroid storm
Check the answer and why

Diabetes insipidus

Dilute high-volume urine with low specific gravity and high serum sodium is DI - dry inside, too little ADH. SIADH is the mirror: scant concentrated urine, high specific gravity, overload and low sodium.

Module 5 · Health promotion and nutrition 9 short topics

Module 5

Health promotion during pregnancy/self-care needs

  • Recognize: Pregnancy changes sleep, hygiene, activity, sexual comfort and medication needs; access and daily routines affect self-care.
  • Act: Ask about actual habits, provide practical comfort teaching, review all medicines/supplements and reinforce prenatal follow-up.
  • Remember: Avoid alcohol, smoking and unapproved drugs; health teaching should fit the person's circumstances and distinguish expected discomforts from symptoms needing evaluation.

Module 5

Minor systemic body changes of pregnancy

  • Recognize: Reflux, constipation, urinary frequency, back discomfort and dependent leg swelling commonly accompany pregnancy adaptations.
  • Act: Offer small meals, appropriate fluids/fiber, body mechanics and position changes; assess severity and associated symptoms before reassuring.
  • Remember: Dyspnea at rest, dysuria/fever, sudden swelling with headache or persistent severe pain requires evaluation rather than routine comfort advice alone.

Module 5

Effects of teratogens on fetus

  • Recognize: Teratogens can interfere with fetal development; alcohol, some medicines, infections and other exposures can cross the placenta.
  • Act: Review exposures and timing; reinforce prescribed folic acid and provider review of medicines before conception and throughout pregnancy.
  • Remember: Organ formation is particularly vulnerable to structural injury, while later exposures may affect growth or function; the placenta is not a complete barrier.

Module 5

Soft (early) signs of labor

  • Recognize: Lightening lowers the presenting part, easing breathing while increasing pelvic pressure; mucus plug passage may appear pink or blood-streaked.
  • Act: Explain what to monitor and when to contact the birth team, especially fluid leakage, heavy bleeding, reduced movement or symptoms before term.
  • Remember: These signs can precede labor; progressive cervical change with contractions establishes labor.

Module 5

Fluid needs during pregnancy

  • Recognize: Adequate fluids support circulation, bowel function and nutrition; vomiting or poor access can reduce intake.
  • Act: Encourage the course's usual goal of about 8–10 glasses of water daily unless the individual care plan limits fluids; assess dehydration and persistent vomiting.
  • Remember: Space fluids between meals when nausea interferes with eating; fluid advice must account for cardiac or renal restrictions.

Module 5

Weight gain during pregnancy

  • Recognize: Recommended pregnancy gain depends on prepregnancy BMI and fetal number; the pattern matters alongside the total.
  • Act: Track weight over time and assess intake, food access and sudden changes; refer for individualized nutrition support when needed.
  • Remember: Singleton targets: underweight 28–40 lb, normal BMI 25–35, overweight 15–25, obesity 11–20. Sudden gain with concerning symptoms is not simply overeating.

Module 5

Nutrition needs during pregnancy (foods to limit/avoid)

  • Recognize: Pregnancy increases needs for iron, folate and other nutrients; food safety reduces infection risk.
  • Act: Teach balanced intake, prenatal supplements and safe preparation; avoid unpasteurized foods and raw/undercooked animal products, and heat deli meats until steaming.
  • Remember: Iron absorbs better with vitamin C than with milk or antacids. No extra first-trimester calories are routinely needed for a normal-BMI singleton pregnancy.

Module 5

Common problems affecting nutritional health during pregnancy (n/v, pica, pyrosis)

  • Recognize: Nausea/vomiting can reduce intake; pica means craving nonfood substances; pyrosis means heartburn.
  • Act: Try small frequent meals and dry food before rising; assess pica/iron status and persistent vomiting, weight loss or dehydration.
  • Remember: Pica can accompany iron deficiency. Persistent severe vomiting needs assessment for hyperemesis rather than reassurance that all morning sickness is harmless.

Module 5

Women with unique needs (age, special diets, multiple gestation, hyperemesis gravidarum)

  • Recognize: Adolescence, restricted diets, multiple gestation, food insecurity and severe vomiting can make nutritional needs harder to meet.
  • Act: Assess the actual diet and barriers, arrange dietitian/food-support services and monitor weight, hydration and indicated laboratory trends.
  • Remember: Hyperemesis may require prescribed IV fluids, electrolyte correction and antiemetics; vegetarian eating requires planning for protein, iron, B12 and other nutrients.

Module 6

Four P’s of labor (passage, passenger, powers, psyche)

  • Recognize: Labor progress depends on passage, passenger, powers and psyche: pelvic route, fetal factors, contractions/pushing and emotional response.
  • Act: Assess all four when progress slows, including bladder, positioning, support and fetal status.
  • Remember: Do not treat every slow labor with stronger contractions; a malpositioned passenger or obstructed passage needs a different response. Some resources add maternal position as a fifth P.

Module 6

Passenger (fetal skull, attitude, lie, presentation, position, engagement, station)

  • Recognize: Attitude is flexion; lie compares fetal/maternal spines; presentation names the leading part; position describes its orientation. Skull molding helps adaptation.
  • Act: Assess presentation, engagement and descent through examination/Leopold maneuvers and confirm uncertain findings as ordered.
  • Remember: Engagement is passage of the widest presenting diameter through the inlet; station describes descent relative to ischial spines, with zero at the spines.

Module 6

Leopold maneuvers

  • Recognize: Abdominal palpation identifies the fetal part in the fundus, the back, presenting part and degree of descent.
  • Act: Explain the maneuvers, have the client empty the bladder and palpate systematically; use the fetal back to help locate heart tones.
  • Remember: Leopold maneuvers estimate lie/presentation and guide assessment; ultrasound can clarify uncertain presentation before procedures.

Module 6

Contractions (frequency, duration, intensity)

  • Recognize: Frequency measures the start of one contraction to the start of the next; duration measures start to finish. Intensity is contraction strength.
  • Act: Assess the pattern, strength and uterine relaxation along with fetal response; recognize excessive activity and inadequate recovery time.
  • Remember: External tocodynamometry shows timing, not precise contraction pressure. Rest between contractions supports uteroplacental perfusion.

Module 6

Cervical changes (dilation, effacement)

  • Recognize: Dilation opens the cervix toward 10 cm; effacement thins it toward 100%.
  • Act: Follow cervical change together with contractions, fetal station and maternal/fetal status; support coping and explain progress without promising a fixed pace.
  • Remember: Regular contractions alone do not confirm true labor. Progressive dilation and effacement distinguish labor from contractions without cervical change.

Module 6

Stages/phases of labor

  • Recognize: First stage ends at full dilation; second ends with birth; third ends with placental delivery; fourth is early recovery.
  • Act: Match assessment/support to the stage and watch fetal tolerance during labor, then fundal tone, bleeding and vital signs after birth.
  • Remember: First-stage latent/active/transition phases describe progression; the second stage begins at full dilation, not with the first painful contraction.

Module 6

Rupture of membranes

  • Recognize: A gush or persistent trickle may indicate membrane rupture; fluid appearance and fetal response matter.
  • Act: Check fetal heart rate promptly, note time/color/odor/amount, assess for prolapse when indicated and monitor temperature/infection signs; minimize unnecessary vaginal examinations.
  • Remember: Sudden fetal bradycardia after rupture can mean cord compression or prolapse, especially with a high presenting part.

Module 6

Maternal response to labor

  • Recognize: Contractions and pushing transiently increase cardiovascular workload; breathing, pulse and blood pressure can rise during effort. Nausea may reflect slowed gastric emptying.
  • Act: Support rest, position changes, appropriate hydration, bladder emptying and coping; compare findings between contractions.
  • Remember: Persistent tachycardia, hypotension, fever, hypoxemia or severe unexpected pain requires evaluation rather than being labeled a normal labor response.

Module 6

Fetal response to labor

  • Recognize: Contractions temporarily reduce uteroplacental flow; the fetus normally recovers during relaxation when reserve is adequate.
  • Act: Interpret baseline, variability, accelerations, decelerations and recovery together with maternal status and contraction activity.
  • Remember: Excess contractions, maternal hypotension or placental disease reduce reserve. A tracing feature alone does not establish the cause; assess the complete pattern.

Module 6

Fetal danger signs of labor (meconium staining)

  • Recognize: Meconium-stained fluid, persistent abnormal heart-rate patterns or poor recovery can signal fetal compromise and need evaluation.
  • Act: Assess fetal heart rate and maternal/contraction status, notify the team and prepare appropriate newborn support at birth.
  • Remember: Meconium raises concern but is not, by itself, proof of hypoxia; persistent bradycardia or late decelerations requires prompt action.

Module 6

Fetal heart rate monitoring

  • Recognize: Assess baseline, variability, accelerations and decelerations in context; the usual baseline is 110–160 beats/min.
  • Act: Position external transducers appropriately and compare the tracing with contractions and maternal vital signs; investigate concerning patterns promptly.
  • Remember: Moderate variability generally supports adequate current oxygenation. External monitoring differs from a scalp electrode or intrauterine pressure catheter, which require appropriate access and precautions.

Module 6

Periodic changes (accelerations, decelerations)

  • Recognize: Accelerations are generally reassuring. Early decelerations mirror contractions; variable decelerations vary abruptly; late decelerations follow the contraction peak.
  • Act: Relate timing to contractions and assess variability, recovery and maternal status before deciding the response.
  • Remember: Early usually means head compression; variable means cord compression; late suggests impaired uteroplacental perfusion. Persistent abnormal patterns need intervention.

Module 6

Management of periodic changes

  • Recognize: Recurrent variable/late decelerations, prolonged deceleration or reduced variability can indicate impaired oxygen delivery.
  • Act: Reposition laterally, stop oxytocin when running, assess maternal pressure/contractions, support ordered fluids and oxygen per the course response sequence, and notify the team.
  • Remember: Treat the cause: variables suggest cord compression; late patterns suggest placental perfusion problems. Persistent abnormalities can require expedited birth.

Module 6

Induction/augmentation of labor

  • Recognize: Induction begins labor; augmentation improves labor already underway. Oxytocin and cervical-ripening methods can produce excess uterine activity.
  • Act: Confirm indication/consent and fetal status, use the ordered method and monitor contractions plus fetal response; stop oxytocin for tachysystole or concerning fetal intolerance.
  • Remember: A favorable cervix improves induction success. Stronger contractions cannot overcome an obstructed passage or contraindication to vaginal birth.

Module 7

Pain and comfort management during labor (pharmacological)

  • Recognize: Labor pain changes from visceral abdominal/back pain toward sharper perineal pain; systemic opioids and regional methods have different effects.
  • Act: Assess preferences, pain relief, breathing, sedation and fetal response; monitor carefully after medication and provide assistance with mobility.
  • Remember: Systemic opioids near birth can depress newborn respirations. Relief must be balanced with maternal/fetal safety, and comfort measures can continue alongside medication.

Module 7

Complementary and alternative therapies for pain management

  • Recognize: Breathing, relaxation, massage, counterpressure, movement, heat and hydrotherapy may improve coping and reduce pain perception.
  • Act: Ask what feels helpful, adapt positioning and involve the chosen support person; assess response and maintain safety.
  • Remember: Nonpharmacologic comfort can continue with medication or an epidural. The client's preference can change during labor without representing failure.

Module 7

Types of anesthesia used during labor

  • Recognize: Epidural analgesia supports ongoing labor; spinal anesthesia acts rapidly for selected births; pudendal block targets perineal pain. General anesthesia affects consciousness and airway protection.
  • Act: Monitor blood pressure, respirations, fetal response and bladder after regional anesthesia; treat hypotension and assist mobility until sensation/motor function returns.
  • Remember: Epidural hypotension can reduce placental perfusion. A pudendal block does not relieve contraction pain.

Every exam: priority + dose checks
  • Clinical judgment: Recognize cues → analyze → prioritize → plan → act → evaluate. Compare with baseline; reassess the response.
  • Dose safety: Verify patient, allergies, drug, dose, route and time. Use kilograms for weight-based doses; check the safe range and maximum.
  • Calculate: Ordered dose ÷ available dose × volume. Example: 250 mg ÷ 125 mg × 5 mL = 10 mL. Clarify an unsafe or unclear order before giving it.

Rules to remember

  • Match a nutrition problem to a practical food choice.
  • Cervical change distinguishes true labor.
  • Read the timing of decelerations against contractions.

What do I do first?

Nutrition & Health Promotion in Pregnancy

  1. First action
    • Help plan an iron-rich meal with a vitamin C source and review prescribed supplements.
  2. Look for a response
    • The patient identifies a realistic meal and knows when follow-up labs are due.

Labor & Birth

  1. First action
    • Assess contraction pattern, maternal status and fetal response.
  2. Look for a response
    • Progress is documented with cervical change and a reassuring maternal-fetal assessment.

Fetal Monitoring, Comfort & Analgesia in Labor

  1. First action
    • Reposition laterally, assess maternal blood pressure, stop oxytocin if running and notify the team.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess the tracing, including baseline variability and whether the late dips resolve.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • Contraction frequency runs from the start of one contraction to the start of the next. Duration runs from the beginning to the end of one contraction.
  • A baseline fetal heart rate of 110–160 beats/min is generally expected. Always interpret the baseline with variability and decelerations.

Practice and memory cues

Early decelerations mirror contractions. Variable dips suggest cord compression. Late dips follow the contraction peak and can signal impaired placental exchange.

A client at 39 weeks reports a gush of clear fluid. The fetal head is at a minus 2 station. Which action does the nurse take first?

  1. Assess the fetal heart rate
  2. Note the color and odor of the fluid
  3. Test the fluid with nitrazine paper
  4. Take the client's temperature
Check the answer and why

Assess the fetal heart rate

The first action after any rupture of membranes is to check the fetal heart rate , because you are ruling out cord prolapse, and an unengaged head raises that risk. Color, odor, nitrazine testing and temperature all matter, but they come second.

A laboring client on oxytocin has repetitive decelerations that begin after each contraction peaks and return to baseline after the contraction ends. Which action does the nurse take first?

  1. Position the client on her left side
  2. Increase the oxytocin to strengthen contractions
  3. Document the finding and continue monitoring
  4. Perform a cervical examination to check dilation
Check the answer and why

Position the client on her left side

Decelerations that begin after the peak and recover after the contraction are late decelerations from uteroplacental insufficiency , and of these options repositioning is the intrauterine resuscitation measure - alongside stopping the oxytocin, an IV bolus and early notification, matched to the cause rather than run in a fixed order. Oxygen is not routine; it is for maternal hypoxia. Increasing oxytocin worsens it and documentation alone is never sufficient.

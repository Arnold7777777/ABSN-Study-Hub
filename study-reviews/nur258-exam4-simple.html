Part 1: The 7 rules that answer most questions

Treat the cause first. A transfusion (giving blood) is just a band-aid. For example, DIC (the body clotting and then bleeding everywhere) caused by sepsis (an overwhelming infection in the blood) → give antibiotics. A sickle cell crisis triggered by an infection → give antibiotics.

Infection kills first. When all the blood counts are low, infection is more dangerous than bleeding or anemia (low red blood cells), unless the patient is actively bleeding right now.

A fever in a cancer patient or a patient with low WBCs (white blood cells, which fight infection) = call the provider for antibiotics. Not Tylenol. Not putting the precautions sign on the door.

When in doubt, fluids. Sickle cell crisis, polycythemia vera (too many red blood cells), tumor lysis syndrome (cancer cells bursting after chemo), high calcium, multiple myeloma (a bone marrow cancer): fluids are the first move. The one exception: TACO (fluid overload from a transfusion) gets diuretics (water pills that make you pee out fluid), NOT saline.

Clinical side note · TACO: avoid adding a fluid load

Stop the transfusion, sit the patient upright, support oxygenation and administer prescribed diuretics. Maintain necessary IV access according to policy; “no saline” is not an instruction to remove IV access. Source

Low platelets (the cells that plug leaks and stop bleeding) = nothing that pokes, scrapes, or strains. No IM shots (injections into the muscle), no rectal temps, no razors, no flossing, no straining on the toilet.

Expected vs. emergency. An enlarged spleen (the organ under your left ribs that filters old blood cells) is expected in sickle cell, polycythemia vera, and leukemia. A ruptured spleen (BP, or blood pressure, dropping; pain in the left shoulder) is the emergency.

Part 4: Every topic in plain words

Each topic answers three questions: What is it? (in everyday words) · Remember it by (a hook) · Do this (the nursing action).

Blood basics

Your blood has 3 kinds of cells, each with one job.

RBCs (red blood cells) carry oxygen. Hgb (hemoglobin) is the part that actually holds the oxygen.

WBCs (white blood cells) fight infection. Neutrophils are the main infection-fighting WBC.

Platelets plug leaks so you stop bleeding.

They're all made in the bone marrow (the soft "factory" inside your bones).

Pancytopenia = all 3 are low. So think: tired (RBCs), infections (WBCs), bleeding (platelets).

Anemia = not enough working RBCs or Hgb → tired, pale, short of breath, dizzy.

Thrombocytopenia = low platelets. Neutropenia = low neutrophils. ANC (absolute neutrophil count) = how many infection fighters the patient actually has.

MCV = the size of the red cells. Small = iron problem. Big = B12/folate problem. ("Megaloblastic" = mega = big.)

Ferritin = your iron savings account. Low ferritin = iron deficiency.

Petechiae = tiny red pinpoint dots under the skin (small bleeds). Purpura = bigger purple patches (worse bleeding).

The anemias

Iron deficiency

What is it?

Not enough iron to build red cells; usually from slow bleeding (GI tract, heavy periods)

Remember it by

"Pica and the cracked mouth": craving ice or dirt, cracks at the corners of the mouth

Do this

Iron pills between meals + orange juice (vitamin C helps absorption); no milk/antacids with it; black stools are normal

B12 (pernicious anemia)

What is it?

The stomach is missing intrinsic factor (a protein the stomach makes so the gut can absorb B12)

Remember it by

B12 = Brain. It's the only anemia with nerve symptoms (tingling, balance problems, confusion).

Do this

Shots for life. Pills won't work because the gut can't absorb them.

Folate

What is it?

Not enough folate, a B vitamin (from poor diet, alcohol, pregnancy)

Remember it by

Like B12 but no nerve symptoms

Do this

Folic acid pills

Aplastic

What is it?

The bone marrow "factory" shuts down. Not cancer.

Remember it by

A-plastic = no factory. All 3 counts drop.

Do this

Stem cell transplant (new marrow cells from a donor); protect from infection and bleeding

Sickle cell

What is it?

Genetic; red cells bend into crescent shapes, jam the blood vessels, and break apart early

Remember it by

The only anemia with pain + jaundice (yellow skin)

Do this

Fluids first (thin the blood so cells stop jamming), then pain meds, O2, bed rest; avoid cold, dehydration, high altitude

Polycythemia vera is the opposite of anemia: the marrow makes too many red cells, so the blood gets thick like syrup → clots. It is a cancer. Hook: red face, itchy after a hot shower. Do: take blood out (phlebotomy, like donating blood, but it's thrown away), fluids, aspirin.

Blood cancers

Leukemia = the marrow floods with baby (immature) WBCs that don't work. The WBC count looks high, but the patient keeps getting infections, because the cells are useless and they crowd out the RBCs and platelets. Treat it like pancytopenia. On the test, don't worry about which letters (AML, CLL…); answer the general way.

ALL (acute lymphocytic leukemia) is the kids' leukemia, with a good prognosis (expected outcome).

Lymphoma = cancer of the lymph nodes (the infection-filtering glands in your neck, armpits, and groin).

Hodgkin = the "good" one: young people, painless neck lump, spreads in order from one node to the next, curable, Reed-Sternberg cells on biopsy.

Non-Hodgkin = the messy one: older people, spreads randomly, found late, aim for remission (cancer under control, though it may come back).

Multiple myeloma = cancer of plasma cells (antibody-making cells) that eats bone. Calcium leaks out of the bones into the blood and clogs the kidneys. Hook: "Old CRAB": older person with high Calcium, Renal (kidney) failure, Anemia, Bone pain. Do: fluids, fall precautions (bones break easily), bone meds. Never give calcium.

Bleeding & clotting

Low platelets (thrombocytopenia): bruises and petechiae. Bleeding precautions sign at < 50,000. Hook: RANDI (electric Razor, Avoid aspirin, small Needles, Decrease sticks, protect from Injury).

Hemophilia = missing a clotting factor (one of the proteins the blood needs to form a clot). Boys, genetic. Platelets are normal. Bleeds go deep (into joints, the head). Do: give the missing factor; any head bump = CT scan; no contact sports.

von Willebrand disease = hemophilia's milder cousin: men and women, surface bleeding (nose, gums, heavy periods). Do: desmopressin (DDAVP, a medication that boosts the missing clotting protein).

DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation) = "clot everywhere, then bleed everywhere." The name breaks down as: disseminated = all over the body, intravascular = inside the blood vessels, coagulation = clotting.

What happens, step by step:

1. Something huge triggers it, usually sepsis (an overwhelming infection in the bloodstream). Trauma, burns, shock, and childbirth emergencies can also do it.

2. The body goes into emergency mode and flips the "clot" switch everywhere at once, so tiny clots form in vessels all over the body.

3. Those tiny clots block blood flow, and organs like the kidneys, brain, and lungs start getting damaged.

4. Making all those clots uses up the platelets and clotting proteins (like fibrinogen).

5. Nothing is left to stop bleeding, so the patient bleeds from everywhere: IV sites, gums, nose, urine, stool, internally.

Analogy: a town has a limited supply of sandbags. A false alarm makes everyone dump sandbags on every street. The roads get blocked (clots cut off the organs), and when a real leak happens, there are no sandbags left (bleeding).

Labs: platelets and fibrinogen down (used up). PT, PTT, INR up (these measure how long blood takes to clot, and it takes longer because the supplies are gone). D-dimer up (clot breakdown pieces).

Do: fix the cause first (antibiotics for sepsis) → fluids + heparin (stops new clots from using up even more supplies) → blood products last (to restock the supplies).

HIT (heparin-induced thrombocytopenia) = the heparin is wrecking the platelets. The body has an immune reaction to heparin that destroys platelets. Platelets drop while on heparin → stop the heparin.

Heparin vs. warfarin (two blood thinners):

Heparin (IV or shot, works fast) → check PTT (therapeutic, or target, range 60–80 seconds) → antidote Protamine. Hook: "HPTT-P."

Warfarin (Coumadin, a pill for long-term use) → check INR (therapeutic 2–3) → antidote vitamin K. Hook: "Warfarin and K go together."

Blood transfusions

Setup: take vitals before the blood arrives → Y-tubing (tubing shaped like a Y so saline and blood share one line) with normal saline only → stay with the patient for the first 15 min (most reactions happen then) → finish within 4 hr.

Every reaction starts the same way: STOP the blood, run saline on new tubing (the old tubing still has blood in it), stay with the patient, call for help.

Then tell the reactions apart by their one clue:

Hemolytic (wrong blood type; the body bursts the donor's red cells): flank pain + blood in the urine + "I'm going to die" → never restart

Febrile (a reaction to the donor's white cells): just a fever → Tylenol; can restart slowly

Clinical side note · A transfusion reaction needs clinical review

Stop the transfusion and assess airway, breathing and circulation. Notify the provider and blood bank. Do not automatically restart for fever or hives: serious causes must be excluded and restart authorized under local policy. Source

Allergic: itchy hives → Benadryl; can restart slowly if mild

TACO (too much fluid, too fast; the heart can't keep up): high BP + wet lungs (crackles) → diuretics, no saline

TRALI (donor antibodies injure the lungs): can't breathe + low BP, but the lungs aren't "wet" with fluid → airway support; never restart

Bacterial (the blood bag was contaminated): high fever + shock → blood cultures (tests to find bacteria in the blood) + antibiotics; never restart

EPO (erythropoietin, a hormone shot that tells the marrow to make more red cells) works slowly. It's for long-term anemia (kidney disease, chemo), not an emergency bleed. Watch BP and Hgb.

Cancer basics

Benign = stays put, doesn't spread. Malignant = invades and spreads. Metastasis = cancer that has spread to other organs.

Prevention levels:

Primary = don't get it (sunscreen, vaccines, no smoking)

Secondary = screen for it to catch it early (Pap smear, mammogram, colonoscopy)

Tertiary = already have it; stop it from spreading

Staging vs. grading:

Staging = how far it has SPREAD. TNM: Tumor size, lymph Nodes involved, Metastasis. Stages 0–4.

Grading = how WEIRD the cells look under the microscope (1 = still look normal, 4 = unrecognizable).

Higher is worse for both.

Warning signs: CAUTION UP. The big one: unexplained weight LOSS, not gain.

Scope of practice: the provider tells the patient about cancer results, not you.

Surgery types:

Prophylactic (preventive) = remove it before cancer develops (e.g., mastectomy with a strong family history)

Palliative (for comfort) = remove it to ease symptoms, not to cure (e.g., a tumor making it hard to breathe)

Reconstructive = rebuild afterward (e.g., breast implants)

Radiation

External radiation (a beam aimed from outside the body): the patient is NOT radioactive. Don't wash off the skin tattoo (it marks where to aim the beam); avoid sun 10 am–3 pm.

Brachytherapy (radioactive seeds placed inside the body, next to the tumor): the patient IS radioactive. Hook: "Time, Distance, Shielding."

Time: cluster care (do everything in one visit), 30-min visitor limit, rotate nurses

Distance: visitors stay 6 ft away; no pregnant visitors or kids

Shielding: lead apron + thyroid shield (protects the neck) + dosimeter badge (measures how much radiation you've been exposed to)

Seed falls out → don't touch it, use tongs.

At home: strain urine for 7–10 days (to catch seeds), no heavy lifting (straining can push seeds out), sleep apart.

Chemo

Chemo kills all fast-growing cells: cancer, plus hair, mouth lining, gut, and bone marrow. That explains almost every side effect:

Hair → alopecia (hair loss)

Mouth → stomatitis (painful mouth sores): magic mouthwash (a numbing rinse) or salt water, a straw, soft cold foods, no alcohol mouthwash

Gut → nausea/vomiting (the most common): Zofran (an anti-nausea med), small bland meals, no fluids 1 hr before treatment

Marrow → pancytopenia (low WBCs are the most dangerous)

Before giving it: check for blood return (pull back on the syringe and see blood, which proves you're in the vein). Swelling or burning at the site → stop (extravasation).

Port = a small device implanted under the chest skin for giving IV meds long-term.

Protect yourself: purple chemo gloves, gown, face shield. The patient's urine and stool are hazardous too.

Which low count is the question about?

Infection words (fever, visitors, fresh flowers, positive-pressure room, which pushes air out so germs can't drift in) → low WBCs

Clinical side note · Positive-pressure rooms are not routine for every low WBC count

CDC protective-environment recommendations specifically address allogeneic stem-cell transplant recipients. Other patients need individualized infection precautions; an airborne infection can change room requirements. Source

Bleeding words (nosebleed, petechiae, stool softener, occult blood, meaning hidden blood in the stool) → low platelets

Tired/dizzy words (fatigue, orthostatic BP, meaning BP drops when standing up; Hgb) → low RBCs

Stem cell transplant (replacing damaged bone marrow with healthy cells) ≠ cured. The first 8–10 days are the most dangerous (infection and bleeding) while the new cells "take." GVHD (graft-versus-host disease) = the donor's cells attack the patient's body.

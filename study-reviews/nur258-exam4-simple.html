Module 9 · Hematologic Disorders 20 short topics

Module 9

Anemia: oxygen-carrying capacity

  • Recognize: Low hemoglobin reduces oxygen delivery, causing fatigue, pallor, exertional dyspnea or tachycardia; severity depends on onset and the patient's condition.
  • Act: Assess bleeding, perfusion and symptoms; compare CBC indices, reticulocytes and nutrient studies to identify the cause.
  • Remember: Anemia is a finding, not one disease. Treatment differs for blood loss, deficient production and hemolysis; do not apply a transfusion threshold without clinical context.

Module 9

Iron-deficiency anemia

  • Recognize: Small pale red cells, fatigue and pica suggest inadequate iron; chronic blood loss is an important adult cause.
  • Act: Find and address the source of loss and administer prescribed replacement; teach GI effects and correct product use.
  • Remember: Iron treats deficiency but does not explain why it occurred. Dark stools from oral iron differ from suspected gastrointestinal bleeding requiring evaluation.

Module 9

Aplastic anemia

  • Recognize: Bone-marrow failure lowers red cells, white cells and platelets, causing fatigue, infection and bleeding risk.
  • Act: Protect against infection and bleeding, monitor CBC trends and urgently report fever; prepare prescribed transfusion or marrow-directed treatment.
  • Remember: Pancytopenia means all three cell lines are reduced. A low neutrophil count can make infection severe even when local inflammatory signs are subtle.

Module 9

Megaloblastic anemia

  • Recognize: B12 or folate deficiency produces large abnormal cells. B12 deficiency can also cause paresthesias, balance problems and cognitive changes.
  • Act: Check the cause and replace the missing nutrient as prescribed; assess neurologic findings and nutrition/absorption history.
  • Remember: Folate can improve blood counts while untreated B12-related neurologic injury continues. Distinguish the deficiencies rather than choosing replacement from cell size alone.

Module 9

Erythropoietin

  • Recognize: Epoetin stimulates marrow red-cell production in selected anemias; response requires adequate iron and monitoring.
  • Act: Trend hemoglobin and blood pressure, assess iron status and follow individualized dosing.
  • Remember: The goal is to reduce transfusion need in appropriate patients, not normalize every hemoglobin. Excessive targets can increase cardiovascular/thrombotic risk; benefit is delayed rather than immediate rescue.

Module 9

Sickle-cell disease

  • Recognize: Sickled cells hemolyze and obstruct small vessels, causing anemia, jaundice and painful vaso-occlusive episodes.
  • Act: Assess pain, hydration and oxygenation; give prescribed analgesia and fluids. New chest pain, fever or breathing difficulty needs urgent acute-chest evaluation.
  • Remember: Prevent hypoxia, dehydration and infection. Oxygen is indicated for hypoxemia, not automatically for every pain episode; avoid both dehydration and fluid overload.

Module 9

Polycythemia vera

  • Recognize: Excess red cells increase blood viscosity; headache, ruddy color, itching after bathing and thrombosis can occur.
  • Act: Monitor clot/bleeding symptoms and follow prescribed phlebotomy or cytoreductive treatment; support hydration as appropriate.
  • Remember: This is excessive cell production, not simply dehydration. New focal deficits, chest pain or dyspnea require urgent assessment for vascular complications.

Module 9

AML, CML, ALL and CLL

  • Recognize: Leukemia crowds marrow, causing infection, anemia and bleeding despite potentially high WBC. Acute types involve immature blasts; chronic types usually progress more slowly.
  • Act: Trend CBC/differential, assess fever/bleeding and support type-specific diagnostic and treatment plans.
  • Remember: AML is myeloid and often adult; CML involves BCR-ABL; ALL is lymphoid and affects children and adults; CLL generally affects older adults. Names encode lineage and tempo.

Module 9

Hodgkin versus non-Hodgkin lymphoma

  • Recognize: Painless lymphadenopathy, fever, night sweats or weight loss may signal lymphoma.
  • Act: Arrange diagnostic follow-up, monitor treatment-related infection risk and assess symptoms suggesting organ or airway compression.
  • Remember: Hodgkin lymphoma classically has Reed-Sternberg cells and more orderly spread; non-Hodgkin includes many diverse lymphoid cancers and can involve extranodal sites. Tissue diagnosis determines classification.

Module 9

Multiple myeloma

  • Recognize: Malignant plasma cells can cause bone pain/fractures, hypercalcemia, kidney injury, anemia and infection risk.
  • Act: Protect fragile bones, monitor renal function/calcium and maintain prescribed hydration and treatment.
  • Remember: CRAB: Calcium elevation, Renal injury, Anemia, Bone lesions. A monoclonal protein supports evaluation; back pain or weakness needs assessment for fracture or spinal compression.

Module 9

Low platelets: ITP versus HIT

  • Recognize: Thrombocytopenia commonly causes petechiae and mucosal bleeding. ITP is immune platelet destruction; HIT is a heparin-associated immune reaction with major clot risk.
  • Act: Assess bleeding and platelet trend, protect from injury and report suspected HIT promptly for stopping all heparin and prescribed non-heparin therapy.
  • Remember: A falling count on heparin is not just bleeding risk. Treatment depends on the cause, not the number alone.

Module 9

Hemophilia

  • Recognize: Clotting-factor deficiency causes deep tissue or joint bleeding; platelet count may be normal.
  • Act: Protect the affected joint, administer prescribed factor replacement and coordinate invasive procedures with the bleeding-disorders plan.
  • Remember: Hemophilia A involves factor VIII; B involves factor IX. Deep bleeding points toward factor failure, whereas platelet disorders more often cause petechiae and mucosal bleeding.

Module 9

von Willebrand disease

  • Recognize: Reduced or abnormal von Willebrand factor impairs platelet adhesion and factor VIII support, producing nose/gum bleeding, heavy menses or procedural bleeding.
  • Act: Assess bleeding history, avoid unapproved platelet-impairing drugs and follow the prescribed desmopressin/factor plan.
  • Remember: It affects people of any sex and often produces mucosal bleeding. Do not assume a normal platelet count excludes a bleeding disorder.

Module 9

DIC

  • Recognize: Widespread coagulation consumes platelets/factors, causing microvascular clots, organ dysfunction and bleeding such as IV-site oozing.
  • Act: Urgently treat the trigger and monitor perfusion, bleeding, platelets, coagulation tests and fibrinogen; give blood components or anticoagulation only as directed for the presentation.
  • Remember: DIC combines clotting and bleeding. Treatment priorities change with the underlying illness and whether bleeding or thrombosis dominates.

Module 9

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura

  • Recognize: Platelet-rich microthrombi cause thrombocytopenia and hemolytic anemia, with possible neurologic or kidney injury.
  • Act: Escalate urgently for specialist treatment such as plasma exchange; monitor organ function and bleeding.
  • Remember: Do not wait for the full classic symptom pentad. TTP differs from DIC and HIT; routine platelet transfusion is generally avoided unless a specialist identifies a critical indication.

Module 9

Blood products and compatibility

  • Recognize: Packed red cells improve oxygen-carrying capacity; platelets support the initial plug; fresh frozen plasma replaces clotting factors.
  • Act: Verify patient/product identity, prescription, compatibility and baseline status; use the facility administration/monitoring procedure.
  • Remember: Compatibility depends on the component: red-cell antigens versus plasma antibodies. Blood products treat specific deficits; do not use one universal donor/recipient rule for every product.

Module 9

Early transfusion-reaction clues

  • Recognize: Acute hemolysis can cause fever, flank pain, hypotension or dark urine. Febrile reactions cause fever/rigors; allergic reactions often cause hives/itching.
  • Act: For any suspected reaction, stop the transfusion, assess ABCs, maintain access per protocol and notify the provider/transfusion service.
  • Remember: Fever is not automatically benign: hemolysis and contamination must be excluded. Never restart a suspect unit without explicit direction.

Module 9

TACO

  • Recognize: Transfusion-associated circulatory overload causes respiratory distress with signs of excess volume, often hypertension, crackles and neck-vein distention.
  • Act: Stop the transfusion, sit upright as appropriate, support oxygenation and give ordered overload treatment; monitor fluid balance.
  • Remember: TACO is a volume problem. Slower/smaller-volume transfusion plans may help susceptible patients; avoid reflexively giving a fluid bolus for every transfusion reaction.

Module 9

TRALI

  • Recognize: Transfusion-related acute lung injury causes acute hypoxemia and bilateral pulmonary edema without circulatory overload; hypotension can occur.
  • Act: Stop the transfusion, summon help and provide respiratory/hemodynamic support according to the reaction protocol.
  • Remember: Both TRALI and TACO can produce wet lungs. Look for evidence of overload rather than deciding solely from crackles; diuresis is not a universal reaction treatment.

Module 9

Bacterial transfusion reaction

  • Recognize: High fever, rigors, hypotension or shock during/after transfusion can indicate a contaminated component.
  • Act: Stop the transfusion, support ABCs and notify the provider/blood bank; prepare cultures of the patient/component and prescribed urgent antibiotics.
  • Remember: Severe fever is not automatically a simple febrile reaction. Preserve the bag/tubing for investigation according to facility policy.

Module 10

Cancer basics, diagnosis and staging

  • Recognize: Carcinogenic exposures and genetic changes promote abnormal growth. Persistent lumps, bleeding, weight loss or other changes require evaluation; biopsy establishes many diagnoses.
  • Act: Assess warning signs, support diagnostic testing and coordinate individualized surgery, systemic treatment, radiation and symptom care.
  • Remember: Stage describes extent/spread; grade describes microscopic abnormality. Treatment goals may be curative, controlling or palliative; cancer is not one uniform disease.

Module 10

Cancer types and immune surveillance

  • Recognize: Immune surveillance can remove abnormal cells, but tumors can evade it. Carcinoma is epithelial, sarcoma connective tissue, leukemia blood-forming, lymphoma lymphoid and myeloma plasma-cell disease.
  • Act: Support type-specific treatment and survivorship monitoring, rehabilitation and recurrence prevention.
  • Remember: Tertiary prevention reduces consequences after disease exists. Immunotherapy redirects immune activity and has distinct adverse effects; it is not interchangeable with cytotoxic chemotherapy.

Module 10

Primary versus secondary prevention

  • Recognize: Primary prevention reduces cancer risk before disease: avoid tobacco, protect skin and use indicated vaccination. Secondary prevention detects disease earlier through appropriate screening.
  • Act: Match screening to age, history, risk and current guidelines; help complete abnormal-result follow-up.
  • Remember: Screening examines people without symptoms; diagnostic testing investigates a finding. An abnormal screen is not itself a confirmed cancer diagnosis.

Module 10

External radiation versus brachytherapy

  • Recognize: Radiation injury depends on the treated tissue and can cause local skin/mucosal effects and fatigue. External-beam treatment does not make the person radioactive; some internal sources require precautions.
  • Act: Follow the specific source's time, distance, shielding and visitor rules; protect treated skin and report toxicity.
  • Remember: For a displaced sealed source, restrict access and notify radiation safety; never pick it up by hand. Restrictions depend on the actual treatment.

Module 10

Chemotherapy administration and staff protection

  • Recognize: Cytotoxic therapy affects rapidly dividing cancer and normal cells; adverse effects vary by agent and dose.
  • Act: Verify regimen, access, labs and required checks; use hazardous-drug PPE/handling and disposal procedures, and assess patient-specific toxicities.
  • Remember: Precautions include exposure during preparation, administration, waste and body-fluid handling according to protocol. Do not assume all anticancer drugs share the same route, timing or toxicity.

Module 10

Chemotherapy extravasation

  • Recognize: Burning, pain, swelling or altered infusion flow can signal drug leaking into tissue; vesicants can cause major injury.
  • Act: Stop infusion immediately, keep access available for aspiration/antidote per protocol and notify the treatment team; do not flush the suspected site.
  • Remember: Antidote and warm/cold compress choice depend on the drug. Follow the agent-specific extravasation plan rather than applying one compress rule to every medication.

Module 10

Nausea, mouth injury and cancer nutrition

  • Recognize: Chemotherapy-related nausea/vomiting and mucositis/stomatitis can reduce intake and damage the oral barrier.
  • Act: Give prescribed preventive antiemetics, provide gentle oral care and bland rinses, manage pain and adapt foods/fluids to tolerance.
  • Remember: Monitor dehydration, weight and infection. Avoid irritating alcohol-containing rinses or abrasive foods when mucosa is injured; nutrition support should fit symptoms and the overall care plan.

Module 10

Bone-marrow suppression

  • Recognize: Low neutrophils increase infection risk, low platelets bleeding risk and low red cells fatigue/dyspnea.
  • Act: Trend CBC/differential, teach urgent fever reporting and appropriate infection/bleeding precautions; assess activity tolerance and prescribed support.
  • Remember: A high total WBC does not ensure functional immunity. Positive-pressure rooms are not routine for every low white count; protective environments have specific indications.

Module 10

Chemotherapy organ toxicities

  • Recognize: Renal injury changes urine/kidney labs; neurotoxicity/peripheral neuropathy causes numbness or weakness; cardiopulmonary injury can cause dyspnea/chest symptoms. Reproductive effects include fertility and pregnancy risks; fatigue is common.
  • Act: Trend agent-specific monitoring, assess falls and function, report new symptoms and teach contraception/fertility guidance before therapy.
  • Remember: Do not dismiss breathlessness as ordinary fatigue. Toxicity monitoring depends on the actual drug and cumulative exposure.

Module 10

Cancer pain

  • Recognize: Cancer pain may be somatic, visceral or neuropathic and may include breakthrough episodes despite baseline treatment.
  • Act: Assess intensity, function and patient goals; use scheduled and breakthrough medication as ordered, with nonpharmacologic support and reassessment.
  • Remember: Monitor sedation, respirations, constipation and other adverse effects. Effective pain relief is individualized; opioid tolerance does not eliminate monitoring needs or justify withholding relief.

Module 10

Hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation

  • Recognize: Conditioning and delayed marrow recovery create infection, bleeding and organ-toxicity risks. Allogeneic transplant adds graft-versus-host disease risk.
  • Act: Monitor engraftment/counts, temperature, mucosa, skin, GI and liver findings; follow transplant-specific precautions and immunosuppressive therapy.
  • Remember: Autologous uses the patient's cells; allogeneic uses donor cells. New rash, diarrhea or jaundice after donor transplant needs prompt evaluation.

Module 10

Four oncologic emergencies

  • Recognize: SVC: face/neck swelling and dyspnea. Tumor lysis: high potassium/phosphate/uric acid and low calcium. Spinal compression: new back pain, weakness or bladder/bowel changes. Hypercalcemia: weakness, confusion and dehydration.
  • Act: Escalate immediately; support ABCs and prepare ordered targeted treatment, ECG/lab monitoring or urgent imaging.
  • Remember: The pattern identifies the threat. New neurologic deficits or breathing compromise cannot wait for a routine oncology appointment.

Palliative, hospice and end-of-life care

  • Recognize: Serious illness creates physical, emotional, social and spiritual needs alongside disease management.
  • Act: Ask what matters to the patient, treat distressing symptoms and support family/communication and advance-care planning.

Part 1: The 7 rules that answer most questions

Treat the cause first. A transfusion (giving blood) is just a band-aid. For example, DIC (the body clotting and then bleeding everywhere) caused by sepsis (an overwhelming infection in the blood) → give antibiotics. A sickle cell crisis triggered by an infection → give antibiotics.
ABO compatibility chart: which FFP or red cells can this patient receive? ↗

Infection kills first. When all the blood counts are low, infection is more dangerous than bleeding or anemia (low red blood cells), unless the patient is actively bleeding right now.

A fever in a cancer patient or a patient with low WBCs (white blood cells, which fight infection) = call the provider for antibiotics. Not Tylenol. Not putting the precautions sign on the door.

When in doubt, fluids. Sickle cell crisis, polycythemia vera (too many red blood cells), tumor lysis syndrome (cancer cells bursting after chemo), high calcium, multiple myeloma (a bone marrow cancer): fluids are the first move. The one exception: TACO (fluid overload from a transfusion) gets diuretics (water pills that make you pee out fluid), NOT saline.

Clinical side note · TACO: avoid adding a fluid load

Stop the transfusion, sit the patient upright, support oxygenation and administer prescribed diuretics. Maintain necessary IV access according to policy; “no saline” is not an instruction to remove IV access. Source

Low platelets (the cells that plug leaks and stop bleeding) = nothing that pokes, scrapes, or strains. No IM shots (injections into the muscle), no rectal temps, no razors, no flossing, no straining on the toilet.

Expected vs. emergency. An enlarged spleen (the organ under your left ribs that filters old blood cells) is expected in sickle cell, polycythemia vera, and leukemia. A ruptured spleen (BP, or blood pressure, dropping; pain in the left shoulder) is the emergency.

  2. then precautions

First → then
  1. IV fluids
  2. pain meds
  3. O2 (oxygen)

  5. transfuse if Hgb < 7.

Bed rest.

DIC

First → then
  1. Treat the cause (antibiotics for sepsis)
  2. fluids/O2/pressors (IV meds that raise blood pressure) + heparin
  3. blood products last

Clinical side note · DIC: the sequence depends on bleeding and thrombosis

Treat the cause promptly. Heparin is considered when thrombosis predominates; it is not a universal step before blood products. Active bleeding may require urgent platelets, plasma or fibrinogen replacement while the cause is treated. Source

Part 4: Every topic in plain words

Each topic answers three questions: What is it? (in everyday words) · Remember it by (a hook) · Do this (the nursing action).

Blood basics

Your blood has 3 kinds of cells, each with one job.

  • RBCs (red blood cells) carry oxygen. Hgb (hemoglobin) is the part that actually holds the oxygen.
  • WBCs (white blood cells) fight infection. Neutrophils are the main infection-fighting WBC.
  • Platelets plug leaks so you stop bleeding.

They're all made in the bone marrow (the soft "factory" inside your bones).

Pancytopenia = all 3 are low. So think: tired (RBCs), infections (WBCs), bleeding (platelets).

Anemia = not enough working RBCs or Hgb → tired, pale, short of breath, dizzy.

Thrombocytopenia = low platelets. Neutropenia = low neutrophils. ANC (absolute neutrophil count) = how many infection fighters the patient actually has.

MCV = the size of the red cells. Small = iron problem. Big = B12/folate problem. ("Megaloblastic" = mega = big.)

Ferritin = your iron savings account. Low ferritin = iron deficiency.

Petechiae = tiny red pinpoint dots under the skin (small bleeds). Purpura = bigger purple patches (worse bleeding).

The anemias

Iron deficiency

What is it?

Not enough iron to build red cells; usually from slow bleeding (GI tract, heavy periods)

Remember it by

"Pica and the cracked mouth": craving ice or dirt, cracks at the corners of the mouth

Do this

Iron pills between meals + orange juice (vitamin C helps absorption); no milk/antacids with it; black stools are normal

B12 (pernicious anemia)

What is it?

The stomach is missing intrinsic factor (a protein the stomach makes so the gut can absorb B12)

Remember it by

B12 = Brain. It's the only anemia with nerve symptoms (tingling, balance problems, confusion).

Do this

Shots for life. Pills won't work because the gut can't absorb them.

Folate

What is it?

Not enough folate, a B vitamin (from poor diet, alcohol, pregnancy)

Remember it by

Like B12 but no nerve symptoms

Do this

Folic acid pills

Aplastic

What is it?

The bone marrow "factory" shuts down. Not cancer.

Remember it by

A-plastic = no factory. All 3 counts drop.

Do this

Stem cell transplant (new marrow cells from a donor); protect from infection and bleeding

Sickle cell

What is it?

Genetic; red cells bend into crescent shapes, jam the blood vessels, and break apart early

Remember it by

The only anemia with pain + jaundice (yellow skin)

Do this

Fluids first (thin the blood so cells stop jamming), then pain meds, O2, bed rest; avoid cold, dehydration, high altitude

Polycythemia vera is the opposite of anemia: the marrow makes too many red cells, so the blood gets thick like syrup → clots. It is a cancer. Hook: red face, itchy after a hot shower. Do: take blood out (phlebotomy, like donating blood, but it's thrown away), fluids, aspirin.

Blood cancers

Leukemia = the marrow floods with baby (immature) WBCs that don't work. The WBC count looks high, but the patient keeps getting infections, because the cells are useless and they crowd out the RBCs and platelets. Treat it like pancytopenia. On the test, don't worry about which letters (AML, CLL…); answer the general way.

ALL (acute lymphocytic leukemia) is the kids' leukemia, with a good prognosis (expected outcome).

Lymphoma = cancer of the lymph nodes (the infection-filtering glands in your neck, armpits, and groin).

  • Hodgkin = the "good" one: young people, painless neck lump, spreads in order from one node to the next, curable, Reed-Sternberg cells on biopsy.
  • Non-Hodgkin = the messy one: older people, spreads randomly, found late, aim for remission (cancer under control, though it may come back).

Multiple myeloma = cancer of plasma cells (antibody-making cells) that eats bone. Calcium leaks out of the bones into the blood and clogs the kidneys. Hook: "Old CRAB": older person with high Calcium, Renal (kidney) failure, Anemia, Bone pain. Do: fluids, fall precautions (bones break easily), bone meds. Never give calcium.

Bleeding & clotting

Low platelets (thrombocytopenia): bruises and petechiae. Bleeding precautions sign at < 50,000. Hook: RANDI (electric Razor, Avoid aspirin, small Needles, Decrease sticks, protect from Injury).

Hemophilia = missing a clotting factor (one of the proteins the blood needs to form a clot). Boys, genetic. Platelets are normal. Bleeds go deep (into joints, the head). Do: give the missing factor; any head bump = CT scan; no contact sports.

von Willebrand disease = hemophilia's milder cousin: men and women, surface bleeding (nose, gums, heavy periods). Do: desmopressin (DDAVP, a medication that boosts the missing clotting protein).

DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation) = "clot everywhere, then bleed everywhere." The name breaks down as: disseminated = all over the body, intravascular = inside the blood vessels, coagulation = clotting.

  • What happens, step by step:
    1. Something huge triggers it, usually sepsis (an overwhelming infection in the bloodstream). Trauma, burns, shock, and childbirth emergencies can also do it.
    2. The body goes into emergency mode and flips the "clot" switch everywhere at once, so tiny clots form in vessels all over the body.
    3. Those tiny clots block blood flow, and organs like the kidneys, brain, and lungs start getting damaged.
    4. Making all those clots uses up the platelets and clotting proteins (like fibrinogen).
    5. Nothing is left to stop bleeding, so the patient bleeds from everywhere: IV sites, gums, nose, urine, stool, internally.
  • Analogy: a town has a limited supply of sandbags. A false alarm makes everyone dump sandbags on every street. The roads get blocked (clots cut off the organs), and when a real leak happens, there are no sandbags left (bleeding).
  • Labs: platelets and fibrinogen down (used up). PT, PTT, INR up (these measure how long blood takes to clot, and it takes longer because the supplies are gone). D-dimer up (clot breakdown pieces).
  • Do: fix the cause first (antibiotics for sepsis) → fluids + heparin (stops new clots from using up even more supplies) → blood products last (to restock the supplies).

HIT (heparin-induced thrombocytopenia) = the heparin is wrecking the platelets. The body has an immune reaction to heparin that destroys platelets. Platelets drop while on heparin → stop the heparin.

Heparin vs. warfarin (two blood thinners):

  • Heparin (IV or shot, works fast) → check PTT (therapeutic, or target, range 60–80 seconds) → antidote Protamine. Hook: "HPTT-P."
  • Warfarin (Coumadin, a pill for long-term use) → check INR (therapeutic 2–3) → antidote vitamin K. Hook: "Warfarin and K go together."

Blood transfusions

Setup: take vitals before the blood arrives → Y-tubing (tubing shaped like a Y so saline and blood share one line) with normal saline only → stay with the patient for the first 15 min (most reactions happen then) → finish within 4 hr.

Every reaction starts the same way: STOP the blood, run saline on new tubing (the old tubing still has blood in it), stay with the patient, call for help.

Then tell the reactions apart by their one clue:

  • Hemolytic (wrong blood type; the body bursts the donor's red cells): flank pain + blood in the urine + "I'm going to die" → never restart
  • Febrile (a reaction to the donor's white cells): just a fever → Tylenol; can restart slowly

    Clinical side note · A transfusion reaction needs clinical review

    Stop the transfusion and assess airway, breathing and circulation. Notify the provider and blood bank. Do not automatically restart for fever or hives: serious causes must be excluded and restart authorized under local policy. Source

  • Allergic: itchy hives → Benadryl; can restart slowly if mild
  • TACO (too much fluid, too fast; the heart can't keep up): high BP + wet lungs (crackles) → diuretics, no saline
  • TRALI (donor antibodies injure the lungs): can't breathe + low BP, but the lungs aren't "wet" with fluid → airway support; never restart
  • Bacterial (the blood bag was contaminated): high fever + shock → blood cultures (tests to find bacteria in the blood) + antibiotics; never restart

EPO (erythropoietin, a hormone shot that tells the marrow to make more red cells) works slowly. It's for long-term anemia (kidney disease, chemo), not an emergency bleed. Watch BP and Hgb.

Cancer basics

Benign = stays put, doesn't spread. Malignant = invades and spreads. Metastasis = cancer that has spread to other organs.

Prevention levels:

  • Primary = don't get it (sunscreen, vaccines, no smoking)
  • Secondary = screen for it to catch it early (Pap smear, mammogram, colonoscopy)
  • Tertiary = already have it; stop it from spreading

Staging vs. grading:

  • Staging = how far it has SPREAD. TNM: Tumor size, lymph Nodes involved, Metastasis. Stages 0–4.
  • Grading = how WEIRD the cells look under the microscope (1 = still look normal, 4 = unrecognizable).
  • Higher is worse for both.

Warning signs: CAUTION UP. The big one: unexplained weight LOSS, not gain.

Scope of practice: the provider tells the patient about cancer results, not you.

Surgery types:

  • Prophylactic (preventive) = remove it before cancer develops (e.g., mastectomy with a strong family history)
  • Palliative (for comfort) = remove it to ease symptoms, not to cure (e.g., a tumor making it hard to breathe)
  • Reconstructive = rebuild afterward (e.g., breast implants)

Radiation

External radiation (a beam aimed from outside the body): the patient is NOT radioactive. Don't wash off the skin tattoo (it marks where to aim the beam); avoid sun 10 am–3 pm.

Brachytherapy (radioactive seeds placed inside the body, next to the tumor): the patient IS radioactive. Hook: "Time, Distance, Shielding."

  • Time: cluster care (do everything in one visit), 30-min visitor limit, rotate nurses
  • Distance: visitors stay 6 ft away; no pregnant visitors or kids
  • Shielding: lead apron + thyroid shield (protects the neck) + dosimeter badge (measures how much radiation you've been exposed to)
  • Seed falls out → don't touch it, use tongs.
  • At home: strain urine for 7–10 days (to catch seeds), no heavy lifting (straining can push seeds out), sleep apart.

Chemo

Chemo kills all fast-growing cells: cancer, plus hair, mouth lining, gut, and bone marrow. That explains almost every side effect:

  • Hair → alopecia (hair loss)
  • Mouth → stomatitis (painful mouth sores): magic mouthwash (a numbing rinse) or salt water, a straw, soft cold foods, no alcohol mouthwash
  • Gut → nausea/vomiting (the most common): Zofran (an anti-nausea med), small bland meals, no fluids 1 hr before treatment
  • Marrow → pancytopenia (low WBCs are the most dangerous)

Before giving it: check for blood return (pull back on the syringe and see blood, which proves you're in the vein). Swelling or burning at the site → stop (extravasation).

Port = a small device implanted under the chest skin for giving IV meds long-term.

Protect yourself: purple chemo gloves, gown, face shield. The patient's urine and stool are hazardous too.

Which low count is the question about?

  • Infection words (fever, visitors, fresh flowers, positive-pressure room, which pushes air out so germs can't drift in) → low WBCs

    Clinical side note · Positive-pressure rooms are not routine for every low WBC count

    CDC protective-environment recommendations specifically address allogeneic stem-cell transplant recipients. Other patients need individualized infection precautions; an airborne infection can change room requirements. Source

  • Bleeding words (nosebleed, petechiae, stool softener, occult blood, meaning hidden blood in the stool) → low platelets
  • Tired/dizzy words (fatigue, orthostatic BP, meaning BP drops when standing up; Hgb) → low RBCs

Stem cell transplant (replacing damaged bone marrow with healthy cells) ≠ cured. The first 8–10 days are the most dangerous (infection and bleeding) while the new cells "take." GVHD (graft-versus-host disease) = the donor's cells attack the patient's body.

Transfusion reactions

TACO = fluid overload from a transfusion

  • T = Transfusion
  • A = Associated
  • C = Circulatory
  • O = Overload
  • In plain words: too much blood went in too fast, so fluid backs up into the lungs. BP goes UP → give diuretics, no saline.

TRALI = lung injury from a transfusion

  • T = Transfusion
  • R = Related
  • A = Acute (sudden)
  • L = Lung
  • I = Injury
  • In plain words: antibodies in the donor's blood attack the patient's lungs. BP goes DOWN → airway support; never restart.

Module 7

Neurologic diagnostic studies

  • Recognize: CT identifies acute structural changes; CT angiography examines vessels with iodinated contrast; PET evaluates metabolic activity.
  • Act: Assess contrast history, kidney considerations and procedure preparation; monitor puncture sites or sedation effects when relevant.
  • Remember: Match each test to its purpose. A vascular image, functional metabolic study and bedside neurologic examination answer different questions and complement rather than replace each other.

Module 7

Lumbar puncture and MRI

  • Recognize: Lumbar puncture samples CSF; MRI offers detailed structural imaging using a strong magnetic field.
  • Act: Before LP, review bleeding risk and possible increased-ICP contraindications; afterward monitor headache, leakage and neurologic changes. Before MRI, screen implants/metal and follow device-specific safety rules.
  • Remember: MRI does not use ionizing radiation. Do not assume every implant is safe or prohibited without verifying its exact compatibility.

Module 7

EEG

  • Recognize: An EEG records electrical brain activity and helps classify suspected seizures; a normal interictal tracing does not exclude epilepsy.
  • Act: Prepare according to test instructions and document observed events, medications and possible triggers; protect the patient if a seizure occurs.
  • Remember: EEG studies function rather than brain anatomy. Correlate the tracing with the clinical episode, not the test result alone.

Module 7

Changes in neurologic status

  • Recognize: New confusion, reduced alertness, pupil changes, weakness or speech changes can signal deterioration.
  • Act: Compare with baseline, assess ABCs and glucose, perform focused neurologic checks and report new deficits promptly.
  • Remember: Trend matters: a changing level of consciousness may precede dramatic vital-sign changes. Document onset/last-known-well and avoid assuming sleepiness is simply fatigue or medication effect.

Module 7

Increased intracranial pressure

  • Recognize: Declining consciousness, headache, vomiting or pupillary changes suggest raised ICP; late findings can include abnormal posturing and Cushing-pattern vital signs.
  • Act: Maintain oxygenation and perfusion, elevate/align the head as appropriate, avoid obstructing venous return and give ordered ICP therapy.
  • Remember: Prevent secondary injury from hypoxia and hypotension. Do not wait for late bradycardia or abnormal respirations before escalating neurologic deterioration.

Module 7

Head-trauma priorities

  • Recognize: Trauma can cause bleeding, edema and evolving neurologic deficits even after an initially reassuring assessment.
  • Act: Protect cervical alignment when indicated, support oxygenation/perfusion and perform serial neurologic assessments; promptly escalate deterioration.
  • Remember: The primary injury has occurred; nursing care aims to prevent secondary injury. Watch consciousness, pupils, motor responses and new vomiting rather than relying only on external wounds.

Module 7

Seizures and status epilepticus

  • Recognize: Observe onset, movements, awareness and recovery. A seizure lasting 5 minutes or recurrent seizures without recovery is an emergency.
  • Act: Time the event, protect from injury, support airway and give prescribed rescue treatment; position safely when possible.
  • Remember: Do not restrain the patient or place objects in the mouth. Afterward assess breathing, injury, glucose and neurologic recovery, and document the sequence.

Module 7

Migraine

  • Recognize: Migraine often causes episodic throbbing pain with nausea and light/sound sensitivity, sometimes preceded by aura.
  • Act: Reduce stimulation and give prescribed acute treatment early; identify triggers and prevention needs.
  • Remember: A thunderclap headache, fever/stiff neck, new focal deficit or a major change from the usual pattern needs urgent evaluation. Do not label every severe headache a routine migraine.

Module 7

Transient ischemic attack

  • Recognize: Transient focal weakness, speech or vision changes can reflect temporary cerebral ischemia even after symptoms resolve.
  • Act: Treat as urgent stroke evaluation: note onset, assess deficits and help complete the prescribed vascular/cardiac workup.
  • Remember: Symptom resolution does not remove the near-term stroke risk. TIA is a warning requiring prevention and evaluation, not reassurance alone.

Module 7

Ischemic stroke

  • Recognize: Sudden facial droop, arm weakness, speech change or other focal deficit suggests interrupted cerebral blood flow.
  • Act: Activate the stroke pathway, establish last-known-well, check glucose and obtain urgent imaging; protect airway and keep NPO until swallowing is assessed.
  • Remember: Reperfusion eligibility depends on imaging, timing and contraindications. Do not give oral medications, food or drink before swallowing safety is established.

Module 7

Hemorrhagic stroke

  • Recognize: Acute neurologic deficit with severe headache, vomiting or falling consciousness can indicate intracranial bleeding; aneurysmal rupture may cause thunderclap headache.
  • Act: Escalate urgently, support airway/perfusion, control blood pressure as ordered and monitor for rising ICP.
  • Remember: Brain imaging distinguishes hemorrhage from ischemia. Thrombolytic or anticoagulant treatment for presumed ischemia can worsen bleeding if hemorrhage has not been excluded.

Module 8

Traumatic brain injury patterns

  • Recognize: Concussion alters brain function. Epidural bleeding can deteriorate rapidly after a lucid interval; subdural bleeding may evolve more slowly; intracerebral bleeding occurs within brain tissue.
  • Act: Protect oxygenation, blood pressure and cervical alignment; repeatedly assess consciousness, pupils and motor findings.
  • Remember: A small external injury or brief initial recovery does not exclude dangerous intracranial bleeding. Prevent secondary injury from hypoxia and hypotension.

Module 8

Primary and metastatic brain tumors

  • Recognize: Primary tumors originate in/near the brain; secondary tumors metastasize from elsewhere. Either can cause seizures, focal deficits or increased ICP.
  • Act: Monitor neurologic changes and implement ordered seizure/edema therapy; after surgery follow location-specific positioning and monitor for deterioration.
  • Remember: Benign does not mean safe inside the skull. Mass, edema and blocked CSF can threaten function regardless of malignancy.

Module 8

Diffuse axonal injury and contusion

  • Recognize: Contusion is bruised brain tissue with focal bleeding/edema. Diffuse axonal injury results from shearing during acceleration/deceleration and may cause prolonged impaired consciousness.
  • Act: Support oxygenation/perfusion, monitor serial neurologic findings and prevent secondary injury.
  • Remember: These differ from a simple concussion. Initial imaging may underestimate diffuse injury, so clinical assessment and trajectory remain essential.

Module 8

Brain death

  • Recognize: Brain death is permanent loss of all brain function, including brainstem function, determined through a formal clinical process.
  • Act: Maintain prescribed physiologic support and clear family communication while qualified clinicians perform required evaluation.
  • Remember: Exclude reversible confounders such as sedatives, hypothermia and metabolic disturbance. Brain death is distinct from coma or a vegetative state; do not infer it from unresponsiveness alone.

Module 8

Spinal cord injury and autonomic emergencies

  • Recognize: Cord injury can impair movement, sensation, breathing and autonomic function. Spinal shock causes transient flaccidity; neurogenic shock causes hypotension/bradycardia; autonomic dysreflexia causes sudden hypertension after susceptible injury.
  • Act: Protect alignment, ventilation and perfusion. For dysreflexia, sit upright, monitor BP and identify triggers, especially bladder distention.
  • Remember: These three conditions are different. A high cord injury creates respiratory and autonomic risks beyond limb weakness.

Module 8

Meningitis versus encephalitis

  • Recognize: Meningitis often causes fever, headache and neck stiffness; encephalitis more directly affects brain function, causing altered behavior, consciousness or seizures.
  • Act: Apply indicated precautions, rapidly escalate neurologic/infection findings and give prescribed antimicrobials/antivirals with supportive care.
  • Remember: Do not delay urgent treatment while awaiting every diagnostic result. Monitor airway, seizures, perfusion and ICP; the organism determines transmission precautions.

Module 8

Brain abscess

  • Recognize: Localized infection can cause headache, fever, seizures, focal deficits and increased ICP; the full classic pattern may be absent.
  • Act: Arrange urgent evaluation and prescribed antimicrobial or surgical treatment, with seizure and neurologic monitoring.
  • Remember: An intracranial mass changes diagnostic safety. Lumbar puncture may be unsafe when mass effect or raised ICP is suspected; follow the treating team's plan.

Module 8

Multiple sclerosis

  • Recognize: CNS demyelination can cause fluctuating visual, sensory, motor and coordination problems with fatigue.
  • Act: Balance rest and activity, manage heat sensitivity and mobility risks, and support prescribed relapse/disease-modifying treatment.
  • Remember: Symptoms vary by lesion location and disease course. Avoid overheating and teach strategies that conserve energy without unnecessary long-term immobility.

Module 8

Myasthenia gravis

  • Recognize: Fatigable weakness, ptosis, diplopia and difficulty swallowing suggest impaired neuromuscular transmission; weakness worsens with use.
  • Act: Assess breathing and swallowing, schedule medication and meals to optimize strength, and urgently escalate respiratory decline.
  • Remember: A myasthenic crisis threatens ventilation. Do not attribute new dyspnea to ordinary fatigue; medication adherence and avoiding triggers help prevent deterioration.

Module 8

Guillain-Barré syndrome

  • Recognize: Acute progressive weakness, often ascending with reduced reflexes after infection, can involve respiratory and autonomic muscles.
  • Act: Closely monitor respiratory function, swallowing, BP and rhythm; prepare ventilatory support and administer prescribed IVIG or plasma exchange.
  • Remember: Weakness can progress despite an initially normal oxygen saturation. Prevent immobility complications while monitoring for autonomic instability.

Module 8

Parkinson disease

  • Recognize: Bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor and postural instability reflect reduced dopamine activity; swallowing and autonomic problems can occur.
  • Act: Give medicines on schedule, support safe mobility, assess swallowing and prevent falls/aspiration.
  • Remember: Timing is part of treatment. Avoid abrupt interruption of antiparkinson medication; use deliberate movements and individualized exercise/communication strategies.

Module 8

Bell palsy

  • Recognize: Acute peripheral facial-nerve palsy weakens one side of the face, including forehead movement and eye closure.
  • Act: Protect the exposed cornea with prescribed lubrication/closure measures and arrange prompt evaluation and treatment.
  • Remember: New facial weakness still requires stroke assessment. Bell palsy is a peripheral diagnosis; do not dismiss associated limb weakness, speech changes or other neurologic deficits.

Module 8

Huntington disease

  • Recognize: Inherited neurodegeneration causes involuntary choreiform movements, cognitive decline and psychiatric changes.
  • Act: Support nutrition, swallowing safety, fall prevention and behavioral needs; include family and genetic counseling resources.
  • Remember: Movement, cognition and mood all require assessment. Progressive disability changes supervision and communication needs; preserve dignity and function as long as possible.

Module 8

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis

  • Recognize: Progressive motor-neuron loss causes weakness, muscle wasting, dysarthria and dysphagia, with increasing respiratory risk.
  • Act: Monitor breathing/cough strength and swallowing; support assistive communication, nutrition, mobility and advance-care planning.
  • Remember: Sensory loss is not the defining feature. Cognition is often preserved but cognitive/behavioral changes can occur; do not assume inability to speak means inability to participate.

Module 8

Alzheimer disease

  • Recognize: Progressive memory and functional decline differs from delirium's acute, fluctuating inattention.
  • Act: Use consistent routines, simple cues and safety supports; assess nutrition, wandering and caregiver strain.
  • Remember: A sudden change in someone with dementia needs evaluation for delirium, infection, medication effects or another reversible cause. Do not attribute every acute decline to Alzheimer progression.

Module 8

Degenerative disc disease

  • Recognize: Disc degeneration or herniation can cause back/neck pain with radiating pain, numbness or weakness.
  • Act: Assess motor/sensory function and promote prescribed positioning, activity, analgesia and rehabilitation; after surgery use ordered body mechanics.
  • Remember: New urinary retention, saddle numbness or progressive weakness suggests cauda equina/cord compromise and needs urgent evaluation. Avoid routine prolonged bed rest unless specifically indicated.

Module 8

Basilar skull fracture and CSF leakage

  • Recognize: Periorbital bruising, mastoid bruising or clear ear/nose drainage after head injury can suggest a basilar skull fracture.
  • Act: Protect airway and cervical alignment as indicated; avoid nasal instrumentation and report suspected CSF leakage.
  • Remember: Course recall: a halo/ring pattern and glucose in drainage are clues to possible CSF. Neither alone confirms it; follow definitive evaluation and infection precautions.

Every exam: priority + dose checks
  • Clinical judgment: Recognize cues → analyze → prioritize → plan → act → evaluate. Compare with baseline; reassess the response.
  • Dose safety: Verify patient, allergies, drug, dose, route and time. Use kilograms for weight-based doses; check the safe range and maximum.
  • Calculate: Ordered dose ÷ available dose × volume. Example: 250 mg ÷ 125 mg × 5 mL = 10 mL. Clarify an unsafe or unclear order before giving it.

Rules to remember

  • Sudden focal changes start the stroke clock.
  • Hypertension and headache after a high spinal cord injury are urgent.

Think…

What do I do first?

Neurologic Dysfunction & Cerebrovascular Disorders

  1. First action
    • Activate the stroke pathway, establish last known well and check glucose.
    • Prepare for urgent brain imaging.
  2. Look for a response
    • Repeat neurologic assessments and keep the patient NPO until swallowing safety is assessed.

Traumatic, Infectious, Oncologic & Degenerative Neuro

  1. First action
    • Sit upright, loosen constrictive items, recheck BP frequently and inspect the catheter or bladder first.
    • Follow the emergency protocol.
  2. Look for a response
    • Confirm blood pressure moves back toward the patient's baseline as the trigger is relieved.

Compare the neurologic cues

Fluctuating double vision, drooping lids or weakness that worsens with use
  • Think myasthenia gravis.
  • A weak cough, difficulty swallowing or shallow breathing calls for urgent respiratory assessment.
Episodes of neurologic deficits separated in time
  • Consider multiple sclerosis.
  • Compare function with baseline and review infection or overheating when symptoms worsen.
Slowness, rigidity and a resting tremor
  • Think Parkinson disease.
  • Assess fall and swallowing risks and keep prescribed medications on schedule.
New confusion, pupillary change or a falling Glasgow Coma Scale score after head trauma
  • Escalate immediately, support airway and oxygenation, and protect spinal alignment.
  • Reassess the individual eye, verbal and motor responses.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • Glasgow Coma Scale: eyes 1–4, verbal 1–5 and motor 1–6. Report individual components and the trend, and document any untestable component.
  • With suspected acute stroke, last known well is the last time the patient was at neurologic baseline, even if the deficit was first noticed later.

Practice and memory cues

A sudden severe BP rise after spinal cord injury suggests autonomic dysreflexia. Lower pressure in neurogenic shock is a different pattern.

Which assessment finding is the earliest indicator of rising intracranial pressure?

  1. A subtle change in level of consciousness
  2. Widening pulse pressure with bradycardia
  3. Cheyne-Stokes respirations
  4. Fixed dilated pupils
Check the answer and why

A subtle change in level of consciousness

A change in LOC is the earliest sign ; Cushing's triad - rising systolic with widened pulse pressure, bradycardia and irregular respirations - is a late herniation sign . If the triad is present, you are already late.

A client with a T4 spinal cord injury has BP 208/112, HR 48, a pounding headache and flushing above the injury. What does the nurse do first?

  1. Administer the prescribed antihypertensive
  2. Sit the client upright
  3. Check for a fecal impaction
  4. Lay the client flat and elevate the legs
Check the answer and why

Sit the client upright

In autonomic dysreflexia the first action is to sit her up , which uses gravity to drop the blood pressure immediately, then find and remove the trigger - check the bladder before the bowel. Antihypertensives are an adjunct, not the first move, and lying flat worsens the hypertension.

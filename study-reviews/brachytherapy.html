Brachytherapy

Internal radiation, close to the cancer.

Your question’s key point: keep the applicator in position.
For the temporary gynecologic implant in this question, lie still and follow the prescribed bed-rest restrictions to prevent displacement. [2]

☢️ Brachytherapy infographic

A sealed radiation source is positioned in or near the tumor. An applicator or catheter guides placement. Examples include treatment of the cervix, prostate, breast and eye. [1]

🛏️ Why “I need to lie still” is correct

  • Movement can shift an applicator or catheters. Radiation must reach the planned treatment site.
  • Follow the ordered position and movement restrictions. Gynecologic interstitial catheters may require remaining in bed between sessions.
  • Call for assistance. Do not adjust or remove the applicator yourself. Provide pain relief, comfort and prescribed immobility-prevention care.

Procedure-specific positioning: MSK gynecologic HDR guide [2].

Clinical distinction: bed rest is not a universal lifelong rule for every brachytherapy patient. Restrictions depend on the applicator, implant and procedure. An applicator can stay in place after the radioactive source has returned to the machine.

Three treatment patterns

Temporary HDR · high dose rate

  • Short sessions; a machine moves the source through the applicator and then retrieves it.
  • After the source is removed, the patient does not emit radiation from that treatment.

Temporary LDR · low dose rate

  • The source may stay for hours or days.
  • Radiation precautions apply while it remains; it is then removed.

Permanent seeds

  • Seeds stay in the body; their radiation decreases over time.
  • Follow written time/contact precautions, especially around pregnant people and young children.

Frequency is individualized

There is no universal “one or two sessions a week” schedule. Cancer site, dose and implant type determine the plan.

NCI [1]; OncoLink [3].

⏱️ Nursing safety: time, distance, shielding

  • Time: organize necessary care to reduce exposure without withholding care.
  • Distance: maximize distance from an active source when hands-on care is not needed.
  • Shielding: use the barriers and protective equipment specified by radiation safety.
  • Dosimeter: tracks exposure; it does not block radiation.
  • Visitors: follow the treatment-specific limits and posted instructions.

CDC ALARA [4]; OncoLink [3].

If a source or applicator is displaced

Do not touch or reinsert it. Keep others away and promptly notify the radiation safety officer and treatment team. Follow the emergency protocol; only trained staff handle a loose source with the designated equipment. [5]

Sealed is different from systemic: an intact sealed source does not make urine, stool or sweat radioactive. A loose or passed seed itself is a radiation source. Follow the specific seed-recovery instructions; do not assume the precautions for radioactive iodine apply. [3]

After treatment: which findings need a call?

  • After gynecologic HDR: report blood in urine, fever ≥100.4°F (38°C), bleeding beyond light spotting or pain not relieved by the prescribed medicine. [2]
  • Procedure matters: mild blood-tinged urine can occur after prostate brachytherapy. Follow that procedure’s instructions; clots or inability to urinate need prompt medical contact. [6]

🧠 Original picture mnemonic: Brachy Brakes

  • Bed brake → keep the applicator still as ordered.
  • Clock → time. Limit unnecessary exposure.
  • Footsteps → distance. Step back when care allows.
  • Shield → shielding. Follow the radiation-safety plan.
  • Source goes home to the machine → temporary HDR is over. No source left means no radiation from the patient.
  • Seeds stay → check the written discharge precautions.

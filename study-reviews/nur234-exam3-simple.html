Module 8 · Pregnancy with a preexisting condition 6 short topics

Module 8

Identifying a high-risk pregnancy

  • Recognize: Risk may predate pregnancy or emerge later: cardiac, renal/endocrine disease, chronic hypertension, prior complications, multifetal pregnancy and care barriers matter.
  • Act: Establish baseline maternal/fetal findings, reconcile medicines and coordinate indicated specialist care and follow-up.
  • Remember: Risk identification should produce an individualized surveillance plan and clear urgent warning signs, not a label without practical support.

Module 8

Cardiovascular disorders and pregnancy (left sided and right sided failure)

  • Recognize: Left-sided heart failure causes pulmonary congestion, dyspnea, orthopnea and crackles; right-sided failure causes systemic congestion, edema and neck-vein distention.
  • Act: Escalate breathlessness at rest, chest pain, syncope or hypoxemia; trend oxygenation, perfusion, fluid balance and fetal status.
  • Remember: Pregnancy increases cardiac workload, and postpartum fluid shifts can also trigger decompensation; both failure patterns may coexist.

Module 8

Chronic hypertensive vascular disease

  • Recognize: Hypertension present before pregnancy or before 20 weeks suggests chronic hypertension; new organ findings can indicate superimposed preeclampsia.
  • Act: Compare pressure, renal findings and urine protein with baseline; reinforce prescribed treatment and urgently report severe headache, visual change, upper abdominal pain or dyspnea.
  • Remember: Chronic hypertension does not protect against preeclampsia. Medication selection must account for pregnancy; ACE inhibitors/ARBs are generally avoided.

Module 8

Venous thromboembolic disease

  • Recognize: Unilateral painful swelling, warmth or redness suggests DVT; sudden dyspnea, chest pain, hemoptysis or collapse suggests pulmonary embolism.
  • Act: Escalate promptly, anticipate imaging and ordered anticoagulation, and avoid massaging the leg.
  • Remember: Heparin-based therapy is commonly used in pregnancy. Anticoagulation affects bleeding precautions, birth planning and neuraxial anesthesia; Homan's sign is not a reliable diagnostic test.

Module 8

Renal / Urinary disorders of pregnancy (UTI’s)

  • Recognize: Cystitis causes dysuria, urgency or suprapubic discomfort; fever/flank pain suggests pyelonephritis. Asymptomatic bacteriuria can be found by prenatal culture.
  • Act: Obtain ordered urine studies, monitor maternal/fetal status and administer prescribed antibiotics; escalate systemic symptoms.
  • Remember: Pyelonephritis can cause sepsis and preterm labor and usually requires initial inpatient care; lack of urinary symptoms does not exclude bacteriuria.

Module 8

Endocrine disorders and pregnancy (Diabetes mellitus)

  • Recognize: Pregestational and gestational diabetes differ in onset. Early hyperglycemia increases anomaly risk; later hyperglycemia promotes excessive fetal growth and neonatal hypoglycemia.
  • Act: Teach glucose monitoring, individualized food/activity plans, prescribed medication and hypoglycemia response; arrange fetal and postpartum follow-up.
  • Remember: After birth, maternal insulin needs can fall rapidly. The infant's high insulin can persist after maternal glucose delivery stops.

Module 9

Spontaneous abortions

  • Recognize: Bleeding/cramps may accompany miscarriage. Threatened loss has a closed cervix; incomplete loss retains tissue, while complete loss passes it.
  • Act: Assess stability and escalate heavy bleeding, syncope, fever or worsening pain; support ultrasound/hCG evaluation, treatment choices and grief care.
  • Remember: Symptoms alone do not establish viability or pregnancy location; routine bed rest does not prevent miscarriage.

Module 9

Ectopic pregnancy

  • Recognize: Ectopic implantation can cause unilateral pelvic pain and bleeding; rupture may cause shoulder pain, fainting and shock despite little visible blood.
  • Act: Assess circulation and urgently escalate suspected rupture; anticipate ultrasound/hCG evaluation and medical or surgical treatment according to stability.
  • Remember: An empty uterus alone does not diagnose ectopic pregnancy. Methotrexate treatment requires reliable follow-up and urgent reporting of worsening symptoms.

Module 9

Gestational trophoblastic disease

  • Recognize: Molar pregnancy may produce bleeding, marked nausea, high hCG and a uterus larger than expected; ultrasound/pathology establish the diagnosis.
  • Act: Support evacuation and serial hCG follow-up, contraception counseling and grief care.
  • Remember: Persistent trophoblastic disease can remain after evacuation. Follow-up duration depends on diagnosis and specialist protocol; a new pregnancy can obscure hCG monitoring.

Module 9

Premature cervical dilation

  • Recognize: Cervical insufficiency commonly causes painless shortening/dilation in the second trimester; prior midtrimester loss or preterm birth increases concern.
  • Act: Review cervical-length findings and history; support prescribed progesterone/cerclage care and teach reporting pressure, bleeding, fluid leakage, fever or contractions.
  • Remember: Painless cervical opening differs from preterm labor driven by contractions with cervical change; not every short cervix receives the same treatment.

Module 9

Placenta previa

  • Recognize: Placenta over the cervical opening classically causes painless bleeding, but ultrasound determines location.
  • Act: Assess maternal circulation and fetal status; obtain urgent obstetric evaluation and prepare the ordered birth/bleeding plan.
  • Remember: Do not perform a digital vaginal examination until previa is excluded. Painless bleeding suggests previa but does not confirm it or measure hemorrhage severity.

Module 9

Placental abruption

  • Recognize: Placental separation can cause abdominal/back pain, uterine tenderness or rigidity and fetal compromise; blood may remain concealed.
  • Act: Escalate urgently, quantify visible loss, assess perfusion/fetal status and prepare IV access, blood studies/products and possible urgent birth.
  • Remember: Small visible blood loss does not exclude major hemorrhage. Watch for DIC; abruption is separation, unlike previa's abnormal location.

Module 9

Disseminated intravascular coagulation

  • Recognize: DIC consumes platelets and clotting factors, producing widespread clotting and bleeding; oozing, bruising and worsening perfusion are warning signs.
  • Act: Treat the precipitating emergency with the team, support oxygenation/circulation and anticipate blood products plus serial coagulation studies.
  • Remember: Abruption, severe preeclampsia/HELLP, sepsis, major hemorrhage and amniotic fluid embolism are important obstetric triggers; trends matter more than one laboratory result.

Module 9

Preterm labor

  • Recognize: Preterm labor occurs before 37 weeks with contractions and cervical change; pressure, cramps, backache or leaking fluid warrant evaluation.
  • Act: Assess membranes, infection, bleeding and fetal status; prepare prescribed corticosteroids, short-term tocolysis or transfer when appropriate.
  • Remember: Steroids promote fetal maturation; tocolytics buy time; magnesium may provide early-gestation neuroprotection. Infection or maternal/fetal compromise can make delaying birth unsafe.

Module 9

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy

  • Recognize: Preeclampsia is hypertension after 20 weeks with proteinuria or qualifying organ dysfunction; severe headache, visual changes or epigastric pain are concerning.
  • Act: Escalate severe findings. During magnesium, assess respirations, reflexes and urine output; stop suspected toxic infusion, support breathing and prepare ordered calcium gluconate.
  • Remember: Magnesium prevents/treats seizures; a separate antihypertensive treats severe pressure. Proteinuria is not required with qualifying organ dysfunction.

Module 9

HELLP syndrome

  • Recognize: HELLP means hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes and low platelets. RUQ/epigastric pain, nausea, malaise or headache can accompany it.
  • Act: Escalate suspected HELLP, assess bleeding/perfusion and anticipate serial blood counts, liver/renal tests, coagulation studies and an obstetric delivery plan.
  • Remember: Severe hypertension or proteinuria may be absent; concerning pain and laboratory trends still require action, including postpartum surveillance.

Module 9

Multiple gestation

  • Recognize: Multiple gestation increases preterm birth, growth, anemia, hypertension and uterine-overdistention risks; shared placentas add specific complications.
  • Act: Review ultrasound findings about fetal number and shared placenta/sacs; individualize nutrition, growth/fluid surveillance and maternal/newborn birth resources.
  • Remember: Chorionicity and amnionicity matter beyond counting fetuses; a shared placenta can create twin-to-twin transfusion risk.

Module 9

Amniotic fluid volume

  • Recognize: Oligohydramnios can accompany rupture, placental insufficiency or fetal urinary problems; polyhydramnios can accompany diabetes or impaired fetal swallowing.
  • Act: Assess membranes, growth and fetal wellbeing; interpret ultrasound volume with gestation and the suspected cause.
  • Remember: Low fluid increases cord-compression concerns; high fluid increases overdistention, preterm-labor and postpartum-atony risk. Volume alone does not identify the cause.

Module 9

Isoimmunization/Rhogam

  • Recognize: An Rh-negative person may form antibodies after exposure to Rh-positive fetal blood; those antibodies can affect a future/current Rh-positive fetus.
  • Act: Verify blood type and antibody screen; administer ordered Rh immune globulin to eligible unsensitized patients around 28 weeks and within 72 hours after an Rh-positive birth.
  • Remember: Prophylaxis prevents sensitization; it does not reverse established antibodies.

Module 10

Dysfunctional labor

  • Recognize: Slow labor can reflect ineffective powers, fetal malposition, a restricted passage or poor coping/support.
  • Act: Assess contraction pattern, cervical change, descent, bladder and maternal/fetal status; address the cause and reassess response.
  • Remember: More oxytocin is not the answer to every slow labor. Obstruction or fetal intolerance requires obstetric reassessment rather than simply stronger contractions.

Module 10

Group B streptococcus (GBS)

  • Recognize: GBS colonization can expose the newborn during birth; late-pregnancy screening generally occurs at 36–37 weeks.
  • Act: Verify results/allergies and administer indicated IV intrapartum antibiotics; communicate treatment timing and relevant history to the newborn team.
  • Remember: GBS is Streptococcus agalactiae, not Staphylococcus aureus. Colonization is not an STI; a prior affected infant or GBS bacteriuria can also establish prophylaxis indications.

Module 10

Induction / Augmentation of labor

  • Recognize: Induction starts labor; augmentation strengthens established labor. Oxytocin/ripening can cause tachysystole and fetal intolerance.
  • Act: Confirm readiness, presentation, consent and contraindications; monitor fetal/contraction response and reduce or stop oxytocin for excessive activity according to the response protocol.
  • Remember: Tachysystole is more than five contractions in 10 minutes, averaged over 30 minutes; the fetus needs recovery between contractions.

Module 10

Cervical ripening/Bishop score

  • Recognize: Bishop scoring combines cervical dilation, effacement, consistency, position and fetal station. A higher score indicates a more favorable cervix.
  • Act: Explain the sequence and prepare prescribed balloon or medication ripening when indicated; assess uterine-scar precautions and monitor contractions/fetal response.
  • Remember: Bishop predicts induction readiness, not spontaneous labor onset. An unfavorable cervix may need ripening before oxytocin.

Module 10

Uterine rupture/uterine inversion

  • Recognize: Rupture can cause fetal bradycardia, pain, bleeding, loss of station or shock. Inversion turns the uterus inside out after birth, causing hemorrhage/shock.
  • Act: Activate emergency help and resuscitation; stop oxytocin for suspected rupture and prepare surgery. For inversion, prepare trained-clinician replacement.
  • Remember: Do not pull the cord or independently remove placenta during inversion; both conditions demand immediate obstetric care.

Module 10

Amniotic fluid embolism

  • Recognize: Amniotic fluid embolism can cause sudden respiratory/cardiovascular collapse during labor or shortly after birth, often followed by DIC.
  • Act: Activate obstetric/resuscitation teams, support oxygenation and ventilation, begin CPR if pulseless and coordinate hemorrhage management and urgent birth when indicated.
  • Remember: AFE is a clinical emergency without a single confirmatory test; team interventions occur simultaneously.

Module 10

Umbilical cord prolapse

  • Recognize: Cord prolapse may follow membrane rupture with a high presenting part; sudden fetal bradycardia/prolonged deceleration suggests compression.
  • Act: Call help, stop oxytocin, elevate the presenting part as trained, position to relieve compression, provide oxygen by mask and prepare urgent birth.
  • Remember: Maintain pressure relief until handover/birth; avoid unnecessary cord handling and never push the cord back into the uterus.

Module 10

Posterior/breech position

  • Recognize: Occiput posterior can produce back pain, slow rotation and prolonged labor; breech presents buttocks or feet first.
  • Act: Support position changes for posterior position; confirm breech and coordinate the individualized birth plan and monitoring.
  • Remember: Persistent malpresentation raises risks such as cord prolapse and may require cesarean birth; stronger contractions do not correct every position problem.

Module 10

Macrosomia

  • Recognize: Large fetal size increases shoulder dystocia, maternal trauma and neonatal metabolic risks, but estimated weight is imprecise.
  • Act: Review growth/diabetes history, prepare for the planned birth and assess the newborn for injury and indicated glucose monitoring.
  • Remember: A large infant does not inevitably develop dystocia, and smaller infants can also have it; the whole labor assessment matters.

Module 10

Shoulder dystocia

  • Recognize: After the head delivers, an impacted shoulder prevents the body from following; head retraction may create the turtle sign.
  • Act: Call the emergency team, record times and assist McRoberts positioning with directed suprapubic pressure; assess maternal/newborn injury after birth.
  • Remember: Never apply fundal pressure or excessive traction on the head; those actions can worsen impaction and injury.

Module 10

Inlet/outlet contraction

  • Recognize: A restricted inlet can prevent engagement; a restricted outlet can obstruct descent or delivery. Size and fetal position influence the fit.
  • Act: Monitor descent, cervical progress, contractions and fetal condition; escalate persistent obstruction for obstetric reassessment and possible cesarean birth.
  • Remember: Do not use oxytocin to force a fetus through an obstructed passage. A pelvic label alone does not determine outcome.

Module 10

External cephalic version

  • Recognize: ECV is external abdominal manipulation by a trained clinician to turn breech toward a head-down presentation.
  • Act: Confirm eligibility/consent, ultrasound findings and fetal wellbeing; monitor before/after in a setting able to perform urgent cesarean birth, and assess indicated Rh prophylaxis.
  • Remember: Report bleeding, pain, contractions, reduced movement or leaking fluid afterward; ECV can cause fetal intolerance.

Module 10

Forceps/Vacuum-assisted delivery

  • Recognize: Forceps or vacuum may expedite selected second-stage births when prerequisites are met.
  • Act: Verify full dilation, ruptured membranes, engaged head, known position, empty bladder, consent and analgesia; monitor fetal status and assess maternal/newborn trauma afterward.
  • Remember: Operative vaginal birth requires a trained operator and backup plan. Scalp swelling, neurologic change or excessive bleeding needs evaluation.

Module 10

Velamentous insertion

  • Recognize: With velamentous insertion, fetal vessels travel through membranes without normal Wharton's-jelly protection before entering the placenta.
  • Act: Communicate ultrasound findings and follow the individualized surveillance/birth plan; assess whether vessels also cross near the cervix.
  • Remember: Unprotected vessels are vulnerable to compression or rupture. Velamentous insertion and vasa previa are related, but they are not identical conditions.

Module 10

Vasa-previa

  • Recognize: Vasa previa places unprotected fetal vessels over or near the cervical opening; rupture may cause rapid fetal blood loss.
  • Act: Escalate bleeding with sudden fetal compromise after membrane rupture; avoid amniotomy when vasa previa is known/suspected and follow the specialist birth plan.
  • Remember: The blood loss is fetal, so maternal vital signs may initially appear stable.

Module 10

Placenta accreta/increta/percreta

  • Recognize: Accreta attaches abnormally to myometrium; increta invades it; percreta extends through the uterine wall and possibly nearby organs.
  • Act: Prepare coordinated obstetric/surgical care with blood-product availability; explain the individualized plan, which may include hysterectomy.
  • Remember: Attempted placental separation can cause massive hemorrhage. Do not forcibly remove the placenta; prior cesarean plus previa raises concern.

Module 10

Amniotomy

  • Recognize: Amniotomy intentionally ruptures membranes to assist selected labor or monitoring procedures; cord compression/prolapse and infection are risks.
  • Act: Verify indication, presentation and engagement; assess fetal heart rate before and immediately afterward, and document fluid/time.
  • Remember: Continue infection surveillance and minimize unnecessary vaginal examinations after rupture; placental or fetal-vessel contraindications must be excluded first.

Module 10

Episiotomy

  • Recognize: Episiotomy incises the perineum for selected birth circumstances. After repair, assess pain, swelling, wound approximation and bleeding.
  • Act: Provide ordered analgesia and perineal care; teach hygiene and reporting worsening pain, pressure, discharge or fever.
  • Remember: Severe persistent pain or pressure may indicate a hematoma rather than expected soreness; routine episiotomy is not required for every vaginal birth.

Module 10

Internal monitoring

  • Recognize: A fetal scalp electrode measures fetal heart rate; an intrauterine pressure catheter measures contraction pressure and resting tone.
  • Act: Confirm ruptured membranes, appropriate access and contraindication review; maintain asepsis and assess for infection/trauma.
  • Remember: The devices answer different questions and do not replace maternal/fetal assessment; relevant infection or bleeding risks can make internal monitoring unsuitable.

Module 10

Cesarean birth

  • Recognize: Cesarean birth requires surgical, anesthesia and newborn preparation, followed by monitoring for hemorrhage, infection, respiratory effects and VTE.
  • Act: Verify consent/allergies and ordered IV, antibiotics and thrombosis measures; afterward assess vitals, fundus/lochia, incision, urine, pain and mobility.
  • Remember: Encourage appropriate early movement and feeding support. Heavy bleeding, chest pain, dyspnea, fever or a swollen painful leg needs urgent evaluation.

Every exam: priority + dose checks
  • Clinical judgment: Recognize cues → analyze → prioritize → plan → act → evaluate. Compare with baseline; reassess the response.
  • Dose safety: Verify patient, allergies, drug, dose, route and time. Use kilograms for weight-based doses; check the safe range and maximum.
  • Calculate: Ordered dose ÷ available dose × volume. Example: 250 mg ÷ 125 mg × 5 mL = 10 mL. Clarify an unsafe or unclear order before giving it.

Rules to remember

  • Bleeding with pain or faintness changes the priority.
  • Check breathing, reflexes and urine output during magnesium therapy.
  • Recognize a delivery emergency from the sequence of events.

See this → think this

An early-pregnancy patient has one-sided pelvic pain, vaginal spotting and new dizziness.

Think…
  • Possible ectopic pregnancy.
  • Faintness or hypotension can signal internal bleeding.

A patient receiving magnesium sulfate becomes drowsy with slow breathing and absent reflexes.

Think…
  • Magnesium toxicity can suppress breathing.
  • Poor renal clearance increases risk.

The head delivers and draws back against the perineum. The shoulders do not follow.

Think…
  • The anterior shoulder may be trapped behind the pubic bone: shoulder dystocia.

What do I do first?

Early Pregnancy Bleeding & Ectopic Pregnancy

  1. First action
    • Assess circulation and summon urgent obstetric assistance.
    • Prepare IV access and evaluation.
  2. Look for a response
    • Trend mental status, pulse and blood pressure while definitive care is arranged.

Hypertensive Disorders & Magnesium Sulfate

  1. First action
    • Stop the infusion, call for help and support ventilation.
    • Prepare prescribed calcium gluconate.
  2. Look for a response
    • Reassess respiratory effort, alertness, reflexes and urine output.

Dysfunctional Labor, Uterine Rupture & Obstetric Drug Math

  1. First action
    • Call for help and assist with McRoberts positioning and suprapubic pressure as directed.
    • Avoid fundal pressure.
  2. Look for a response
    • After delivery, assess newborn breathing and arm movement and assess maternal bleeding.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • Blood pressure at or above 160 systolic or 110 diastolic in pregnancy is severe range. Notify the team promptly and confirm according to the urgent pathway.
  • Painless bleeding raises concern for previa; painful bleeding with a tender uterus suggests abruption. Either needs urgent assessment. Avoid digital vaginal examination until previa is excluded.

Practice and memory cues

McRoberts flexes the maternal hips. Suprapubic pressure is applied above the pubic bone by a trained team member. Fundal pressure can worsen shoulder impaction.

A client arrives in the emergency department with a suspected unruptured ectopic pregnancy. She is alert with stable vital signs. Which action does the nurse take first?

  1. Establish large-bore intravenous access
  2. Place her in Trendelenburg position
  3. Prepare her immediately for laparoscopic surgery
  4. Administer a folic acid supplement
Check the answer and why

Establish large-bore intravenous access

Large-bore IV access comes first so fluids and blood can be given instantly if she ruptures, along with continuous vital signs and methotrexate as prescribed. Trendelenburg is not indicated, surgery is not automatic for an unruptured ectopic, and folic acid is contraindicated with methotrexate.

A provider prescribes oxytocin at 3 milliunits/min for labor augmentation. The pharmacy supplies 20 units of oxytocin in 1,000 mL of lactated Ringer's. How many mL/hr does the nurse set on the pump?

  1. 3 mL/hr
  2. 9 mL/hr
  3. 15 mL/hr
  4. 30 mL/hr
Check the answer and why

9 mL/hr

20 units = 20,000 milliunits in 1,000 mL, so the bag is 20 milliunits/mL . Then 3 milliunits/min divided by 20 milliunits/mL = 0.15 mL/min, and 0.15 x 60 = 9 mL/hr . Skipping that final x60 leaves 0.15 and is the single most commonly missed step.

Module 1 · Pediatric assessment, comfort and medication safety 6 short topics

Module 1

Medication administration and techniques

  • Recognize: Immature kidneys and liver change drug handling; the correct adult dose cannot simply be scaled down.
  • Act: Check allergies, weight in kilograms, safe dose, route and measuring device. Give oral medicines in a small approved volume, never a full bottle.
  • Remember: Offer a choice of how, not whether; never call medicine candy. Reassess effect and adverse reactions.

Module 1

Therapeutic communication

  • Recognize: Developmental ability determines how a child understands illness, time and instructions; caregiver anxiety also affects the child.
  • Act: Use short, honest explanations, medical play and limited real choices. Speak directly to the child and include the caregiver.
  • Remember: Demonstrate unfamiliar procedures; never promise that a painful procedure will not hurt. Adolescents need privacy and participation.

Module 1

Diagnostic procedures and specimen collection

  • Recognize: Fear and movement can interfere with testing; contaminated specimens can produce misleading results.
  • Act: Explain at the child's level, use comfort positioning and collect the correct specimen with ordered technique. Label promptly and check preparation requirements.
  • Remember: Use least invasive steps first when appropriate. A urine collection bag carries contamination risk; a positive bag result needs proper confirmation.

Module 1

Hospitalization and perception of illness

  • Recognize: Separation, unfamiliar routines and loss of control may cause crying, withdrawal or regression; reaction varies with developmental stage.
  • Act: Encourage caregiver presence, familiar comfort objects and home routines. Preserve age-appropriate independence and therapeutic play.
  • Remember: A quiet, withdrawn child may still be distressed. Toddlers need routines; school-age children need competence; adolescents need privacy and peer contact.

Module 1

Pain assessment and management

  • Recognize: Use self-report when the child can reliably communicate; otherwise use a validated behavioral scale such as FLACC.
  • Act: Combine prescribed analgesia with comfort positioning, distraction and caregiver support. Reassess with the same pain tool after intervention.
  • Remember: FLACC scores face, legs, activity, cry and consolability. Lack of crying does not prove comfort; monitor sedation and breathing with opioids.

Module 1

Weight-based dosage calculations

  • Recognize: A calculation may be mathematically correct yet unsafe if pounds, kilograms, daily dose and dose frequency are confused.
  • Act: Convert weight to kilograms; find the safe range, compare the order and calculate the volume from concentration. Clarify an unsafe order before giving it.
  • Remember: mg/kg/day is divided across scheduled doses; mg/kg/dose is per administration. Check units, maximum dose and decimal placement.

Module 2 · Infant growth, development and health promotion 9 short topics

Module 2

Infant assessment: expected and unexpected findings

  • Recognize: Observe breathing, color, interaction, feeding and hydration before disturbing the infant; compare findings with age-specific expectations.
  • Act: Keep the caregiver close, use a warm environment, and auscultate while quiet. Trend weight, length and head circumference rather than judging one measurement.
  • Remember: Poor feeding, lethargy, respiratory distress or abnormal growth needs assessment; a newborn may show subtle illness signs.

Module 2

Infant development: Erikson and Piaget

  • Recognize: Erikson: trust versus mistrust. Piaget: sensorimotor learning through movement and the senses; object permanence develops during infancy.
  • Act: Respond consistently to hunger and comfort cues. Provide safe sensory exploration, caregiver presence and simple play such as peekaboo.
  • Remember: Consistent care builds trust; separation or stranger anxiety reflects development. Match teaching to what the infant can experience, not verbal reasoning.

Module 2

Infant milestones and growth

  • Recognize: Growth is rapid; birth weight generally doubles around 4–6 months and triples by one year. Motor control develops from head downward.
  • Act: Trend growth and motor, language and social progress. Ask caregivers about new skills and any lost skills; arrange follow-up for concerning delays.
  • Remember: Milestones are ranges, not a race. Loss of an acquired skill is a warning sign, not normal variation.

Module 2

Infant health promotion

  • Recognize: Well-child care tracks growth, development, feeding, hearing, vision and caregiver coping before a problem becomes obvious.
  • Act: Support feeding and oral care, review newborn-screen follow-up and immunizations, and teach a safe soothing plan. Reinforce supervised awake tummy time.
  • Remember: Routine visits include prevention and screening even when the infant looks well. Place an overwhelmed caregiver's infant safely down and seek support.

Module 2

Common infant health concerns

  • Recognize: Spitting up, crying and sleep changes may be common; poor growth, dehydration, lethargy or illness with fever needs assessment.
  • Act: Assess intake, wet diapers, growth and overall appearance. Teach urgent evaluation for fever in an infant under three months or for breathing difficulty.
  • Remember: A common symptom is reassuring only when feeding, growth and examination fit. Never dismiss persistent vomiting or an unusually sleepy infant.

Module 2

Infant immunizations

  • Recognize: Infants need a series of vaccines, not a single protective visit; missed doses require a catch-up plan.
  • Act: Review the current schedule and record, screen for true contraindications, provide the Vaccine Information Statement and use the appropriate injection site and comfort measures.
  • Remember: Document administration and teach expected reactions versus emergency allergy signs. Mild illness alone usually does not require postponing vaccines; check individual guidance.

Module 2

Infant abuse and neglect recognition

  • Recognize: Injury inconsistent with developmental ability, unexplained bruising, poor care or changing histories raises concern; abusive head trauma may present with neurologic change.
  • Act: Ensure immediate safety, assess injury, document objective findings and report suspected maltreatment through the required process. Support caregivers without accusation.
  • Remember: An infant cannot explain what happened. Teach never to shake a baby and use safe breaks when crying becomes overwhelming.

Module 2

Infant safety

  • Recognize: A mobile infant can choke, fall or reach hazards before a caregiver expects it; sleep environments add preventable risk.
  • Act: For every sleep use supine positioning on a firm, flat, clear surface. Teach rear-facing car-seat use within seat limits, close water supervision and removal of choking hazards.
  • Remember: Tummy time is awake and supervised. Safe sleep does not change to prone because an infant has reflux.

Module 2

Infant nutrition

  • Recognize: Breast milk or infant formula supplies early nutrition; readiness and growth guide complementary feeding around six months.
  • Act: Support safe preparation and responsive feeding. Introduce age-appropriate iron-rich foods and textures; assess swallowing, intake and growth.
  • Remember: Avoid honey before one year and cow's milk as the main drink before one year. Do not prop bottles or add cereal without a clinical reason.

Module 3 · Toddler and preschool growth and health promotion 9 short topics

Module 3

Toddler and preschool assessment

  • Recognize: Stranger fear, resistance and short attention span can limit assessment; expected findings differ from adult norms.
  • Act: Let the child stay near the caregiver, demonstrate on a doll, and use least threatening steps first. Offer two acceptable choices and save uncomfortable procedures for last when possible.
  • Remember: Observe play and behavior as assessment data. Approach according to developmental ability, not age alone.

Module 3

Toddler and preschool development: Erikson and Piaget

  • Recognize: Toddlers seek autonomy; preschoolers seek initiative. Preoperational thinking includes egocentrism and magical explanations, so illness may be interpreted as punishment.
  • Act: Give limited real choices, maintain routines and use simple concrete explanations or medical play. Correct the belief that thoughts or misbehavior caused illness.
  • Remember: Parallel play fits toddlers; associative play becomes prominent in preschoolers. Support independence without shaming mistakes.

Module 3

Toddler and preschool milestones and growth

  • Recognize: Walking advances to running, climbing and improved hand skills; language and pretend play expand. Growth slows compared with infancy.
  • Act: Assess gross motor, fine motor, language and social skills across visits. Provide safe practice and investigate loss of skills or persistent developmental concerns.
  • Remember: Toilet training depends on physical and behavioral readiness, not pressure. Praise effort and avoid punishment for accidents.

Module 3

Toddler and preschool health promotion

  • Recognize: Routines, play, dental care and caregiver limit-setting shape health during increasing independence.
  • Act: Encourage predictable sleep and meals, active play, oral care and regular well-child checks. Use consistent limits and positive reinforcement; build self-care into routines.
  • Remember: Offer choices within safe boundaries. Preschool medical play can reduce fear; avoid turning health care or medication into a threat.

Module 3

Common toddler and preschool concerns

  • Recognize: Tantrums, picky eating and sleep fears often accompany development; pain, poor growth, prolonged distress or developmental regression needs assessment.
  • Act: Use calm consistent routines and assess triggers. Distinguish nightmares, where the child wakes and recalls fear, from sleep terrors with incomplete awakening.
  • Remember: Comfort after nightmares; protect safety during sleep terrors. Avoid assuming every behavior problem is intentional or every food refusal is disease.

Module 3

Toddler and preschool immunizations

  • Recognize: Children may need scheduled boosters or catch-up vaccines; school entry does not replace reviewing the actual record.
  • Act: Check current recommendations, vaccine history and contraindications. Give the Vaccine Information Statement and use developmentally appropriate explanation, caregiver support and distraction.
  • Remember: A severe allergic reaction to a prior dose or component requires evaluation. Keep a clear record and explain expected soreness versus urgent breathing or swelling symptoms.

Module 3

Toddler and preschool abuse and neglect recognition

  • Recognize: Injuries inconsistent with the story, developmental ability or ordinary play patterns raise concern. Children may disclose fear or abuse indirectly.
  • Act: Ensure safety; listen calmly without leading questions, document the child's words and objective findings, and follow required reporting procedures.
  • Remember: Do not promise secrecy or conduct an independent interrogation. Abuse can be physical, sexual or emotional; neglect also threatens health.

Module 3

Toddler and preschool safety

  • Recognize: Exploration outpaces judgment; poisoning, drowning, burns, falls and choking remain major hazards.
  • Act: Lock medicines and chemicals, supervise around water, use appropriate car restraints and helmets, and provide food sized for safe chewing.
  • Remember: A child-resistant cap is not childproof. Never store chemicals in drink containers; supervision and environmental controls work together.

Module 3

Toddler and preschool nutrition

  • Recognize: Slower growth can reduce appetite; excessive milk and grazing may displace iron-rich balanced meals.
  • Act: Offer small portions, regular meals and snacks, varied textures and nutritious choices. Assess growth over time rather than forcing a clean plate.
  • Remember: Caregivers choose what and when; the child regulates how much. Avoid choking hazards and pressure-based feeding; evaluate poor growth or suspected deficiency.

Module 4 · School-age and adolescent growth and health promotion 9 short topics

Module 4

School-age and adolescent assessment

  • Recognize: School-age children can describe symptoms concretely; adolescents may withhold sensitive concerns when privacy is absent.
  • Act: Explain the examination, protect modesty and assess growth, puberty, function and psychosocial health. Include confidential adolescent discussion within legal and safety limits.
  • Remember: Ask directly about mood, safety, substances and sexual health without judgment. Chronological age alone does not determine understanding.

Module 4

School-age and adolescent development: Erikson and Piaget

  • Recognize: School-age: industry versus inferiority and concrete operations. Adolescence: identity versus role confusion and emerging abstract reasoning.
  • Act: Give school-age children achievable tasks and concrete explanations. Include adolescents in decisions, respect privacy and acknowledge peer relationships.
  • Remember: Competence motivates school-age children; identity motivates adolescents. Abstract ability develops gradually, so confirm understanding instead of assuming it.

Module 4

School-age and adolescent milestones and growth

  • Recognize: Steady school-age growth changes to a pubertal growth spurt; timing and body changes vary between individuals.
  • Act: Trend height, weight and pubertal progression; assess body image, peer relationships, school function and coping. Investigate abnormal growth patterns or delayed development.
  • Remember: Compare the child with their own trajectory. Avoid treating normal variation in puberty as failure or assuming physical maturity equals emotional maturity.

Module 4

School-age and adolescent health promotion

  • Recognize: Peers, school demands, sleep habits and growing independence influence health choices.
  • Act: Support adequate sleep, physical activity, dental care and age-appropriate screening. Teach substance avoidance, sexual health and safer decision-making; keep school and family supports involved appropriately.
  • Remember: Use collaborative goals and factual explanations. Private, respectful conversation makes sensitive disclosure more likely than lectures or threats.

Module 4

Common school-age and adolescent concerns

  • Recognize: School stress, body-image concerns, bullying, mood changes and disordered eating can affect functioning and safety.
  • Act: Ask about school, peers, sleep, eating and coping. Evaluate persistent impairment or self-harm concerns promptly and connect the child with appropriate support.
  • Remember: Do not dismiss sustained withdrawal or major functional change as normal adolescence. Assess hearing and vision when school difficulty suggests a sensory problem.

School-age and adolescent immunizations

  • Recognize: Boosters and adolescent vaccines maintain protection; records may be incomplete after school or family transitions.
  • Act: Review the current schedule, prior doses and individual risks. Discuss vaccines with the adolescent and caregiver, obtain appropriate consent and provide the Vaccine Information Statement.
  • Remember: Use the current catch-up plan for missed doses. Explain expected reactions and urgent allergy symptoms, and document every administration.

Module 4

School-age and adolescent abuse and neglect recognition

  • Recognize: Unexplained injuries, fearful behavior, abrupt withdrawal, sexual concerns or inadequate care warrant assessment in context.
  • Act: Provide a safe, private conversation; listen without leading, document objectively, address immediate danger and follow required reporting processes.
  • Remember: Do not promise absolute confidentiality. Explain safety limits respectfully; physical, emotional and sexual abuse and neglect can occur at any age.

Module 4

School-age and adolescent safety

  • Recognize: Increasing independence adds sports, traffic, substance and online risks while judgment is still developing.
  • Act: Teach consistent seat-belt and helmet use, safe sports practices and avoidance of impaired driving. Assess violence, bullying, substance use and access to dangerous items.
  • Remember: Match guidance to actual activities and risk. Involve adolescents in a practical safety plan instead of relying on warnings alone.

Module 4

School-age and adolescent nutrition

  • Recognize: Growth and puberty increase nutritional needs; restrictive eating, poor food access or excess low-nutrient intake can disrupt health.
  • Act: Assess growth trajectory, diet, activity and body image without shaming. Encourage balanced meals with adequate iron, calcium and energy for growth.
  • Remember: Weight alone does not define nutritional health. Screen for disordered eating and support achievable family routines rather than extreme diets.

Every exam: priority + dose checks
  • Clinical judgment: Recognize cues → analyze → prioritize → plan → act → evaluate. Compare with baseline; reassess the response.
  • Dose safety: Verify patient, allergies, drug, dose, route and time. Use kilograms for weight-based doses; check the safe range and maximum.
  • Calculate: Ordered dose ÷ available dose × volume. Example: 250 mg ÷ 125 mg × 5 mL = 10 mL. Clarify an unsafe or unclear order before giving it.

What do I do first?

Intro to Pediatric Nursing

  1. First action
    • Score face, legs, activity, cry and consolability.
    • Provide appropriate pain relief.
  2. Look for a response
    • Repeat the same scale after the intervention and compare with caregiver observations.

The Infant (0–1 year)

  1. First action
    • Teach safe sleep using an empty crib and ask the caregiver to demonstrate.
  2. Look for a response
    • The caregiver shows the sleep setup without loose blankets, pillows or toys.

Toddler & Preschooler

  1. First action
    • Offer a small choice such as which arm to check and keep the caregiver nearby.
  2. Look for a response
    • The child participates with less distress and the needed assessment is completed.

School-Age & Adolescent

  1. First action
    • Show the supplies, explain the steps and give the child a manageable job.
  2. Look for a response
    • The child can describe the sequence and identify a coping strategy.

Numbers and distinctions

What to remember

  • FLACC has five behaviors scored 0–2 each, for a total of 0–10. Follow the change after an intervention.
  • Use serial growth measurements and a developmentally appropriate chart. A trend is more informative than a single percentile.

Practice and memory cues

Parallel play fits toddlers; cooperative rule-based play becomes more prominent in school-age children. Match teaching to what the child can understand.

A nurse observes a parent settle a 2-month-old for a nap prone on a soft quilt with crib bumpers in place. Which action should the nurse take first?

  1. Document the observation in the record
  2. Reposition the infant supine on a bare, firm mattress
  3. Ask the parent why she prefers the prone position
  4. Plan to teach safe sleep at the discharge visit
Check the answer and why

Reposition the infant supine on a bare, firm mattress

Safe sleep is supine in a bare, firm crib with no co-sleeping, and an unsafe sleep surface is an active airway risk — remove it now, then teach. Teaching later does not protect the infant currently in the crib.

A nurse is counseling a 16-year-old who has just been licensed to drive. Which instruction is the priority?

  1. Keep an emergency kit with flares and a blanket in the trunk
  2. Verify that his insurance card is in the glove compartment
  3. Never drive after using alcohol or drugs, and never ride with a driver who has
  4. Install a GPS app so his parents can locate the car
Check the answer and why

Never drive after using alcohol or drugs, and never ride with a driver who has

Unintentional injury — motor vehicle crashes above all — is the leading cause of adolescent death , with alcohol and drugs a major contributor. Emergency kits, insurance and GPS are reasonable, but none of them prevents the crash.

Guillain-Barré Syndrome ⬆️

GROUND-UP paralysis — legs first, diaphragm next. Airway is the whole game.

NG-245 NEURO · PERIPHERAL DEMYELINATION ADHD-friendly visual edition

An infection ends. A week or two later the immune system, still hunting the bug, attacks something that looks like the bug — the myelin on your peripheral nerves. Weakness starts in the feet and climbs. It is usually reversible, so the entire nursing job is keeping the patient alive and un-harmed while it climbs, plateaus and retreats. The one thing that kills: the paralysis reaching the diaphragm.

THE NERVE & WHAT ATTACKS IT

STEP 1 · CAUSE

Guillain-Barré is an autoimmune attack on the insulation of the peripheral nerves — not the brain, not the cord.

🧬 Healthy nerve vs Guillain-Barré nerve

Myelin is insulation. It lets the impulse leap from node to node instead of crawling. Strip it and the signal slows, then blocks completely — the muscle never gets the message.

myelinaxon & nodesdestroyed myelin
🧠 Myelin = the plastic on the wire. Strip the plastic, the current leaks away.

📖 Definition in one line

An acute, autoimmune, inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy of the PERIPHERAL nervous system.

  • Acute — hours to days, not years
  • Autoimmune — your own antibodies
  • Demyelinating — the sheath, not the wire
  • Polyneuropathy — many nerves, both sides, symmetrical
🧠 A-I-D-P — the formal name (acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy) is the definition read aloud.

🦠 It follows an infection

60–70% of patients had an infection 1–3 weeks before the weakness started. Always ask: “Were you sick recently?”

  • Campylobacter jejuni — the classic; undercooked poultry, gastroenteritis
  • Respiratory viruses / influenza-like illness
  • CMV, Epstein-Barr, Mycoplasma
  • Occasionally after surgery or vaccination (rare)
🧠 “The bug is long gone — the antibodies stayed.” The diarrhea has stopped by the time the legs give out.

🎭 Molecular mimicry — the actual mechanism

The immune system is not broken; it is fooled. The sugar coat on Campylobacter looks almost identical to gangliosides sitting on human myelin, so the antibodies built to kill the bug also fit the nerve.

🧠 Mistaken identity. The immune system arrests the nerve because it is wearing the same coat as the criminal.

⭐ Peripheral, NOT central

This is the single most useful sorting question on an exam.

  • GBS → peripheral nerves & roots → Schwann cells
  • MS → brain & spinal cord → oligodendrocytes

Because Schwann cells can rebuild myelin, GBS is usually reversible. CNS scars in MS are not.

SEE ALSONG-253 · Multiple Sclerosis — the CNS version of the same idea.
🧠 S for Schwann, S for Surface — GBS is on the OUTSIDE.

🧠 GBS vs MS — where the myelin dies

Guillain-Barré (NG-245)Multiple sclerosis (NG-253)
Peripheral nerves & rootsCentral — brain, cord, optic nerve
Schwann cell myelinOligodendrocyte myelin

🧠 GBS = Gets Better Slowly. MS = More Scars.

🧪 The lab fingerprint

Lumbar puncture shows albuminocytologic dissociation — high protein with a normal cell count.

It usually takes 1–2 weeks to appear, so an early normal LP does not rule GBS out.

Nerve conduction studies show slowed conduction / conduction block — the picture in the top diagram.

🧠 “Protein up, cells calm.” Lots of debris, no infection — that is why it is not meningitis.

❌ What GBS is NOT

  • Not a stroke — a stroke is sudden and one-sided; GBS is symmetrical.
  • Not contagious — you cannot catch GBS from the patient.
  • Not primarily a sensory disease — but paraesthesia and pain are common and under-treated.
  • Not a loss of consciousness — the patient is awake and aware the whole time.

Never assume a fully paralyzed GBS patient cannot hear you.

🧠 Locked in, listening. Talk to them, explain everything, set up a blink/eye code.

ASCENDING — READ THE PATTERN

STEP 2 · CLUES

Symmetrical, ascending, areflexic. If it starts at the top or only on one side, think again.

⬆️ Which way it moves, and how it recovers

The direction is the exam question. Weakness climbs: feet → legs → hips → trunk → diaphragm → arms → face and swallowing. Recovery runs the film backwards — head and arms first, feet last.

Side note (newer guidance): the order is a usual pattern, not a fixed ladder. Breathing can weaken before the arms do, so check it early, and recovery takes weeks to months. For the exam, your textbook stands: weakness ascends, and strength usually returns from the top down.
🧠 “GROUND-up Barré.” It starts on the ground and climbs. Recovery falls back down.

⭐ The classic triad

  • 1. Ascending weakness — legs first
  • 2. Symmetrical — both sides equally
  • 3. Areflexia — deep tendon reflexes absent or markedly reduced

All three together in a patient who was well last month is GBS until proven otherwise.

🧠 A-S-A: Ascending, Symmetrical, Areflexic.

🔨 Why the knee-jerk vanishes

A reflex needs an intact loop: sensory nerve in, cord, motor nerve out. Demyelination anywhere on that loop breaks it. Absent reflexes with normal consciousness is a very strong GBS clue.

🧠 Broken loop = no kick. Check reflexes on every shift and chart the level.

🦶 First complaints you will actually hear

  • “My feet feel like pins and needles / like they are asleep”
  • “My legs are heavy — I keep tripping on the stairs”
  • “I can't get out of the chair without pushing”
  • Deep aching pain in the back and thighs, worse at night

Sensory tingling often arrives before the weakness.

🧠 Tingle → trip → can't stand → can't breathe. That is the whole timeline in four steps.

🚨 Signs it has reached the danger zone

These are the early respiratory failure signs — long before the sats drop.

  • Can't cough — a weak, wet, ineffective cough NCLEX TIP
  • Can't lift the head off the pillow NCLEX TIP
  • Can't raise the eyebrows / weak facial movement
  • Shallow, rapid, abdominal breathing; talking in short phrases
  • Restlessness and anxiety — the earliest hypoxia sign

Never wait for a falling SpO₂ — by then the reserve is gone.

🧠 HEAD + COUGH. If they cannot lift their head or cough, the diaphragm is next.

Every priority question on this topic is really the question: has the paralysis reached the muscles of breathing?

🧠 Airway beats everything. Numbness is not an emergency. A weak cough is.

😐 Cranial nerve involvement

When it climbs high enough it takes the cranial nerves too:

  • Facial weakness — often both sides (unlike Bell's palsy, which is one side)
  • Dysphagia — aspiration risk; swallow screen before anything by mouth
  • Dysarthria — slurred, breathy speech
  • Eye movement problems in some variants
SEE ALSONG-237 — one-sided facial droop is a different problem.
🧠 Bilateral face = think GBS. One-sided face = think Bell's or stroke.

📈 Autonomic instability

The autonomic nerves are peripheral nerves too — so they get demyelinated as well. This is what causes sudden deaths in patients whose breathing was fine.

🧠 The thermostat is broken. Expect swings, and never over-treat a single reading.

📉 GBS can disrupt automatic controls

Move these patients slowly, pre-oxygenate before suctioning (suction can trigger a vagal bradycardia), and treat blood pressure swings cautiously — the next swing may go the other way.

🧠 Don't chase the number, watch the pattern. Aggressive treatment of one high BP can drop them into the floor five minutes later.
🚨

THE AIRWAY IS THE PRIORITY

STEP 3 · EMERGENCY

Everything else on this page can wait. This cannot.

🫁 Watch breathing before oxygen falls

Roughly 1 in 3 patients with GBS need mechanical ventilation at some point. The nursing job is to spot the slide before it becomes a crash intubation.

Side note (newer guidance): pulse oximetry can look fine until the breathing muscles are close to failing, so trend vital capacity and NIF instead. The 20/30/40 numbers prompt urgent assessment; they are not an automatic rule on their own.
🧠 C3, 4, 5 keep the diaphragm alive. When the wave reaches the neck, the breathing goes.

🚨 Priority nursing action

Prepare for mechanical ventilation. For a worsening GBS patient the correct answer is almost always airway readiness, not comfort or mobility.

  • Intubation setup AT THE BEDSIDE — not down the hall
  • Ventilator available; notify respiratory therapy early
  • Suction set up and working
  • Bag-valve-mask with oxygen at the head of the bed
  • Consider a higher level of care before the crisis
🧠 Set it up while they can still talk to you.

🧪 Numbers that decide

Vital capacity and negative inspiratory force are the bedside tests — they fall long before the pulse oximeter reacts.

  • Normal VC ≈ 65–75 mL/kg
  • Commonly taught worry threshold: VC < 20 mL/kg
  • NIF weaker (less negative) than −30 cmH₂O
  • Maximal expiratory pressure < 40 cmH₂O

Thresholds vary between facilities — follow your unit protocol; the trend matters more than one value.

🧠 20 / 30 / 40. VC under 20 mL/kg, NIF weaker (less negative) than −30 cmH₂O, MEP under 40 cmH₂O → prepare for intubation.

❌ Oxygen is not the fix

This is pump failure, not gas-exchange failure. The lungs are fine — the muscle driving them is not.

Oxygen will keep the saturation looking normal while the CO₂ climbs and the patient tires out. Never let a normal SpO₂ reassure you in GBS.

🧠 Good sats, bad breathing. Trust the vital capacity, not the oximeter.

✅ Positioning & feeding safety

  • Head of bed up — 30–45° unless contraindicated
  • NPO until a swallow screen is passed; involve speech therapy early
  • Suction within reach at all times
  • Chest physiotherapy / incentive spirometry while they still can
  • Reposition slowly — sudden movement can drop the blood pressure
🧠 Up, screened, suction ready.

🗣️ Communication when they cannot speak

A ventilated GBS patient may be fully alert with no way to move. Set up a communication method before they lose the ability.

  • Agree a blink code early: one blink = yes, two = no
  • Letter board or eye-gaze board at the bedside
  • Call bell they can actually activate — ask what still works
  • Orient, explain and warn before every touch
🧠 Plan the code on day one. By day four there may be no way to negotiate one.

TREATMENT & DAILY NURSING CARE

STEP 4 · CARE

Two disease-modifying options, and a long list of things that go wrong if nobody prevents them.

💉 Treatment: plasma exchange or IVIG

Plasma exchange physically removes the circulating antibodies that are eating the myelin. Most effective when started early — within the first couple of weeks of symptoms.

Side note (newer guidance): IVIG calms the immune attack (it does more than dilute antibodies), and plasma exchange and IVIG are alternatives, one or the other, not routinely both. Steroids do not help GBS.
  • Needs large-bore vascular access — monitor the site
  • Watch for hypotension during and after treatment
  • Watch for hypocalcemia from citrate anticoagulant — tingling around the mouth, tetany, positive Chvostek / Trousseau
  • Clotting factors are removed too → bleeding risk; check for bruising and oozing
  • Infection risk from the line
🧠 Wash the blood. The antibodies go in the bin; the cells come home.

💊 IVIG — flood the system instead

High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin gives the immune system so much borrowed antibody that it stops attacking the nerve. Equally effective as plasmapheresis; often easier to give.

  • Check renal function before giving — IVIG carries a renal risk
  • Hydrate and infuse at the ordered rate — do not speed it up
  • Watch for headache, flushing, chills, fever, myalgia during infusion
  • Risk of thrombosis and, rarely, aseptic meningitis
  • Check for IgA deficiency before the first dose (anaphylaxis risk)
🧠 IVIG = “I.V. In place of Getting washed.” Same goal, different route.

⚖️ Plasmapheresis vs IVIG

PlasmapheresisIVIG
Removes antibodiesDilutes / neutralizes them
Needs a large-bore line + machinePeripheral IV is often enough
Hypotension, hypocalcemia, bleedingRenal impairment, headache, thrombosis
Removes clotting factors and drugsDoes not remove anything
Equally effective — do NOT combine them routinely
🧠 Take it out (PLEX) or drown it out (IVIG) — pick one.

❌ Steroids are not the answer here

Unlike an MS relapse, corticosteroids alone are not recommended for Guillain-Barré — they have not been shown to improve the outcome.

That contrast is worth memorizing because it catches people out.

🧠 MS relapse → steroids. GBS → PLEX or IVIG.

🩸 VTE prevention is not optional

A paralyzed, immobile patient is a clot waiting to happen — and a pulmonary embolus in someone with a vital capacity of 15 mL/kg is catastrophic.

  • Prophylactic anticoagulation as ordered
  • Sequential compression devices / graduated stockings
  • Passive range of motion every shift
  • Assess calves, but do not massage a suspicious leg
🧠 Still legs clot. Move the joints even when the muscles cannot.

🛏️ Immobility care bundle

  • Skin — turn q2h, pressure-redistributing surface, heels off the bed
  • Contractures — splints, footboard, passive ROM, physio daily
  • Eyes — if the face is weak, lubricate and protect (they may not blink fully)
  • Bowel — ileus is common; listen, and use a bowel regimen
  • Bladder — retention is common; bladder scan rather than assume
  • Nutrition — high energy needs; enteral feeding if swallowing is unsafe
🧠 Everything that touches the bed will break if you don't move it.

💊 Pain is real and often missed

Deep, aching, neuropathic pain in the back and legs affects most patients and is worse at night. A patient who cannot move may not be able to show you pain.

  • Use a scale they can answer — blinks, eye gaze, a pain thermometer
  • Neuropathic agents (e.g. gabapentin) are commonly used
  • Repositioning, warmth and massage help
  • Watch autonomic responses — a BP or HR spike may be the only pain signal
🧠 Silent does not mean comfortable.

🧯 Preventing the complications that actually kill

🧠 They rarely die of the nerve disease — they die of what immobility does to them.

RECOVERY & WHAT TO TELL THEM

STEP 5 · TEACH

This is one of the few devastating neuro diagnoses with a genuinely hopeful answer.

✅ The honest prognosis

  • Symptoms usually progress for up to ~4 weeks, then plateau
  • Plateau lasts days to weeks
  • Recovery takes weeks to months, sometimes 1–2 years
  • The majority regain the ability to walk
  • A minority are left with residual weakness, numbness or fatigue
  • Recurrence is uncommon
🧠 Up fast, down slow. It arrives in days and leaves over months.

🔁 Recovery goes the OTHER way

Strength comes back descending — head and shoulders first, feet last. Tell the patient this on day one so that regaining a smile before regaining a step feels like progress, not failure.

🧠 Last in, first out. The last muscles to be paralyzed are the first to recover.

💪 Rehab is the long game

  • Physiotherapy and occupational therapy from the ICU onward
  • Pace activity — fatigue is the most persistent leftover symptom
  • Assistive devices, home modification, driving reassessment
  • Return to work is often gradual and part-time first
  • Psychological support — ICU delirium and PTSD are common after this illness
🧠 The muscles come back before the stamina does.

📞 Call the provider for…

  • Any new difficulty breathing, or a weak/ineffective cough
  • Difficulty swallowing, choking or a wet voice
  • Fainting, palpitations or big blood pressure swings
  • New weakness after starting to improve
  • Fever or signs of infection
🧠 Breathe, swallow, faint. Three words to send home with the family.

🧠 The five things students get wrong

Trap 1Calling it a CNS disease. It is peripheral.
Trap 2Saying it descends. GBS ascends; myasthenia gravis descends.
Trap 3Reassured by a normal SpO₂. Trend the vital capacity.
Trap 4Giving steroids as the main treatment. It is PLEX or IVIG.
Trap 5Assuming the patient is unconscious. They are awake.

Myasthenia Gravis ⬇️

Droopy eyes first · worse with USE · better with REST · airway is still the priority

NG-251 NEURO · NEUROMUSCULAR JUNCTION ADHD-friendly visual edition

The nerve is fine. The muscle is fine. The handshake between them is broken — antibodies have blocked and destroyed the acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction. So the first few contractions work, and then the muscle simply stops answering. That is why myasthenia gravis worsens with activity and improves with rest, and why the weakness starts at the top — the droopy eyelid — and works downward. It is the exact mirror image of Guillain-Barré, which is why the two get confused.

THE JUNCTION & WHAT BREAKS IT

STEP 1 · CAUSE

One picture explains this entire disease: the receptors on the muscle side of the synapse are being destroyed.

🔌 Normal junction vs myasthenic junction

The nerve still releases plenty of acetylcholine. The problem is on the other side of the cleft: antibodies plug the receptors, destroy them, and flatten the junctional folds, so most of the ACh floats past and finds nothing to bind.

ACh (the signal)ACh receptorauto-antibody / destroyed receptor
🧠 The lock is broken, not the key. Plenty of keys (ACh), too few locks (receptors).

📖 Definition in one line

A chronic autoimmune disorder of the neuromuscular junction in which antibodies attack the nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on the muscle membrane.

  • Autoimmune — your own IgG
  • Postsynaptic — the muscle side, not the nerve
  • Fluctuating — better and worse hour to hour
  • A subset have MuSK antibodies instead of AChR antibodies
🧠 MG = Muscles Give up. They work once, then quit.

🫧 Why it fades with use

A healthy junction has a huge safety margin — far more receptors than it needs. Normally the amount of ACh released falls a little with repeated firing, and nobody notices.

In MG the margin is gone. That same small drop-off is now enough to fail, so the tenth contraction is much weaker than the first.

🧠 No spare change. A healthy junction has money left over; an MG junction spends it on contraction one.

🧪 How it is diagnosed

  • AChR antibody blood test — the main confirmatory test (MuSK antibody if negative)
  • Repetitive nerve stimulation / single-fiber EMG — shows a decremental response, the fatigue drawn as a graph
  • Ice-pack test — cooling the eyelid briefly improves ptosis
  • Edrophonium (Tensilon) test — historic: if strength improves, it points to MG. Rarely used now
  • Chest CT/MRI — looking for a thymoma
🧠 Antibody, EMG, ice, imaging. Blood test first, picture of the chest last.

🫀 The thymus connection

🧠 Thymus = the training school that started teaching the wrong lesson.

👥 Who gets it

  • Classic bimodal pattern: women in their 20s–30s and men over 50–60
  • Not inherited in a simple way; not contagious
  • Often coexists with other autoimmune disease (thyroid disease, rheumatoid arthritis)
  • A transient neonatal form can occur in babies of mothers with MG — antibodies cross the placenta
🧠 Young women, older men. Two humps on the age curve.

DESCENDING — READ THE PATTERN

STEP 2 · CLUES

Eyes first, then the muscles you eat and talk with, then the limbs, then breathing.

⬇️ MG vs GBS: compare the pattern

If you only remember one thing from this page and NG-245, remember the arrows. Myasthenia gravis starts at the eyelids and descends. Guillain-Barré starts at the feet and ascends. Both can reach the diaphragm — which is why both are airway emergencies.

Side note (newer guidance): MG weakness fluctuates and is not a fixed downward march, and either disease can weaken breathing. For the exam, your textbook stands: MG starts at the eyes and face and descends; GBS starts at the feet and ascends.
PAIR THIS WITHNG-245 · Guillain-Barré — learn them together or you will keep swapping them.
🧠 MG = My Gaze goes first. GBS = Ground-up.

😴 The classic first signs — and the fatigue test

Ptosis and diplopia are ocular signs and are the presenting complaint in most patients. Ask about the time of day: “Is your vision worse in the evening?” A yes is worth more than any single exam finding.

🧠 Droopy in the evening, fine after a nap.

⭐ The four D's of bulbar weakness

  • Diplopia — double vision
  • Dysphagia — trouble swallowing (aspiration risk)
  • Dysarthria — slurred, nasal, fading speech
  • Dyspnea — the one that kills

Add ptosis and you have the whole classic presentation.

🧠 PTOSIS + 4 D's. Droop, then the four D words.

📉 Worse with activity, better with rest

This is the single most diagnostic feature. It shapes everything you do: schedule the most demanding activities in the morning, build rest periods in, and time meals to the medication.

🧠 A battery that will not hold charge. Full in the morning, flat by night, back up after a nap.

🗣️ Bedside signs you can actually elicit

  • Ask them to look up at your finger for 60 seconds — the lid slowly droops
  • Ask them to count aloud to 50 — the voice fades and turns nasal
  • Ask them to hold their arms out — they drift down within a minute
  • Watch them chew — jaw fatigue makes them stop mid-meal and support the jaw with a hand
  • Ask them to lift their head off the pillow — head drop is neck extensor weakness
🧠 Make them repeat it. Any test that involves repetition will show MG.

❌ What MG does NOT do

  • No sensory loss — no numbness, no tingling. It is purely motor.
  • No pain as a primary feature.
  • Reflexes are normal — unlike GBS where they disappear.
  • Pupils are normal in MG itself (pinpoint pupils suggest cholinergic excess).
  • Cognition is normal.

Never explain a numb, tingling patient with myasthenia gravis.

🧠 Pure motor, pure fatigue. If you feel it, it is not MG.

THE TWO CRISES

STEP 3 · EMERGENCY

Both look like sudden severe weakness. One is too little drug; the other is too much.

🚨 MG crisis: breathing comes first

The therapeutic window for anticholinesterase drugs is narrow. Fall off the left edge and the myasthenia takes over; fall off the right edge and the drug itself paralyzes the junction.

Side note (newer guidance): myasthenic crisis is not only under-dosing (infection and some medicines trigger it too), and cholinergic excess is uncommon. Dry versus wet is a study clue, not a dosing rule: weak breathing or swallowing means get help now, and the provider decides the medication.
🧠 Too little = MyastheNIC. Too much = CHOLINergic (CHOLINE overload).

🚨 Myasthenic crisis

Not enough acetylcholine effect. Severe weakness including the muscles of breathing.

Triggers: missed or late doses, infection, fever, surgery, pregnancy/childbirth, emotional stress, tapering immunosuppressants, and certain drugs.

  • Weakness worsens over hours to days
  • Weak cough, shallow breathing, cannot clear secretions
  • Cannot swallow — high aspiration risk
  • Often tachycardic, anxious, restless

Treatment is supportive airway management plus IVIG or plasmapheresis, and treating the trigger.

🧠 Missed a dose, caught a bug. That is the myasthenic crisis story.

⚠️ Cholinergic crisis

Too much acetylcholine effect from overmedication. The junction is over-stimulated into depolarizing block — and the result is, confusingly, the same weakness.

The difference is the extra cholinergic excess signs:

  • Salivation · Lacrimation · Urination · Defecation · GI cramping · Emesis SLUDGE
  • Miosis — pinpoint pupils
  • Bradycardia
  • Muscle twitching and fasciculations
  • Sweating, bronchial secretions, wheeze

Treatment: stop the anticholinesterase, support the airway, and atropine is the antidote for the muscarinic effects.

🧠 Wet everywhere, small pupils, slow heart. That is too much drug.

🔍 Telling them apart — carefully

The edrophonium (Tensilon) distinction is a classic exam point but the test is now rarely performed in practice — it carried its own bradycardia risk. In real life the decision is made on the history, the secretions, the pupils and the heart rate.

🧠 DRY = need more. WET = had too much.

🚨 Whichever crisis it is — do this first

1
Airway & breathing. Assess the cough, the ability to speak in full sentences, the vital capacity. Suction and an intubation set-up at the bedside.
▼
2
Position & protect. Upright, NPO until a swallow is confirmed safe, oxygen, continuous monitoring.
▼
3
Call for help early and get them to a higher level of care before the crash.
▼
4
Then work out which crisis it is — medication history, secretions, pupils, heart rate.

Never delay airway management to decide which crisis it is.

🧠 Breathe them first, label them second.

⭐ “Which client do I see first?”

The classic stem: an MG patient with difficulty swallowing and a temperature of 100.7 °F.

See that one first. Two reasons stack up:

  • Dysphagia = an airway/aspiration problem right now
  • Fever/infection is the commonest trigger of myasthenic crisis

Teach patients to report a temperature over about 100.4 °F (38 °C) — thresholds vary, so use the one their team gives them.

🧠 Can't swallow + fever = crisis brewing.

DRUGS, TIMING & SAFETY

STEP 4 · CARE

The medication is only as good as the clock it is given by.

💊 What pyridostigmine actually does

It is an anticholinesterase: it blocks the enzyme that destroys acetylcholine, so the ACh stays in the cleft longer and gets more chances to find one of the surviving receptors. It treats the symptom, not the autoimmunity.

🧠 -STIGMINE adds Secretions. Every drug ending in “-stigmine” makes the body wetter.

⏰ TIMING is the nursing intervention

Give anticholinesterase doses ON TIME — this is one of the few drugs where a “late” dose is a genuine safety event.

  • Schedule the dose before meals — commonly 30–60 min beforehand — so the patient is strong enough to chew and swallow
  • Do not hold it for a routine reason without asking
  • Peri-operatively, clarify the plan for doses in advance
  • Patients should carry a supply and a medic-alert bracelet

Never give the dose after the meal tray has arrived and call it done.

🧠 Dose, then dinner. Never dinner, then dose.

💊 The rest of the drug list

  • Corticosteroids (e.g. prednisone) — dampen the immune attack. Watch glucose, infection risk, mood, bone health. Symptoms can briefly worsen when steroids are started
  • Immunosuppressants (e.g. azathioprine, mycophenolate) — steroid-sparing, slow to work, monitor blood counts and liver function
  • IVIG or plasmapheresis — rapid, short-term rescue for crisis or pre-operatively
  • Thymectomy — surgical, benefit builds over months
🧠 Symptom drug + immune drug + rescue. Three layers of treatment.

⚠️ Drugs that can make myasthenia worse

Several common medicines interfere with neuromuscular transmission. These are commonly cited cautions — always check the current reference and the patient's own list, and involve pharmacy.

💉Aminoglycosidesgentamicin etc.
🧂IV magnesiumblocks ACh release
🦠Fluoroquinolonescaution
💓Beta blockerscaution
🫀Some antiarrhythmicse.g. procainamide

Anesthesia teams must know the patient has MG — sensitivity to neuromuscular blocking agents is markedly increased.

🧠 “Mycins and magnesium.” Two triggers worth memorizing.

🍽️ Swallowing safety & the timed meal

Aspiration is the commonest avoidable harm on this diagnosis. Every meal is an airway event for a patient with bulbar weakness.

🧠 Upright, small bites, semi-solid, suction ready — and the dose already on board.

✅ Everyday nursing priorities

  • Assess respiratory status and swallow every shift and before every meal
  • Cluster care and plan rest periods; big activities in the morning
  • Eye care — artificial tears and a patch/tape if the lid will not close
  • Alternate the eye patch side if diplopia is being managed that way
  • Fall precautions — proximal weakness and diplopia both cause falls
  • Communication plan if speech fatigues
🧠 Breathe, swallow, rest, protect the eyes.

LIVING WITH IT — WHAT TO TEACH

STEP 5 · TEACH

MG is chronic and manageable. Most of the danger comes from triggers that can be planned around.

🧠 AVOID THE 4 S's — flare triggers

  • Stress — physical or emotional; also exercise, surgery, pregnancy
  • Sun & heat — hot showers, hot weather, saunas
  • Smoking — and second-hand smoke
  • Sickness / Sepsis — any infection

Add a fifth that matters just as much: skipped doses.

🧠 Stress · Sun · Smoke · Sickness — the 4 S's that trigger a flare.

✅ Infection prevention

  • Avoid crowds during cold and flu season
  • Keep vaccinations up to date (influenza, pneumococcal, per provider advice)
  • Hand hygiene; avoid people who are obviously unwell
  • Report fever, cough or urinary symptoms early — infection is the top crisis trigger
  • Extra caution if on immunosuppressants
🧠 An ordinary cold is not ordinary here.

🪪 Safety kit

  • Medic-alert bracelet at all times — this is a select-all-that-apply favorite
  • A current medication list in the wallet/phone, including drugs to avoid
  • Emergency plan: who to call, where to go, what to say (“I have myasthenia gravis and I cannot breathe well”)
  • Never let the prescription run out — plan refills ahead of holidays
🧠 Bracelet, list, plan, supply.

🗓️ Energy budgeting

  • Do the hardest things first thing in the morning
  • Plan a rest before the fatigue, not after
  • Sit to shower, dress, cook and do hair
  • Break tasks into chunks with breaks built in
  • Ask for help with things done above the head (hair, high shelves) — arms fatigue fastest
🧠 Spend the battery on what matters, then plug in.

👁️ Eye and vision teaching

  • Ptosis may need a lid crutch on glasses
  • An eye patch (alternating eyes) can relieve double vision
  • Do not drive while diplopia is uncontrolled
  • Artificial tears if the lids do not close fully
🧠 One eye covered = one image.

🧠 The five things students get wrong

Trap 1Saying MG ascends. It descends — eyes first.
Trap 2Thinking rest makes it worse. Rest improves it; activity worsens it.
Trap 3Giving pyridostigmine after the meal. Give it before.
Trap 4Mixing up the crises. Dry & tachycardic = too little; wet, pinpoint & bradycardic = too much.
Trap 5Expecting numbness. MG is purely motor — sensation and reflexes are normal.

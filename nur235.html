🎮 Pediatric Matching Games

187 cards, seven topics. Six pairs a round, on a stopwatch — a wrong match adds a second. Every match comes with an explanation and a source. Pick a board:

  • MilestonesCDC checklist ages (24)
  • Piagetcognitive stages, ages approximate (16)
  • Eriksonall eight psychosocial stages (21)
  • ImmunizationsCDC 2025 study edition (16)
  • Play typessix types, scenarios and age bands (24)
  • Vital signsHR, RR, BP and temperature by age (56)
  • Pain scalesFLACC, FACES, numeric, NIPS, FPS-R (30)

Milestones are CDC checklist ages — the age by which most children have the skill, not the average age they get it.

The vital signs are Fuller’s course chart. That is course recall, not emergency thresholds and not a universal clinical normal. Temperatures are axillary.

On pain, the game labels both rules. Fuller puts numeric ratings at over age 5 on cognitive level; RCH uses over 7. Neither is quietly substituted for the other.

Hints and the answer guides count as help, not a first-try hit. Lecturer and source names stay off the tiles — they are in the answer guide.

🎧 Exam 2 exam-prep recording

What Dr. Hansen actually said in the review session for this exam — her numbers, her worked calculations, her priority rulings. On an exam, this beats the textbook.

  • Assess first: sickle cell with sudden chest pain, fever and dyspnea = acute chest syndrome. It outranks pallor in iron-deficiency anemia, a mild post-bump knee swelling in hemophilia, and a sore throat in mono — all expected findings.
  • A mild cold or runny nose is NOT a reason to defer MMR. Only moderate-to-severe illness postpones it. Mild rash or fever afterward is expected. Second dose at 4–6 years.
  • Measles: airborne precautions plus supportive care and fluids. Antibiotics only for secondary bacterial infection. Restrict unvaccinated and high-risk visitors, not all visitors.
  • Allergy skin testing: hold antihistamines 5–7 days (longer per provider). Itching during and after is expected. Acetaminophen is neither required nor helpful.
  • Pediatric HIV: persistent diarrhea with poor weight gain is the finding needing immediate action — failure to thrive, malabsorption, opportunistic infection. A mild rash, an occasional clear-sputum cough and past ear infections are lower priority.
  • Pinworms: treat every household member regardless of symptoms, then repeat the dose in about 2 weeks to kill newly hatched worms. Hot-wash bedding and pajamas daily; short fingernails and hand hygiene break the cycle.
  • Infant eczema: daily bathing with soap makes it worse. Bathe a few times weekly with minimal mild soap, fragrance-free moisturizer twice daily, cotton clothing, nursery humidifier.
  • Thrombocytopenia: the highest-priority intervention is a soft toothbrush — gum and mucosal bleeding is the daily repeated exposure. Avoid aspirin and NSAIDs; acetaminophen is the analgesic of choice.
  • Infant raised ICP looks different: bulging fontanelle plus a high-pitched cry, because open sutures change the picture. Non-specific irritability, mild forehead bruising and sleeping after a feed with normal vitals are not it.
  • Myelomeningocele: sterile, moist, non-adherent dressing over the sac, prone or side-lying. Supine risks rupture. ROM and feeding are deferred.
  • Baclofen for spastic CP: daytime drowsiness that interferes with physical therapy → notify the provider for a dose review. Nurses do not stop or re-time it, and abrupt discontinuation causes rebound spasticity and withdrawal.
  • Post-op supratentorial brain tumor: monitor for raised ICP, HOB elevated 30°. Not flat, not early ambulation, not scheduled q2h opioids.
  • Calc: ceftriaxone 600 mg from 300 mg/mL over 30 min → volume 2 mL, rate 2 ÷ 0.5 = 4 mL/hr.
  • Calc: acetaminophen 15 mg/kg, 18 kg, 160 mg/5 mL → 270 mg → 8.4 mL. Do not round up — the digit after the tenths place is 3.

The child with a respiratory disorder

Given age, respiratory signs and intake, connect the airway problem to findings, prioritize oxygenation/airway and hydration, implement indicated treatment and reassess work of breathing, alertness and feeding.

  • Tonsillitis / tonsillectomy

  • Viral and bacterial pharyngitis

Bacterial epiglottitis
Simple Nursing handoutAirway picmonic
Causes and risk factors
Bacterial infection, historically Hib but also other bacteria, can cause epiglottic swelling; incomplete Hib vaccination increases preventable risk.
What changes in the body
Rapid supraglottic inflammation threatens airflow through a child’s already small airway.
Diagnostic evaluation
Treat suspected epiglottitis as an airway emergency: diagnosis and any imaging or visualization belong in a controlled setting with airway expertise, without delaying airway stabilization.
Clinical findings
High fever, severe sore throat, drooling, dysphagia, muffled voice, tripod posture and respiratory distress distinguish it from typical barking croup.
Medical management
An expert airway team may secure the airway; give oxygen as tolerated and prescribed IV antibiotics and fluids after the airway plan is established.
Nutrition and fluids
Keep oral food, drink and medicines withheld while swallowing and airway safety are uncertain; use prescribed IV hydration.
Nursing priorities
Keep the child calm, upright in the position of comfort and with a caregiver; activate emergency airway help and do not force a throat examination or swab.
Child and family teaching
Explain why agitation and throat manipulation are dangerous, review Hib immunization and teach urgent action for recurrent drooling or breathing difficulty.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 40: Child with a Respiratory Disorder

Asthma
Causes and risk factors
Family history, atopy, viral respiratory infections, tobacco smoke, allergens, exercise and pollution can contribute to asthma or trigger symptoms.
What changes in the body
Airway inflammation, bronchial smooth-muscle constriction and mucus cause variable expiratory airflow obstruction and air trapping.
Diagnostic evaluation
Use symptom and trigger history with spirometry and bronchodilator response when developmentally feasible; peak flow tracks control and does not replace diagnostic assessment.
Clinical findings
Recurrent cough, wheeze, prolonged expiration, chest tightness and activity limitation are common; exhaustion, reduced air entry or a silent chest with distress signal severe obstruction.
Medical management
Acute care treats hypoxemia and obstruction with oxygen when indicated, a prescribed rapid bronchodilator and systemic corticosteroids when indicated; ongoing control uses an individualized anti-inflammatory plan.
Nutrition and fluids
No special asthma diet is routine: maintain age-appropriate nutrition and hydration, addressing documented food allergy or weight concerns without unnecessary restrictions.
Nursing priorities
Assess work of breathing, oxygenation, air entry and alertness; position comfortably, give ordered treatment promptly and reassess response, escalating for poor response or fatigue.
Child and family teaching
Have child and caregiver demonstrate spacer/inhaler technique and the written action plan, identify reliever versus controller, rinse after ICS, avoid smoke and arrange school access to prescribed rescue medicine.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 40: Child with a Respiratory Disorder · NHLBI: Asthma Care Quick Reference

Nasopharyngitis (common cold)
Causes and risk factors
Respiratory viruses spread through close contacts; childcare exposure and immature immunity contribute to frequent childhood infections.
What changes in the body
Inflamed nasal and pharyngeal mucosa produces edema and secretions that interfere with nasal breathing, sleep and infant feeding.
Diagnostic evaluation
An uncomplicated cold is usually diagnosed clinically; testing is reserved for findings suggesting another infection or complication.
Clinical findings
Congestion, rhinorrhea, sneezing, cough and low fever are common, with irritability and reduced feeding in infants.
Medical management
Care is supportive; use appropriate analgesic/antipyretic treatment for discomfort, and antibiotics only for a separately diagnosed bacterial complication.
Nutrition and fluids
Offer frequent usual milk feeds or age-appropriate fluids and foods; clear nasal secretions before feeds when congestion impairs intake.
Nursing priorities
Assess breathing and hydration, use saline and gentle nasal suction when needed, and evaluate persistent/worsening fever, ear pain or respiratory distress.
Child and family teaching
Teach hand hygiene, smoke avoidance, safe medicine measurement and avoidance of unapproved cough/cold products; explain that colored mucus alone does not establish a bacterial infection.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 40: Child with a Respiratory Disorder · CDC: Pediatric outpatient antibiotic guidance

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD)

A very preterm infant may still need breathing support weeks after birth. Look for fast breathing, inward pulling between or below the ribs, tiring during feeds and slow weight gain.

Assess the infant promptly for deeper retractions or rising oxygen needs. Apnea, severe distress or poor responsiveness needs emergency assistance.

Bronchiolitis (often RSV)
Causes and risk factors
RSV and other viruses most often affect infants and children under two; young age, prematurity and underlying heart/lung disease increase risk of severe illness.
What changes in the body
Bronchiolar edema, mucus and cellular debris obstruct small airways, producing air trapping, impaired gas exchange and feeding fatigue.
Diagnostic evaluation
Typical bronchiolitis is diagnosed from history and examination; routine chest radiographs and blood tests are usually unnecessary.
Clinical findings
A cold-like prodrome can progress to cough, wheeze/crackles, retractions and poor feeding; apnea, hypoxemia or reduced responsiveness are dangerous.
Clinical clarification for this handout

Palivizumab on the original handout reflects an earlier prevention approach and is not a complete summary of current RSV prevention. Current CDC guidance uses maternal RSV vaccination or an eligible infant's long-acting antibody, nirsevimab or clesrovimab; most infants do not need both maternal vaccination and an infant antibody. Eligibility depends on age, maternal vaccination, season, and risk, and these products prevent severe RSV disease rather than treat an active infection.

References: CDC: RSV immunization guidance for infants and young children

Medical management
Support oxygenation and hydration; routine antibiotics, bronchodilators and corticosteroids do not treat uncomplicated bronchiolitis.
Nutrition and fluids
Assess whether breathing permits safe oral feeds; offer smaller frequent feeds when safe, or prescribed enteral/IV fluids when intake is inadequate or aspiration risk is high.
Nursing priorities
Trend work of breathing, saturation, apnea, intake and urine output; use gentle upper-airway suction for obstructing secretions and escalate for exhaustion or respiratory failure.
Child and family teaching
Demonstrate secretion care, teach return precautions for apnea, poor intake or worsening effort, follow infection-control policy and review current RSV prevention eligibility.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 40: Child with a Respiratory Disorder · NICE: Bronchiolitis recommendations

Tonsillitis / tonsillectomy
Causes and risk factors
Tonsillar inflammation may be viral or bacterial; recurrent significant infections or obstructive tonsillar enlargement may prompt surgical evaluation.
What changes in the body
Inflamed lymphoid tissue causes painful swallowing and sometimes upper-airway obstruction; surgery leaves a vascular wound with bleeding risk.
Diagnostic evaluation
Assess airway, hydration and infection findings, test for group A strep when indicated, and evaluate obstructive symptoms before a planned tonsillectomy.
Clinical findings
Sore throat, fever, enlarged erythematous tonsils and dysphagia occur; after surgery, frequent swallowing, fresh blood, tachycardia or pallor can signal hemorrhage.
Medical management
Use supportive care for viral illness, antibiotics for confirmed bacterial infection and prescribed postoperative pain control; surgery is selected for specific recurrent-infection or obstruction indications.
Nutrition and fluids
Prioritize fluids, then advance to soft/nonirritating foods as tolerated and per surgeon instructions; do not teach clear liquids as the entire recovery diet.
Nursing priorities
After surgery monitor airway and bleeding, position safely during emergence, assess hydration and pain, and obtain urgent review for fresh bleeding or respiratory deterioration.
Child and family teaching
Teach scheduled prescribed pain control, hydration, activity restrictions and the surgeon’s return plan; fresh bleeding, breathing difficulty or inability to drink requires prompt evaluation.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 40: Child with a Respiratory Disorder · CDC: Group A streptococcal pharyngitis

Laryngotracheobronchitis (croup)
Causes and risk factors
Viral infection, commonly parainfluenza, most often causes croup in toddlers and preschool-aged children.
What changes in the body
Subglottic inflammation narrows the upper airway and produces turbulent inspiratory airflow.
Diagnostic evaluation
Typical croup is diagnosed clinically from cough and stridor; atypical toxicity, drooling or poor response warrants evaluation for a more serious cause.
Clinical findings
Barking cough, hoarseness and inspiratory stridor often worsen at night; stridor at rest, marked retractions or lethargy indicate greater severity.
Clinical clarification for this handout

Cold outdoor air may temporarily lessen moderate croup symptoms, but the freezer-air memory cue is not a complete treatment plan. Stridor at rest, increasing work of breathing, or deteriorating alertness requires prompt assessment and escalation. Management depends on severity and includes corticosteroids when indicated, with nebulized epinephrine and skilled airway support for severe or life-threatening illness.

References: Royal Children's Hospital: Croup guideline

Medical management
Corticosteroids reduce airway inflammation and nebulized epinephrine is used for more severe symptoms; humidified air is not an established treatment.
Nutrition and fluids
Offer small amounts of fluid when the child can safely swallow; severe distress may require withholding oral intake and prescribed IV hydration.
Nursing priorities
Keep child and caregiver together and calm, assess stridor at rest and effort, provide oxygen if needed and reassess after epinephrine because its effect can wear off.
Child and family teaching
Teach urgent review for stridor at rest, increasing work of breathing, drooling, lethargy or dehydration; avoid hot-steam treatment because benefit is unproven and burns are possible.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 40: Child with a Respiratory Disorder · Canadian Paediatric Society: Acute croup management

Viral and bacterial pharyngitis
Causes and risk factors
Viruses cause most sore throats; group A streptococcus is an important bacterial cause, especially in school-aged children with close exposure.
What changes in the body
Inflammation of the pharynx causes pain and swelling; GAS can trigger later immune-mediated rheumatic fever.
Diagnostic evaluation
Clear viral features generally need no GAS testing; when indicated use rapid antigen testing or culture and back up a negative rapid antigen test in symptomatic children aged three or older.
Clinical findings
Viral illness often includes cough, rhinorrhea or hoarseness; GAS more often causes sudden sore throat, fever, tender anterior nodes and absent cough, but findings alone are not definitive.
Medical management
Give comfort care for viral illness and prescribed penicillin/amoxicillin or an appropriate alternative for confirmed GAS.
Nutrition and fluids
Offer fluids and soft, soothing foods because painful swallowing can reduce intake; no special long-term diet is needed.
Nursing priorities
Assess airway and hydration, give analgesia and obtain indicated tests; drooling, inability to swallow, neck swelling or respiratory distress requires urgent reassessment.
Child and family teaching
Complete prescribed antibiotics, avoid sharing utensils, practice hand hygiene and follow school exclusion guidance; use gargles only when the child can do so safely.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 40: Child with a Respiratory Disorder · CDC: Group A streptococcal pharyngitis

Pneumonia
Causes and risk factors
Viruses, bacteria and atypical organisms can cause pneumonia; young age, chronic illness, aspiration risk and preceding respiratory infection influence risk and cause.
What changes in the body
Inflammation and exudate in alveoli reduce gas exchange and may cause consolidation or pleural complications.
Diagnostic evaluation
Use history, examination and oxygenation assessment; imaging and organism testing are selected for severity, uncertainty or complications rather than assumed necessary for every mild case.
Clinical findings
Fever, cough, tachypnea, retractions, crackles or diminished breath sounds may occur; children can also present with chest or abdominal pain and poor feeding.
Medical management
Provide supportive care and prescribed antibiotics for suspected/confirmed bacterial disease; oxygen, fluids and hospitalization depend on severity.
Nutrition and fluids
Offer fluids and small frequent nutritious meals as tolerated, avoiding forced oral intake during significant respiratory distress.
Nursing priorities
Monitor breathing, saturation, hydration and response to therapy; position for comfort and escalate for hypoxemia, exhaustion, sepsis or pleural complications.
Child and family teaching
Teach medicine adherence, hydration and rest, immunization/smoke-avoidance prevention and return precautions for worsening breathing or inability to maintain intake.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 40: Child with a Respiratory Disorder

Cystic fibrosis
Causes and risk factors
CF is an autosomal recessive CFTR disorder; a child is affected after inheriting disease-causing variants from both biological parents.
What changes in the body
Abnormal salt transport dehydrates secretions, obstructing airways and pancreatic ducts and contributing to infection, malabsorption and excess salt loss.
Clinical clarification for this handout

In CF, blocked pancreatic ducts keep digestive enzymes from reaching the intestine, causing malabsorption. CF-related diabetes develops mainly because pancreatic damage reduces insulin production; insulin resistance can also contribute, especially during illness or steroid treatment. Insulin is released into the blood, so the handout's description of insulin being unable to escape a mucus-blocked duct confuses endocrine insulin secretion with exocrine enzyme delivery.

References: Cystic Fibrosis Foundation: CF-related diabetes, Cystic Fibrosis Foundation: Digestive system

Diagnostic evaluation
Newborn screening can show elevated immunoreactive trypsinogen; confirm through an accredited CF evaluation with sweat chloride and/or CFTR testing, interpreting intermediate results rather than calling screening diagnostic.
Clinical findings
Recurrent respiratory symptoms, poor growth, greasy bulky stools, salty skin and sometimes newborn meconium ileus reflect multisystem involvement.
Medical management
Individualized treatment may include airway clearance, inhaled mucus therapies, antibiotics, pancreatic enzymes and eligible CFTR modulators.
Nutrition and fluids
A CF dietitian tailors energy, protein, fat, salt and fat-soluble vitamins to growth and pancreatic function; give prescribed enzymes with feeds/meals/snacks, using acidic soft food for opened beads without crushing them.
Nursing priorities
Trend respiratory status, sputum, growth, stool and hydration; coordinate airway clearance away from meals if it provokes vomiting and monitor treatment response.
Child and family teaching
Have caregivers demonstrate enzymes and airway-clearance equipment, plan school routines and supplies, follow CF infection-prevention instructions and seek review for respiratory decline or poor growth.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 40: Child with a Respiratory Disorder · Cystic Fibrosis Foundation: Enzymes · Cystic Fibrosis Foundation: Diagnosis guidelines

Influenza
Causes and risk factors
Influenza viruses spread through respiratory exposure; children under five, especially under two, and children with chronic disease have greater complication risk.
What changes in the body
Respiratory viral infection causes airway inflammation and systemic illness and can be complicated by pneumonia or secondary bacterial infection.
Diagnostic evaluation
Assess symptoms, exposure and severity; influenza testing can guide care, but high-risk or hospitalized children should not have necessary treatment delayed for results.
Clinical findings
Abrupt fever, cough, fatigue, myalgia and headache are typical; children may also have vomiting or diarrhea.
Medical management
Provide supportive care and prescribed antiviral treatment such as oseltamivir when indicated; benefit is greatest early, but severe/high-risk illness can warrant treatment after 48 hours.
Nutrition and fluids
Give frequent fluids and usual foods as tolerated, tracking urine output and intake during fever or gastrointestinal symptoms.
Nursing priorities
Assess hydration, work of breathing and oxygenation; watch for pneumonia, clinical worsening after initial improvement and inability to drink.
Child and family teaching
Review annual influenza vaccination and current infection-control guidance, teach accurate medication dosing and avoid aspirin for routine viral fever.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 40: Child with a Respiratory Disorder · CDC: Treatment of flu in children

COVID-19
Causes and risk factors
SARS-CoV-2 respiratory exposure causes COVID-19; infants and children with certain underlying conditions can have higher risk of severe illness.
What changes in the body
Viral respiratory infection ranges from mild upper-airway illness to lower-airway and systemic disease; a delayed inflammatory complication, MIS-C, is distinct from routine acute infection.
Diagnostic evaluation
Use an appropriate viral test with clinical assessment; a negative antigen result may need repeat testing or NAAT depending on the situation.
Clinical findings
Fever, cough, congestion, sore throat, fatigue and gastrointestinal symptoms can occur; respiratory distress, altered alertness or persistent fever with multisystem symptoms warrants escalation.
Medical management
Most children receive supportive care; eligible high-risk patients may receive time-sensitive antiviral treatment, chosen using current age, weight, interaction and organ-function criteria.
Nutrition and fluids
Maintain usual age-appropriate nutrition and frequent fluids; monitor intake and urine output and provide prescribed fluid support if oral intake fails.
Nursing priorities
Assess oxygenation, breathing effort, hydration and mental status, use current facility precautions and investigate deterioration or possible MIS-C promptly.
Child and family teaching
Teach current prevention/testing guidance and return precautions, including trouble breathing, dehydration or persistent fever with rash, abdominal pain or unusual fatigue after recent infection.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 40: Child with a Respiratory Disorder · Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 43: Infectious disorders · CDC: Pediatric COVID care · CDC: COVID outpatient treatment, October 8 2026

Allergic rhinitis
Causes and risk factors
Environmental allergens such as pollen, dust mites, molds and animal dander trigger symptoms in sensitized children, often with a personal/family atopic history.
What changes in the body
IgE-mediated inflammation swells nasal mucosa and increases watery secretions without being a contagious infection.
Diagnostic evaluation
Use symptom timing, exposure history and examination; targeted allergy testing helps when diagnosis, trigger control or response to treatment is uncertain.
Clinical findings
Sneezing, itchy nose/eyes, clear rhinorrhea and congestion are typical, with pale swollen mucosa, allergic shiners and disturbed sleep or school function.
Medical management
Use environmental control and age-appropriate prescribed intranasal corticosteroids or antihistamines; selected persistent disease may warrant allergy specialist care.
Nutrition and fluids
No special diet is routine for inhaled-allergen rhinitis; avoid food elimination unless a separate food allergy is established.
Nursing priorities
Assess sleep, daytime function and coexisting asthma, demonstrate nasal-spray technique and monitor medication effects such as sedation.
Child and family teaching
Identify realistic trigger-reduction measures, use medicines consistently as directed and seek review if symptoms affect breathing, sleep or school participation.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 42: Immune disorders

Otitis media
Causes and risk factors
Acute otitis media often follows a viral URI; young age, Eustachian-tube anatomy, smoke exposure and childcare exposure increase risk.
What changes in the body
Eustachian-tube dysfunction traps middle-ear fluid that becomes inflamed/infected; distinguish acute infection from effusion without acute infection.
Diagnostic evaluation
Diagnose with otoscopy demonstrating middle-ear effusion and characteristic tympanic-membrane findings such as bulging; ear pulling alone is insufficient.
Clinical findings
Ear pain, fever, irritability and impaired sleep/feeding occur; middle-ear fluid may temporarily affect hearing.
Medical management
Treat pain; choose antibiotics or monitored observation according to age, severity, laterality and reliable follow-up, with reassessment if symptoms fail to improve.
Nutrition and fluids
No special diet is needed; maintain fluids and usual nutrition, using comfortable upright feeding when sucking worsens discomfort.
Nursing priorities
Use an age-appropriate pain assessment, monitor response and hydration, and arrange evaluation of persistent effusion or hearing/language concerns.
Child and family teaching
Explain the antibiotic or watchful-waiting plan, avoid smoke exposure and seek reassessment for worsening symptoms, ear drainage or failure to improve within the specified interval.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 50: Eye and ear disorders · CDC: Pediatric outpatient antibiotic guidance · CDC: Ear infection basics

The child with a cardiac disorder

Given anatomy and perfusion findings, explain the circulation problem, select a nursing plan for oxygenation/perfusion and growth, identify deterioration and evaluate the response and family readiness for home care.

  • Cardiac catheterization
  • Common congenital heart defects
  • Kawasaki disease
  • Rheumatic fever
Cardiac catheterization
Causes and risk factors
Catheterization is a diagnostic or interventional procedure for selected structural/hemodynamic problems; bleeding, thrombosis, dysrhythmia and contrast reactions are procedure risks.
What changes in the body
A vascular catheter reaches the heart to measure pressures/oxygen content or perform an intervention such as device closure or balloon dilation.
Diagnostic evaluation
Before the procedure verify consent, baseline rhythm/vitals and distal perfusion, prior contrast reactions and ordered laboratory/renal assessment; sedation or anesthesia depends on the child and intervention.
Clinical findings
Afterward watch for access-site bleeding or hematoma, new loss of distal pulses, pallor/coolness, dysrhythmia and delayed recovery or respiratory compromise.
Medical management
The catheterization/anesthesia team sets fasting, medications, hydration and recovery restrictions according to the procedure and access site.
Nutrition and fluids
Follow prescribed preprocedure fasting; resume oral intake only when recovery/swallowing are safe, and give ordered fluids while respecting cardiac or renal restrictions.
Nursing priorities
Monitor airway, rhythm, vital signs, access site and bilateral distal perfusion; maintain ordered positioning, apply direct pressure for bleeding and urgently report perfusion loss or instability.
Clinical clarification for this handout

A new or worsening weak distal pulse after arterial catheterization is not automatically a normal finding. Compare pulses with the preprocedure baseline and opposite limb, assess warmth, color, capillary refill, sensation, and movement, and promptly escalate a changeâ€”especially a pale or cool limb, delayed refill, or an absent pulse. Document the actual prior contrast agent and reaction: an isolated shellfish or povidone-iodine allergy does not establish an allergy to iodinated contrast. Follow the procedure team's access-site, positioning, and activity orders rather than applying one bed-rest interval to every catheterization.

References: Royal Children's Hospital: Post-cardiac-catheterization care, ACRâ€“AAAAI: Radiocontrast hypersensitivity consensus

Clinical clarification for this handout

The calculations below evaluate the handout's own expressions; they are not universal drainage orders. Multiplying mL/kg by body weight in kg leaves mL, so the 6 kg example gives 30â€“60 mL, not 30â€“60 mL/kg. For a 2 kg child, 3 mL/kg/hour equals 6 mL/hour and 18 mL over 3 hours; the printed 12 mL/hour does not follow that formula. Assess the child and the drainage trend, use the surgical team's reporting thresholds, and escalate excessive bleeding or a sudden stop in drainage with deterioration.

References: Royal Children's Hospital: Chest-drain management

Child and family teaching
Use age-appropriate play or explanation, include caregivers and teach wound care, activity limits, follow-up and urgent signs such as bleeding, a cold/pale limb or increasing pain.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 41: Child with a Cardiovascular Disorder · ACR/AAAAI 2025 contrast hypersensitivity consensus

Common congenital heart defects
Causes and risk factors
Congenital structural abnormalities arise during fetal development; genetic syndromes, family history and some maternal conditions contribute, although many causes are unknown.
What changes in the body
Classify the actual anatomy as increased pulmonary flow, obstructed flow, decreased pulmonary flow or parallel/mixing circulation; the defect name alone does not determine severity.
Diagnostic evaluation
Echocardiography defines most defects; assess pulses, four-limb pressures when indicated, oxygenation and ECG, using catheterization for selected questions or interventions.
Clinical findings
Murmurs may accompany feeding fatigue, tachypnea, diaphoresis, poor growth, cyanosis or poor systemic perfusion, and a critical lesion may have little audible murmur.
Medical management
Management ranges from observation to heart-failure medicines, catheter intervention or surgery; prostaglandin is used for selected duct-dependent lesions, with monitoring for apnea and hypotension.
Nutrition and fluids
Protect growth with individualized feeding support, smaller energy-dense feeds when needed and dietitian/lactation input; fluid and sodium plans depend on the child’s physiology.
Nursing priorities
Track breathing, oxygenation against the child-specific target, pulses/perfusion, intake/output and weight; cluster care and escalate for cyanosis spells, shock or worsening feeding intolerance.
Child and family teaching
Teach the child’s blood-flow problem, medicines, feeding plan, emergency signs and follow-up, with developmental surveillance, school planning and clear individualized activity advice.
Patent ductus arteriosus
Causes and risk factors
Persistence of the fetal ductus is more common in premature infants.
What changes in the body
After birth, aortic blood usually passes through the ductus into the pulmonary artery, increasing pulmonary flow and left-heart workload.
Diagnostic evaluation
Echocardiography confirms the connection and evaluates shunt size and cardiac effect.
Clinical findings
A continuous machinery-like murmur, bounding pulses and widened pulse pressure may occur; a large shunt causes tachypnea, feeding difficulty and poor growth.
Medical management
Observe or treat based on hemodynamic significance; selected preterm infants receive closure medicines, while catheter or surgical closure may be needed.
Nutrition and fluids
If feeding is tiring or growth falters, use the prescribed energy-dense feeding plan and individualized fluids.
Nursing priorities
Assess breathing, feeding, weight, perfusion and response to treatment, including the monitoring required for the ordered closure medicine.
Child and family teaching
Explain closure versus observation and follow-up; a PDA must remain open in certain other critical heart defects, so closure is never a universal neonatal goal.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 41: Child with a Cardiovascular Disorder

Atrial septal defect
Causes and risk factors
Incomplete formation of atrial septal tissue creates an ASD; location and associated defects vary.
What changes in the body
Blood usually shunts from left atrium to right atrium, increasing pulmonary blood flow and right-heart volume.
Diagnostic evaluation
Echocardiography defines defect type, size and right-sided volume effects.
Clinical findings
Many children are asymptomatic; fixed split S2 and a pulmonary-flow systolic murmur may occur, with fatigue or respiratory symptoms in larger defects.
Medical management
Small defects may close or need observation; significant suitable secundum defects may receive catheter closure, while others need surgery.
Nutrition and fluids
No special diet is required for an asymptomatic small ASD; support growth and adjust feeding only when symptoms warrant.
Nursing priorities
Monitor activity tolerance and growth, and after repair assess rhythm and the access site or surgical recovery.
Child and family teaching
Teach follow-up and any temporary activity/antiplatelet instructions; do not promise every ASD needs surgery.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 41: Child with a Cardiovascular Disorder

Ventricular septal defect
Causes and risk factors
Incomplete ventricular septal formation produces a defect that may be isolated or part of a more complex lesion.
What changes in the body
As pulmonary resistance falls, left-to-right flow can increase pulmonary circulation and return volume to the left heart.
Diagnostic evaluation
Echocardiography identifies anatomy and shunt effects; the murmur and symptoms may become clearer during the first weeks after birth.
Clinical findings
A harsh holosystolic murmur is typical; larger defects produce tachypnea, sweating with feeds and poor weight gain.
Medical management
Small VSDs may close spontaneously; symptomatic children may receive heart-failure therapy and catheter or surgical closure when indicated.
Nutrition and fluids
Support efficient calorie intake with shorter feeds and prescribed fortification or tube supplementation if necessary.
Nursing priorities
Trend respiratory effort, weight and intake/output; after repair monitor for conduction disturbance including heart block.
Child and family teaching
Explain that absence of cyanosis does not exclude significant heart failure and teach worsening feeding or breathing signs.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 41: Child with a Cardiovascular Disorder

Coarctation of the aorta
Causes and risk factors
Congenital aortic narrowing may become evident as ductal tissue constricts or as the child grows.
What changes in the body
An obstructive lesion raises pressure before the narrowing and reduces flow beyond it; it is not a left-to-right shunt.
Diagnostic evaluation
Compare right-arm and leg blood pressure and brachial/femoral pulses; echocardiography defines narrowing and its effect.
Clinical findings
Upper-body hypertension with weak/delayed femoral pulses is characteristic; critical neonatal disease can cause poor perfusion, acidosis or shock.
Medical management
Critical duct-dependent disease may need prostaglandin stabilization before repair; surgery, balloon treatment or stenting depends on age and anatomy.
Nutrition and fluids
Maintain prescribed feeding and fluid plans; an unstable infant may need temporary nutrition support until systemic perfusion is restored.
Nursing priorities
Assess limb perfusion, blood pressure, urine output and shock signs; after repair monitor blood pressure and distal circulation.
Child and family teaching
Stress ongoing blood-pressure and cardiology follow-up because recurrent narrowing or hypertension can occur.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 41: Child with a Cardiovascular Disorder

Aortic stenosis
Causes and risk factors
Congenital outflow narrowing may be valvar, subvalvar or supravalvar; a bicuspid valve is a common valvar cause.
What changes in the body
Left-ventricular outflow obstruction increases ventricular pressure/work and can reduce cardiac output.
Diagnostic evaluation
Echocardiography defines location, gradient and ventricular function, supplemented by ECG or catheter assessment when needed.
Clinical findings
A systolic murmur may be the only sign; severe disease can cause exertional chest pain, fatigue, syncope or neonatal low-output signs.
Medical management
Observe mild disease with follow-up; significant obstruction may need balloon valvuloplasty or surgical treatment.
Nutrition and fluids
No universal special diet exists; tailor nutrition to growth and any heart-failure/low-output state.
Nursing priorities
Assess perfusion and exertional symptoms, escalate syncope or chest pain, and apply the cardiologist’s individualized activity limits.
Child and family teaching
Explain ongoing reassessment and prompt reporting of chest pain, fainting or reduced exercise tolerance.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 41: Child with a Cardiovascular Disorder

Pulmonary stenosis
Causes and risk factors
Congenital pulmonary valve/outflow narrowing may occur alone or with other heart abnormalities and can run in families.
What changes in the body
The right ventricle works against a narrowed outlet; critical obstruction can greatly limit pulmonary blood flow.
Diagnostic evaluation
Echocardiography evaluates valve anatomy and severity; oxygenation, ECG and selected catheter studies add information.
Clinical findings
Mild disease may only cause a murmur; severe disease can cause fatigue, chest pain, fainting or cyanosis.
Medical management
Mild disease may be observed; balloon valvuloplasty treats suitable valve obstruction, while other anatomy may require surgery.
Nutrition and fluids
No special diet is routine for mild disease; provide growth/feeding support if severe disease increases fatigue.
Nursing priorities
Monitor oxygenation, perfusion and symptoms, including access-site and distal-pulse checks after catheter treatment.
Child and family teaching
Teach follow-up and individualized activity advice, with urgent review for syncope or worsening cyanosis.

Sources: CHOP: Pulmonary stenosis · Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 41: Child with a Cardiovascular Disorder

Tetralogy of Fallot
Causes and risk factors
TOF is a congenital combination sometimes associated with genetic syndromes, including 22q11.2 deletion.
What changes in the body
VSD, overriding aorta, right-ventricular outflow obstruction and RV hypertrophy create anatomy-dependent right-to-left flow and reduced pulmonary flow.
Clinical clarification for this handout

The cyanotic 'T' mnemonic is a memory aid, not one shared right-to-left shunt mechanism. In d-transposition of the great arteries, blood follows parallel systemic and pulmonary circuits, and communication such as an ASD, VSD, or PDA allows mixing that can deliver oxygenated blood to the body. In persistent truncus arteriosus, a common arterial trunk and usually a VSD produce mixing, often with excessive pulmonary blood flow and heart failure as well as cyanosis.

References: CDC: d-Transposition of the great arteries, CDC: Truncus arteriosus

Diagnostic evaluation
Echocardiography defines the four features and obstruction; oxygenation and other studies guide repair planning.
Clinical findings
Cyanosis, murmur and hypercyanotic spells may occur; less obstruction can produce initially pink physiology.
Medical management
Surgical repair is required, sometimes preceded by palliation; a severe duct-dependent form may need prostaglandin.
Nutrition and fluids
Use calm, efficient feeds and prescribed calorie support when spells or fatigue limit intake; prevent dehydration.
Nursing priorities
For an acute hypercyanotic spell, calm the child, bring knees toward the chest, summon help and provide oxygen/ordered emergency treatment while reassessing.
Child and family teaching
Have caregivers demonstrate the spell response and emergency plan, and explain the need for lifelong cardiac follow-up even after repair.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 41: Child with a Cardiovascular Disorder · CHOP: Tetralogy of Fallot

Transposition of the great arteries
Causes and risk factors
The great arteries connect to the wrong ventricles during fetal development; maternal diabetes is one associated risk factor.
What changes in the body
Aorta from RV and pulmonary artery from LV create parallel circulations, so survival depends on adequate mixing through atrial/ventricular or ductal connections.
Clinical clarification for this handout

The cyanotic 'T' mnemonic is a memory aid, not one shared right-to-left shunt mechanism. In d-transposition of the great arteries, blood follows parallel systemic and pulmonary circuits, and communication such as an ASD, VSD, or PDA allows mixing that can deliver oxygenated blood to the body. In persistent truncus arteriosus, a common arterial trunk and usually a VSD produce mixing, often with excessive pulmonary blood flow and heart failure as well as cyanosis.

References: CDC: d-Transposition of the great arteries, CDC: Truncus arteriosus

Diagnostic evaluation
Echocardiography confirms anatomy and mixing pathways and maps coronary anatomy for surgery.
Clinical findings
Early cyanosis and tachypnea can be prominent despite little or no murmur.
Medical management
Prostaglandin supports ductal patency; inadequate atrial mixing may need balloon atrial septostomy before an arterial switch repair.
Nutrition and fluids
Use a specialist-directed feeding plan while unstable and support growth after repair, balancing energy needs with cardiorespiratory tolerance.
Nursing priorities
Monitor oxygenation against prescribed goals, perfusion and acid-base status; maintain the prostaglandin line and prepare for apnea/hypotension.
Child and family teaching
Explain why mixing is temporarily essential and why repair/follow-up are required rather than treating cyanosis with oxygen alone.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 41: Child with a Cardiovascular Disorder

Tricuspid atresia
Causes and risk factors
Absent tricuspid-valve formation produces a hypoplastic right ventricle, often with associated septal or outflow abnormalities.
What changes in the body
Systemic venous blood must cross an atrial communication and mix; pulmonary flow depends on associated anatomy and may be too low or too high.
Diagnostic evaluation
Echocardiography defines the missing valve and connections; saturation and selected catheter studies help evaluate flow.
Clinical findings
Cyanosis, feeding difficulty, poor growth, fatigue or heart-failure signs vary with pulmonary blood flow.
Medical management
Selected infants need prostaglandin or a pulmonary-flow intervention, followed by individualized staged single-ventricle palliation such as Glenn and Fontan procedures.
Nutrition and fluids
Use a monitored nutrition plan with weight and intake tracking because poor growth can signal inadequate or excessive pulmonary flow.
Nursing priorities
Follow the child-specific saturation/perfusion targets and interstage monitoring plan, escalating changes in feeding, weight, oxygenation or alertness.
Child and family teaching
Teach daily home monitoring and emergency contacts and explain that not every infant with tricuspid atresia needs prostaglandin, while all need long-term specialist care.

Sources: CHOP: Tricuspid atresia · Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 41: Child with a Cardiovascular Disorder

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 41: Child with a Cardiovascular Disorder · CHOP: Pulmonary stenosis · CHOP: Tricuspid atresia · CHOP: Tetralogy of Fallot

Kawasaki disease
Causes and risk factors
Kawasaki disease has an incompletely understood trigger in susceptible children, usually under five, and is not spread person to person.
What changes in the body
Systemic vasculitis can injure coronary arteries, producing dilation/aneurysm and later thrombosis or ischemia.
Diagnostic evaluation
Diagnosis is clinical using fever and characteristic features, with inflammatory tests and echocardiography to assess coronary involvement; incomplete presentations also require evaluation.
Clinical findings
Persistent fever with nonpurulent conjunctivitis, oral changes, rash, extremity changes and cervical lymphadenopathy is characteristic, with irritability and later peeling.
Medical management
Prompt specialist-directed IVIG and aspirin reduce risk; aspirin phase and duration and further anti-inflammatory or antithrombotic treatment depend on fever and coronary findings.
Clinical clarification for this handout

Prescribed salicylates, including aspirin, can be part of acute rheumatic fever treatment, so the handout's blanket instruction to never give aspirin is inaccurate. After the 2 g/kg IVIG dose used for Kawasaki disease, CDC recommends waiting 11 months before measles- or varicella-containing vaccines, such as MMR and varicella. Inactivated influenza vaccine does not require that IVIG-related delay. Live attenuated nasal influenza vaccine (LAIV) is also an exception to the antibody-product spacing rule, but it must not be given to children ages 2â€“17 who are taking aspirin or salicylates. Check the actual vaccine, IVIG dose, and current contraindications when planning immunization.

References: CDC: Acute rheumatic fever treatment, CDC: Timing and spacing of immunobiologics, CDC: Nasal spray influenza vaccine

Nutrition and fluids
Offer frequent fluids and soft nonacidic foods because fever and painful lips/mouth reduce intake; monitor hydration and use prescribed IV support if needed.
Nursing priorities
Monitor fever, perfusion, cardiac status and treatment response, protect swollen skin, provide mouth/lip care and cluster care for an irritable child.
Child and family teaching
Explain prescribed aspirin is a specific exception to routine viral-fever care, review bleeding/illness precautions and vaccine timing after IVIG, and stress scheduled cardiac imaging/follow-up.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 41: Child with a Cardiovascular Disorder · AHA 2024 Kawasaki scientific statement

Rheumatic fever
Causes and risk factors
Acute rheumatic fever follows a group A streptococcal infection in susceptible children; recurrence is more likely after another GAS infection.
What changes in the body
An abnormal immune response causes inflammation involving joints, heart, brain and skin; valve injury can lead to chronic rheumatic heart disease.
Diagnostic evaluation
Use Jones criteria with evidence of preceding GAS infection and echocardiography; an elevated ASO titer supports prior infection but does not by itself diagnose rheumatic fever.
Clinical findings
Major findings include carditis, migratory polyarthritis, chorea, erythema marginatum and subcutaneous nodules, with fever and raised inflammatory markers among supporting findings.
Medical management
Eradicate residual GAS, treat inflammation and any heart failure, and institute long-term secondary antibiotic prophylaxis according to cardiac involvement and the specialist plan.

References: CDC: Acute rheumatic fever treatment, CDC: Timing and spacing of immunobiologics, CDC: Nasal spray influenza vaccine

Nutrition and fluids
Provide a balanced diet and adequate intake; soft foods and feeding assistance may help chorea, while cardiac failure requires its individualized fluid/nutrition plan.
Nursing priorities
Monitor for carditis, perfusion changes, pain and involuntary movements, provide safe assistance and rest/activity according to acute symptoms and cardiac status.
Child and family teaching
Teach why prolonged prophylaxis matters even when symptoms resolve, support school and family coping, and maintain cardiology follow-up for possible delayed valve disease.

Sources: Maternal & Child textbook, Chapter 41: Child with a Cardiovascular Disorder · CDC: Acute rheumatic fever

Apply the foundations to every case

For every case, start with age and developmental ability: infants need feeding/hydration assessment and caregiver soothing; toddlers benefit from simple choices and play; school-age children can demonstrate care; adolescents need privacy and shared responsibility. Include caregiver teach-back, medication access, school participation and the family’s ability to carry out the plan.

Dosage practice

First distinguish mg/kg/day from mg/kg/dose. Verify kilograms, the prescribed range and maximum, doses per day, concentration, final volume and time units. Round at the end using the required precision.

Practice order only: 45 mg/kg/day in two doses, weight 33 lb, supply 400 mg/5 mL. 33 ÷ 2.2 = 15 kg; 45 × 15 = 675 mg/day; 675 ÷ 2 = 337.5 mg/dose; 337.5 × 5 ÷ 400 = 4.21875 mL/dose → 4.2 mL if instructed to round to tenths. This arithmetic example is not a treatment recommendation.

Maintenance-fluid calculations are estimates to check against the prescribed plan; a child with a cardiac disorder may need individualized fluid limits.

🎧 Exam 4 exam-prep recording

What Dr. Hansen actually said in the review session for this exam — her numbers, her worked calculations, her priority rulings. On an exam, this beats the textbook.

  • Duchenne is progressive with no cure — children do not outgrow it. Symptom management, physical therapy to delay contractures, and prompt reporting of respiratory infections.
  • DMD respiratory protection: cough-assist device · nighttime BiPAP · upright or semi-Fowler · adequate hydration to thin secretions — do NOT restrict fluids · no prone positioning.
  • Type 1 DM before 30 minutes of soccer at glucose 70: give a COMPLEX carbohydrate snack for steady release through prolonged activity. Rapid-acting insulin deepens the hypoglycemia; protein digests too slowly; delaying play is unnecessary.
  • DKA: fruity breath, Kussmaul respirations, glucose 400. IV fluids and an IV insulin infusion. Verify urine output before any IV potassium, and watch potassium closely — high on presentation, falling with treatment. Oral fluids are unsafe with dehydration and altered LOC. Bicarbonate is rarely indicated.
  • Hypothyroid = everything slows: weight gain, constipation, fatigue, cold intolerance, dry skin. Hyperthyroid is the mirror: heat intolerance, tachycardia, big appetite, insomnia, warm moist skin, diarrhea.
  • Primary hypothyroidism: HIGH TSH with LOW free T4. Her free T4 reference is 0.8–2.0 ng/dL.
  • Propylthiouracil: fever and sore throat must be reported immediately — agranulocytosis. Stop the drug, urgent evaluation. Mild nausea, hair loss, rash and headache are common. Weight gain and cold intolerance mean drug-induced hypothyroidism — concerning, not emergent.
  • DDH: a POSITIVE Ortolani means hip instability → further evaluation. Symmetric thigh folds, equal leg lengths and a negative Barlow are reassuring; asymmetric folds and leg-length shortening are the abnormal versions.
  • JIA home care: daily ROM, warm compresses BEFORE activity to cut stiffness, scheduled rest. NSAIDs are first-line and do not damage joints when used appropriately — refusing them is the teaching error in the stem.
  • JIA's serious complication is uveitis — vision changes or loss, so regular ophthalmology exams are mandatory. JIA reduces bone density, it does not increase it.
  • Hirschsprung: absent ganglion cells → no peristalsis → constipation, ribbon-like stools, distension, poor feeding, growth delay. The serious complication is enterocolitis — fever, distension, explosive diarrhea, progressing to sepsis.
  • Projectile vomiting with metabolic alkalosis is pyloric stenosis, a different disease — it appears here only as a distractor.
  • Acute gastroenteritis expected findings: hyperactive bowel sounds and frequent watery stools. Dry mucous membranes and low urine output are dehydration, a complication, not the primary manifestation. Expect weight loss and tachycardia.
  • Calc: 3% sodium chloride at 2 mL/kg, 18 kg = 36 mL. Units cancel; no rounding.

▸🎯 How she wants these questions answered — 13 rules

Stated out loud across all four recordings. These are not course-specific — they work on every exam you sit, including NCLEX.

  • ABCs first, then safety and security, then higher Maslow levels. Maslow is the tiebreaker when several options are all technically correct.
  • When two answers are both defensible, choose the LEAST INVASIVE first — reassess before you escalate to the provider or start oxygen.
  • Name what is actually being asked before you look at the options: "first," "priority," "most appropriate," "highest priority," "further teaching."
  • "Indicates a need for further teaching" means hunt the WRONG statement. She flags this at nearly every occurrence.
  • For any developmental item: pin the child's exact age first, then match the behavior. Eliminate options that are too advanced and options that are regressed.
  • Eliminate options that are not age-appropriate to the client at all — colorectal screening or osteoporosis in an adolescent stem.
  • Watch absolute qualifiers. "Only," "always," "never" usually mark a wrong option — "fluids only during waking hours" is the example she uses.
  • Dosage formatting: leading zero, never a trailing zero · round only at the very END, never mid-problem and never the weight · follow the stated target (tenth vs whole) · round up only when the next digit is 5 or more · ATI's rule for oral liquids is the nearest tenth.
  • Check first whether a weight conversion is needed (lb ÷ 2.2 = kg). If the weight is already in kg, skip it.
  • To find doses per day, divide 24 by the interval — q6h → 4 doses. Not by the interval number itself.
  • For pump rates: volume ÷ time in hours. Convert minutes first — 30 min = 0.5 hr.
  • Three methods are all acceptable — dimensional analysis, ratio-proportion, desired-over-have. She presents dimensional analysis as the most accurate. Do a couple of ATI Dosage Calculation modules a week.
  • Normal pediatric vital sign parameters come from the Joyce catalog and the Canvas modules; milestone tables come from the ATI book. Use those, not a random chart.
▸⚠️ Before you trust the recordings — 18 corrections

❌ Flat wrong in the recording — 12 of them

Repeat these and they cost you marks. Corrected here.

Exam 1 — milestone SATA
She keys "A, B, D, E," then explains option B by saying infants sit steadily at 8 months, not 6 — declaring the option she just keyed correct to be wrong. She then narrates option C approvingly and leaves it out of the key.
The internally consistent key is A, C, D, E. Also: "walks with one hand held" is about 12 months; by 15 months most children walk independently.
Exam 4 — TSH range
She gives normal TSH as "between 3 and 5," then says hypothyroidism means TSH "greater than 4." Both cannot hold.
Reference is roughly 0.4–4.0 mIU/L. Her 3–5 range would make a normal TSH look abnormal. The free T4 range she gives (0.8–2.0) is correct.
Exam 3 — aspirin in rheumatic fever
She applies a blanket Reye-syndrome caution to aspirin in ARF.
High-dose aspirin is the standard anti-inflammatory FOR acute rheumatic fever and is one of the recognized exceptions to withholding salicylates in children. Her wider ranking — bed rest and carditis monitoring above aspirin — is fine.
Exam 3 — Jones criteria
She recites the major criteria and adds "fascicular rashes and petechiae," then says those are not typical of ARF. She also says ARF has no motor manifestations.
Neither rashes nor petechiae are Jones criteria. And chorea is a motor manifestation and a major criterion — the "no motor" claim is wrong.
Exam 2 — meningitis workup
CT before LP because "CT is the least invasive diagnostic study."
Least-invasiveness is not the reason — herniation risk is. And routine CT before LP is not current practice; imaging is reserved for focal deficits, papilledema, immunocompromise or persistently altered consciousness. She also downgrades blood cultures, when cultures should be drawn and empiric antibiotics started immediately rather than waiting on imaging.
Exam 4 — DKA sequencing
She names starting insulin as the initial intervention.
In pediatric DKA, isotonic IV fluid resuscitation comes first, with insulin typically started 1–2 hours later, specifically to cut cerebral edema risk. The keyed option bundles fluids with insulin so the answer survives — the emphasis does not.
Exam 4 — glucose range
Normal glucose given as "60 to 110," and 70 mg/dL described as comfortably within the safe range. Elsewhere: "between 600 and 110."
70 is the conventional lower threshold for hypoglycemia, not a comfortable middle. The intervention taught — complex carbohydrate before prolonged exercise — is still correct.
Exam 2 — thrombocytopenia SATA
She keys "A, C and D," but option D was monitoring for hyperactivity and mood changes.
That is not a thrombocytopenia intervention at all. Only A and C hold.
Exam 1 — sedation positioning
"Supine side laying is preferred, not to maintain the airway, though."
Muddled. Prone is correctly rejected, but lateral / side-lying is the airway-protective recovery position, not supine.
Exam 1 — juice limit
She generalizes "4 to 6 ounces per day" for a 2-year-old.
Current guidance is a 4 oz/day maximum for ages 1–3; 4–6 oz applies to ages 4–6.
Exam 4 — PTU as the framing drug
PTU is used as the hyperthyroid drug throughout.
Correct for the agranulocytosis teaching point, but methimazole is preferred first-line in children because of PTU hepatotoxicity. PTU is reserved for first-trimester pregnancy and thyroid storm.
Exam 2 — double-check rule
"All pediatric math… has to be double-checked by another registered nurse."
Independent double-checks are institution-dependent and generally mandated for high-alert medications, not literally every pediatric calculation.

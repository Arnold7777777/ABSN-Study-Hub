DKA:
Blood Glucose Level: Usually 250-800 mg/dL (11.1-44.4 mmol/L)

Diabetes insipidus (DI)
,
 and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)
, are all states of endocrine dysfunction characterized by increased urine output.
DI 
occurs when there is decreased antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion causing 
excessive urination
 and therefore, 
excessive thirst
 to compensate.
In 
DKA
,
 
an excess of unused, circulating glucose (i.e., 
hyperglycemia
) causes a breakdown of fat for energy, forming acidic ketone bodies in the blood and creating an 
excessive hunger
 response (i.e., 
polyphagia
) as the body attempts to provide fuel for the cells. Acidic ketones create a state of metabolic acidosis which the lungs attempt to correct by blowing off carbon dioxide, causing 
deep, rapid 
Kussmaul
 
breaths
 and the 
fruit-like breath odor
.
  
Ketones are excreted into the urine (i.e., 
ketonuria
) along with glucose, pulling water along with it (i.e., 
frequent urination
). Fluid loss through urination causes dehydration and 
excessive thirst 
(i.e., polydipsia).

Original speaker notes · preserved verbatim

Linked to this slide through the original PowerPoint relationships. Any clinical qualifications are identified in the visible instructor-review notes.

Diabetic ketoacidosis 
(DKA) is a life threatening complication of type 1 diabetes. Osmotic diuresis in DKA causes polyuria and without rapid fluid replacement can lead to 
severe dehydration
. Acidosis and dehydration create electrolyte imbalances, placing the client at risk for deadly arrhythmias and 
cardiac arrest
. 
When administering IV fluids to treat DKA, the sudden increase in intravascular fluid can quickly move fluid into the cells. If fluid and insulin are replaced too quickly, there is an increased risk of swelling in the brain (i.e., 
cerebral edema
). Cerebral edema is more commonly seen in pediatric clients.
Therefore, 
the nurse should prioritize monitoring the client for 
cardiac arrest
, 
cerebral edema
, and 
severe dehydration
.
Incorrect:
Stroke, Neuropathy, Retinopathy
Diabetes can damage nerves, causing decreased sensation and pain (i.e., neuropathy), and microvascular damage to the vessels of the retina (i.e. retinopathy) with loss of vision. Over time, hyperglycemia can damage vasculature, increasing the risk of atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, and stroke. These chronic complications are not immediately life threatening.
Urinary tract infection
Glucose in the urine (i.e., glycosuria) increases bacterial and fungal growth, increasing risk for UTIs. Although a concern, bacteria takes time to grow, so this is not the highest risk at this time.

Original speaker notes · preserved verbatim

Linked to this slide through the original PowerPoint relationships. Any clinical qualifications are identified in the visible instructor-review notes.

Hyperkalemia
 occurs in diabetic ketoacidosis when high serum osmolality (e.g., from hyperglycemia) causes hydrogen ions to enter the cell in exchange for potassium. After administering IV fluids and insulin, insulin drives potassium back into the intracellular space, which may precipitate 
hypokalemia
.
Once blood glucose is between 250-300 mg/dL (13.9-16.7 mmol/L), the nurse should 
start IV fluids 
containing 5% to 10% dextrose, like 
dextrose 5% in sodium chloride 0.9%
,
 
to maintain normal glucose levels and prevent hypoglycemia. 
Administer IV ondansetron 
to treat nausea.
Obtain a basic metabolic panel 
to monitor potassium and other electrolytes
Incorrect:
Start subcutaneous insulin, Stop the IV insulin infusion
IV insulin is discontinued once glucose levels are low enough to utilize subcutaneous insulin, nausea and vomiting have resolved, and the client can tolerate oral intake. 
Obtain a troponin level, Administer IV labetalol, Prepare for cardioversion
Troponin levels are not indicated for this client, as the ECG changes are caused by hypokalemia rather than coronary ischemia. Cardioversion and IV beta blockers are indicated in unstable tachyarrhythmias (e.g., atrial fibrillation).
Administer IV potassium
The nurse must confirm hypokalemia by checking a BMP before administering potassium. 
Key Takeaway:
Only administer potassium once hypokalemia is confirmed with a BMP.

Teaching is effective if the client demonstrates an 
understanding
 of these teaching points:
Manage hypoglycemia by 
checking blood sugar and eating a snack when feeling 
hypoglycemic
 
Bring snacks during physical activities 
to prevent hypoglycemia after exercise
 
Choose
 
complex carbohydrates and whole fruits over simple carbohydrates and refined sugars
. Simple carbohydrates from white bread and sugary foods quickly increase blood sugar, while complex carbohydrates and whole fruits with fiber are absorbed more slowly.
No understanding
:
In type 1 diabetes, the insulin-producing pancreatic beta cells are permanently damaged and the pancreas can no longer produce any insulin. Clients with type 1 diabetes require frequent exogenous insulin. 
Healthy diet and exercise
 can help manage glucose levels but cannot restore pancreatic function
.
Managing diabetes during illness requires clients to 
take insulin even when not eating
. Infection and illness cause a stress-induced hyperglycemia that requires insulin to treat. 
Hypoglycemic clients should consume a ½ cup of non-diet soda or juice
 which is higher in sugar to quickly increase blood sugar
Clients should 
not recap needles
 
or throw needles in a trash bag
 because of possible injury
 [Choice 8]
. Place sharps in puncture resistant containers and dispose following community guidelines.
Key Takeaway:
Clients with type 1 diabetes should: 
Monitor blood glucose 
Bring snacks when exercising
Understand signs of hypoglycemia
Drink ½ cup of a high sugar beverage when hypoglycemic
Follow a low carbohydrate and low sugar diet
Properly dispose of insulin needles

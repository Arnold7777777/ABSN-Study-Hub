Assess HR, BP, peripheral pulses, cap refill, and skin color

20 / 129Multiple Trauma

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Multiple Trauma

Single catastrophic event

Life-threatening injuries to at least two organs or organ systems​

Requires a large healthcare team

Primary and secondary surveys

Determine extent of injuries and establish priorities of treatment

Spinal precautions: Log roll, hard cervical collar

Injuries interfering with vital physiologic function have highest priority

Example: A pedestrian is hit by an oncoming vehicle at 20 miles/hr. The client is brought to the ED with the following vitals: BP: 78/50, HR: 150/min regular and RR: 40/min. There are obvious facial injuries, chest injuries, abdominal bruising, and deformed open fractures of both femurs. The GCS is 6.

What is the priority assessment/intervention sequence when caring for this client?

Discuss the vital signs and GCS, what are potential causes of these data points in this client?

21 / 129Multiple trauma: assessment and action

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Priorities: control catastrophic external bleeding while the team performs ABCDE. Protect the cervical spine, suction/open the airway and prepare a definitive airway for GCS 6.

Breathing/circulation: support oxygenation and ventilation, identify chest threats, establish large-bore IV/IO access and prepare hemorrhage resuscitation and urgent trauma intervention.

Interpretation: BP 78/50 with HR 150 suggests shock; abdominal injury and bilateral femur fractures may conceal major blood loss. RR 40 may reflect hypoxemia, chest injury or shock. GCS 6 may reflect brain injury, hypoxia and/or hypoperfusion.

Sources for this addition

  • NICE: Major trauma assessment and initial management
  • Resuscitation Council UK: ABCDE approach

29 / 129Crush injury: immediate priorities

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Airway and breathing: anterior neck swelling threatens the airway; GCS 8 and SpO2 82% require immediate airway/ventilation support with spinal precautions.

Circulation: shock plus reduced right air entry suggests a life-threatening chest injury. Muffled heart sounds raise concern for tamponade. Prepare urgent decompression or other trauma treatment without delaying for nonessential tests.

Rhabdomyolysis: damaged muscle releases myoglobin (dark urine) and potassium. Monitor ECG, potassium, CK and renal function; provide ordered fluids and hyperkalemia treatment while watching for overload.

Sources for this addition

  • NICE: Major trauma assessment and initial management
  • CDC: Rhabdomyolysis

Environmental Emergencies: Heat Exhaustion/Heat Stroke

Heat exhaustion: Usually from prolonged exposure to high temperatures

Results in excessive sweating, resulting in tachycardia and finally dehydration

Rehydration with oral and IV fluids will prevent the onset of heat stroke

Heat stroke: Failure of heat-regulating mechanisms in the body

Can result in death

31 / 129Heat stroke: risk and prevention

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

At risk: infants, older adults, outdoor workers/athletes, people without cooling, those not acclimatized, and people with chronic illness or medications that impair heat tolerance.

Prevention: plan cooler times, shade and rest breaks; gradually acclimatize, wear suitable clothing, drink fluids and check vulnerable people. Never leave children in parked vehicles.

Emergency cue: altered mental status with heat exposure requires immediate emergency response and rapid cooling. Do not give oral fluids to someone with impaired consciousness.

Sources for this addition

  • CDC: Heat-related illnesses

33 / 129Frostbite

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Frostbite

Trauma from freezing temperature and actual freezing of fluid in the intracellular and intercellular spaces.

Extent of injury may not be evident for 24 hours

1st degree superficial : Only layers of exposed skin are affected with hyperemia and edema. Numb central white area without blistering.

2nd degree partial thickness: Blisters cover the exposed skin areas causing necrotic tissue death and swelling.

3rd degree full thickness: Extensive edema and hemorrhagic vesicles and blisters. No blanching. Need debridement.

4th degree full thickness: Full thickness freezing with necrosis. Complete lack of blood flow. Potential for gangrene.

35 / 129Management of Frostbite

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Management of Frostbite

Immediate treatment: cover affected areas and rewarming

Controlled but rapid rewarming with warm bath (104 to 108 °F)

Administer tetanus toxoid

Administer analgesics for pain during rewarming

Do not massage or rub

If feet are involved, do not allow client to walk

Once re-warmed, elevate affected extremity

Assess for compartment syndrome

Swelling

Increase in pain

Needs immediate treatment!

Clinical side note

36 / 129Side note: frostbite rewarming

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

The source lists 104–108°F. Wilderness Medical Society guidance recommends controlled water rewarming at 37–39°C (98.6–102.2°F). Use a thermometer; avoid burning numb tissue.

Do not rub or massage. Protect the part and avoid walking on injured feet when possible. Do not thaw tissue if it will refreeze before rescue.

Treat associated hypothermia, provide pain relief and obtain specialist assessment; injury depth can evolve after rewarming.

Sources for this addition

  • Wilderness Medical Society: Frostbite guidelines

37 / 129Hypothermia

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Hypothermia

Internal core temperate is 35°C (95° F) or less

Elderly, infants, persons with concurrent illness, the homeless, and trauma victims are at risk

Alcohol ingestion increases susceptibility (systemic vasodilation and diuretic effects)

Hypothermia may be seen with frostbite

Physiologic changes in all organ systems

Continuous monitoring

38 / 129Management of Hypothermia

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Management of Hypothermia

Use ABCs, remove wet clothing, and re-warm

Active core re-warming

Cardiopulmonary bypass, warm fluid administration, warm humidified oxygen, and warm peritoneal lavage, warm bladder irrigation

Passive external re-warming

Warm blankets and over-the-bed heaters (Bair-Huggers)

Cold blood returning from the extremities has high levels of lactic acid

Can cause potential cardiac dysrhythmias and electrolyte disturbances

42 / 129Carbon Monoxide (CO) Poisoning

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Carbon Monoxide (CO) Poisoning

Inhaled carbon monoxide binds to hemoglobin as carboxyhemoglobin, which prevents the transport oxygen

Hemoglobin binds carbon monoxide 200 times more readily than oxygen

Hemoglobin molecule is saturated with carbon monoxide rather than oxygen

Manifestations

CNS symptoms predominate

Initial symptom(s) often headache and/or dizziness

Skin color is not a reliable sign and pulse oximetry is not valid

Color ranges from pink to cherry-red to cyanotic and pale

44 / 129Management of Clients with Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Management of Clients with Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

Permanent brain damage may occur, dependent on amount and length of exposure to the carbon monoxide

Get to fresh air immediately​ from high CO area

Administer 100% oxygen

High flow or oxygen under hyperbaric pressure ​to help remove CO from the hemoglobin

Continuously monitor VS, mental status, CO levels in blood, and cardiac status

45 / 129Food Poisoning

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Food Poisoning

Sudden illness caused by the ingestion of contaminated food or drink

Infectious organisms or their toxins are the most common causes of food poisoning

Symptoms: abdominal cramping, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea

Most food poisoning is mild and resolves without treatment

Adequate hydration is the most important aspect of treatment

Botulism (honey) or certain fish poisonings, may result in respiratory paralysis and death​

ABCs

Supportive measures such as oral or IV fluids​

Determination of food poisoning (stool samples for C&S if diarrhea persists)​

Control nausea and vomiting

Clear liquid diet and progression of diet after nausea and vomiting subside

Treat fluid and electrolyte imbalances ​

Avoid anti-diarrheal medications to allow flushing out the diarrhea

47 / 129Management of the Client with Acute Alcohol Intoxication

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Management of the Client with Acute Alcohol Intoxication

Acute alcohol intoxication = multisystem toxin

Acute alcohol poisoning may result in death from respiratory depression

Maintain airway and observe for CNS depression and hypotension

Rule out other potential causes of the behaviors before it is assumed the client is intoxicated

Use a nonjudgmental, calm manner

May need sedation if agitation or delirium occurs

Withdrawal syndrome will require high doses of benzodiazepines to control symptoms

CIWA (Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol) score is used to assess and manage the withdrawal stage of alcohol

48 / 129Snakebites

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Snakebites

Venomous snakebite is a medical emergency

Treatment

Lie down

Remove constrictive items (rings)

Provide warmth

Cleanse the wound

Cover with dressing

Immobilize injured body part below the level of the heart

No ice, tourniquets, heparin, and corticosteroids in the first 6 to 8 hours after the bite

Administer antivenom within 4 to 12 hours if needed

Assess for edema every 15-30 minutes

S: Mr. Raymond is a 48-year-old man who comes in complaining of severe upper abdominal pain rated 10 out of 10.

B: He has a history of type 2 DM diagnosed 6 months ago and admits he is non-compliant monitoring his BG and occasionally forgets to take his metformin. He states he hasn't eaten in 12 hours due to epigastric pain, which has lessened slightly but hasn't gone away. He admits to having at least a six-pack of beer after work each night to relax.

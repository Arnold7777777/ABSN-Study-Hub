1 / 2 Pediatric orthopedic disorders

🔎 Open full size

Completed study notes · suggested additions

Scoliosis

  • Lateral spinal curve with rotation.
  • Uneven shoulders/hips; rib prominence on forward bend.
  • Radiographs measure Cobb angle.
  • Observation, brace or surgery depends on curve and growth.
  • Brace adherence and skin checks; support body image.

Torticollis

  • Tight sternocleidomastoid → head tilt with rotation.
  • Early physical therapy and gentle prescribed stretching.
  • Encourage turning both ways; assess head shape.

Plagiocephaly

  • Flattening from external pressure.
  • Supervised awake tummy time; vary head position.
  • Always place infant supine for sleep.
  • Helmet in selected cases; assess for craniosynostosis.

Craniosynostosis

  • Early fusion of skull suture(s).
  • Abnormal head shape/growth.
  • Craniofacial evaluation; surgery may be needed.

Clubfoot

  • Foot turns inward/downward.
  • Ponseti serial casts; often Achilles tenotomy, then brace.
  • Check skin, toes and circulation; follow brace schedule.

Slipped capital femoral epiphysis

  • Adolescent growth-plate slip; hip/groin or referred knee pain, limp.
  • Limited internal rotation; leg may turn outward.
  • Stop weight bearing; urgent orthopedic assessment.
  • Surgical fixation prevents further slip.

Legg–Calvé–Perthes

  • Loss of blood supply to femoral head in childhood.
  • Limp, hip/knee pain, restricted hip motion.
  • Imaging; preserve motion and femoral-head containment.
  • Activity/weight-bearing plan, therapy; selected surgery.

Developmental dysplasia of hip

  • Shallow/unstable hip joint.
  • Limited abduction, unequal leg length; infant screening.
  • Ultrasound or radiographs by age.
  • Pavlik harness/cast or surgery as ordered.
  • Do not adjust harness straps; skin/neurovascular checks.
  • Use hip-safe swaddling, allowing flexion/abduction.

2 / 2 Fractures, immobilization and chronic conditions

🔎 Open full size

Completed study notes · suggested additions

Fractures

  • Pain, swelling, deformity or reduced use.
  • Immobilize; assess distal color, warmth, pulse, sensation and movement.
  • Growth-plate injuries can affect future growth.
  • Open fracture: cover and seek urgent treatment.
  • Reassess pain and neurovascular status after reduction/casting.

Casting

  • Support wet plaster with palms; keep dry.
  • Never insert objects or powders.
  • Elevate for routine initial swelling as ordered.
  • Report escalating pain, numbness, cool/pale digits or restricted movement.

Traction

  • Maintain prescribed alignment and pull.
  • Weights hang freely; ropes stay in pulleys.
  • Skin/pin care and neurovascular checks; do not remove weights without an order.

Complications

  • Compartment syndrome: severe unrelieved pain, pain with passive stretch, paresthesia.
  • URGENT surgical assessment; pulse loss is late.
  • Monitor skin injury, infection and immobility effects.

Muscular dystrophy

  • Progressive muscle weakness; Duchenne commonly affects boys.
  • Gowers maneuver: uses hands to climb up legs when standing.
  • Monitor respiratory and cardiac function.
  • Preserve mobility; stretching, assistive devices, prescribed therapies.
  • Support family, nutrition and genetic counseling.

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis

  • Persistent inflammatory arthritis beginning in childhood.
  • Pain, swelling, morning stiffness; some types have fever/rash.
  • Balance rest with range-of-motion and low-impact activity.
  • NSAIDs, disease-modifying drugs/biologics as prescribed.
  • Regular eye exams for silent uveitis.
  • Monitor growth, function and medicine effects.

Scoliosis

What: Sideways spinal curve with vertebral rotation. Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis often appears during a growth spurt.

Recognize: Uneven shoulders, waist or hips; rib prominence on the Adams forward-bend test. Often painless.

Diagnosis: Standing spinal X-rays measure the Cobb angle and guide follow-up.

Treatment: Depends on curve size, progression and remaining growth. Smaller curves are observed; bracing limits progression while growing; severe curves may need spinal fusion.

Typical thresholds: Observe below 25 degrees; brace about 25-45 degrees in a growing child; consider surgery above 45-50 degrees. Individual plans vary.

Nursing: Reinforce prescribed brace wear, check skin and fit, encourage activity allowed by the team, and support body image. Keep follow-up appointments. [1]

Torticollis

What: Tight/short sternocleidomastoid (SCM) muscle.

Signs: Head tilts toward the tight side; chin turns away. Limited neck motion; sometimes an SCM lump.

Assess: Neck motion, head shape and feeding; screen for associated DDH. Usually a clinical diagnosis.

Care: Early physical therapy, gentle taught stretches and varied awake positioning; supervised tummy time. Supine sleep.

Follow-up: Check symmetry and progress. New painful or acute torticollis needs evaluation for other causes. [2]

Plagiocephaly

What: Positional skull flattening from external pressure; sutures remain open.

Signs: Flat occiput; ear/forehead may shift forward on that side. Assess torticollis.

Diagnosis: Clinical exam; assess for craniosynostosis if atypical or worsening.

Care: Vary awake positioning, supervised tummy time and PT if needed. Always supine for sleep; no positioning pillows.

Treatment: Helmet for selected persistent severe cases after specialist review. [3]

Craniosynostosis

What: Premature fusion of one or more skull sutures.

Signs: Abnormal head shape; a firm suture ridge or altered head growth.

Care: Early craniofacial/neurosurgical referral; imaging as directed. Surgery often occurs in infancy. Monitor head growth and neurologic status; raised intracranial pressure is a risk. [4]

Clubfoot

What: Congenital rigid foot deformity: foot turns inward and down, with a high arch and inward heel. Diagnosed by examination.

Treatment: Early Ponseti stretching and serial casts; often Achilles tenotomy, then boots-and-bar bracing for several years.

Nursing: Check toes, circulation and skin; keep casts dry. Brace adherence prevents relapse. Report swelling, cool toes or persistent pain. [5,10,12]

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH)

What: Shallow acetabulum with an unstable, subluxed or dislocated femoral head. Risks include breech position, family history and female sex.

Signs: Limited hip abduction, unequal leg length or later limp. Trained clinicians perform Barlow (provokes instability) and Ortolani (reduces a dislocated hip) maneuvers.

Diagnosis: Hip ultrasound in young infants; radiographs become useful as the femoral head ossifies.

Treatment: Pavlik harness in early infancy; reduction and hip-spica cast or surgery when indicated.

Nursing: Do not change prescribed strap settings. Check skin and leg movement/circulation, provide diaper/cast care and attend follow-up. Swaddle with hips free to flex and spread. [6]

Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE)

What: Femoral head slips at the growth plate, usually during adolescence. Risks include obesity and endocrine disorders.

Signs: Limp, hip/groin/thigh or referred knee pain; outward-turned leg and reduced internal rotation.

Diagnosis: Hip/pelvis X-rays. Inability to bear weight indicates an unstable slip.

Priority: Stop weight bearing and obtain urgent orthopedic evaluation. Do not force hip movement.

Treatment: Surgical fixation; follow ordered activity limits and assess pain/neurovascular status. Watch for avascular necrosis and later arthritis. [7]

Legg-Calve-Perthes disease

What: Temporary loss of femoral-head blood supply causes avascular necrosis, followed by repair. Usually ages 4-10, more often boys.

Signs: Limp with hip, thigh or knee pain; reduced abduction and internal rotation. Activity may worsen pain.

Diagnosis: Hip X-rays; follow imaging over the healing period.

Treatment: Maintain range of motion and contain the femoral head in the socket while it heals. Activity/weight-bearing limits, physical therapy, anti-inflammatory medication; selected casting, bracing or surgery.

Nursing: Support the prescribed mobility plan, avoid high-impact activity, reinforce follow-up and assess pain. Healing takes years; residual deformity can cause early arthritis. [8]

Fractures

Types: Buckle = compression; greenstick = incomplete break with bending; complete = through the bone. Growth-plate injury may affect future growth.

Signs: Pain, swelling, deformity and refusal to use the limb. Confirm with X-rays.

First care: Immobilize, manage pain and check distal color, warmth, capillary refill, pulses, sensation and movement before and after treatment.

Treatment: Splint/cast; reduction or fixation if displaced or unstable. Cover an open wound with a sterile dressing and obtain urgent care.

Follow-up: Recheck pain and neurovascular status, teach activity limits and keep healing/growth checks. [9,12]

Casting

Care: Support wet plaster with palms; keep cast dry and avoid pressure on edges/heels.

Routine swelling: Elevate as ordered and encourage finger/toe movement. Follow prescribed weight-bearing limits.

Never: Insert objects, powders or lotions; trim or alter the cast yourself.

Assess/report: Increasing pain, numbness, pale/cool digits, reduced motion, odor, drainage, fever or a tight/damaged cast. Check neurovascular status regularly. [10,12]

Traction

Purpose: Maintain alignment, immobilize the limb and reduce muscle spasm. Skin traction uses straps; skeletal traction uses bone pins.

Maintain: Prescribed line of pull and countertraction; ropes in pulleys and weights hanging freely. Do not change/remove weights without an order.

Nursing: Skin/pin-site care per protocol, neurovascular checks, pressure relief, pain control and immobility prevention. [11,12]

Complications

Compartment syndrome: Escalating pain despite analgesia, pain with passive stretch, tingling and tense swelling. Pulses may remain present; pulse loss/paralysis are late.

Action: Urgent surgical review. Keep limb at heart level; trained staff relieve constriction as directed. Fasciotomy may be required.

Also monitor: Pressure injury, infection, stiffness and muscle wasting with immobilization. [10,12]

Muscular dystrophy

What: Genetic disorders causing progressive muscle weakness. Duchenne (DMD) is X-linked, mainly affects boys and involves absent/deficient dystrophin.

Signs: Early-childhood proximal weakness, frequent falls, waddling/toe-walking, enlarged calves and Gowers maneuver (hands push up thighs to stand).

Diagnosis: Elevated creatine kinase suggests muscle injury; genetic testing identifies the cause. Biopsy is sometimes needed.

Care: Prescribed disease-specific therapy/corticosteroids, stretching, PT and assistive devices; monitor cardiac and respiratory function.

Nursing: Prevent contractures and falls, protect nutrition/bone health, support independence and family/genetic counseling. [13,17]

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA)

What: Immune-mediated inflammatory arthritis beginning before age 16, lasting at least 6 weeks after other causes are excluded.

Signs: Swollen, warm joints; morning stiffness and limp. Systemic forms may cause fever and rash.

Diagnosis: History/exam, inflammatory markers and selected imaging; no single confirmatory blood test.

Treatment: Rheumatology care; NSAIDs for symptoms, DMARDs such as methotrexate and/or biologics to control inflammation; selected corticosteroids.

Nursing: Balance rest with gentle motion and low-impact exercise (swimming). Warmth eases stiffness; cold can ease swelling. Monitor growth, function and medicine effects.

Eye care: Scheduled slit-lamp exams are essential because uveitis may be silent. [14-16]

3 / 74Hemodynamic shock

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Hemodynamic shock

A condition in which tissue perfusion is inadequate to deliver oxygen and nutrients to support vital organs and cellular function and can lead to organ failure

Any condition that compromises oxygen delivery to organs and tissues can lead to shock

Low blood pressure occurs in all forms of shock!

Affects all body systems, resulting in potential organ failure

Rapidly-progressing and life-threatening process

Certain physiologic responses are common to all types of shock: hypoperfusion of tissues, hypermetabolism, and activation of the inflammatory response

Early detection of shock with rapid response is necessary to improve client outcomes

Treatment is based on the type and stage of the shock state

Types of shock (ATI, 2023)

Hypovolemic: A decrease in intravascular volume of at least 15% to 30%

Cardiogenic: failure of the heart to pump effectively due to a cardiac factor

Obstructive: Impairment of the heart to pump effectively as a result of a noncardiac factor

Distributive: Widespread vasodilation and increased capillary permeability, including neurogenic, septic, and anaphylactic shock

6 / 74Stages of shock (ATI, 2023)

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Stages of shock (ATI, 2023)

All types of shock progress through the same stages of shock and produce similar effects on body systems

Initial: no visible changes in client parameters; only changes on cellular level

MAP decreases 5 to 10 mm Hg from baseline; mild vasoconstriction; HR increases

Compensatory (non-progressive): Measures that increase CO to restore tissue perfusion and oxygenation

Vasoconstriction increases; HR increases; MAP decreases 10 to 15 mm Hg from baseline; mild acidosis and hyperkalemia

Progressive: Compensatory mechanisms start to fail to maintain CO and BP

MAP decreases >20 mm Hg from baseline; vital organs experience hypoxia; moderate acidosis and hyperkalemia

Refractory (irreversible): Irreversible shock and total body failure

Severe tissue hypoxia; MODS; possibly death

8 / 74Compensatory Stage of Shock 

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Compensatory Stage of Shock

Sympathetic NS causes vasoconstriction, increased HR, and increased contractility

This attempts to maintain blood pressure and CO

Body shunts blood away from skin, kidneys, and GI tract

Results in cool, clammy skin, hypoactive BS, and decreased UO

Perfusion of tissues has become inadequate

Acidosis occurs because of anaerobic metabolism (lactic acid formation)

Respiratory rate increases due to acids circulating in the bloodstream

Confusion may occur from evolving acidosis

10 / 74Progressive Stage of Shock

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Progressive Stage of Shock

Mechanisms that regulate BP can no longer compensate, and BP and MAP decrease

All organs suffer from hypoperfusion (including heart, lung, and brain)

Vasoconstriction of arterioles continues, further compromising cellular perfusion to organs

Mental status further deteriorates because of decreased cerebral perfusion and hypoxia

11 / 74Progressive Stage of Shock

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Progressive Stage of Shock

Lungs begin to fail:

Hypoxemia develops, CO2 increases, alveoli collapse, pulmonary edema can occur

Inadequate perfusion of the heart leads to dysrhythmias and ischemia

GFR cannot be maintained, and acute renal failure may occur when MAP falls below 65 mmHg- metabolic acidosis develops

Liver function, GI function, and hematologic function are all affected by low blood pressures

DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation) may occur as a complication of shock

Widespread clotting and bleeding occur

Bruises and bleeding appear on skin and mucous membranes

Coagulation times are increased from consumption of clotting factors (PTT, PT, and INR)

Clotting factors & platelets are consumed and may require replacement (lower clotting factors such as fibrinogen, platelets)

Nursing care for all types of shock

Monitor the following:

Oxygenation status- PRIORITY!

VS

Hourly urine output

16 / 74Why passive leg raising helps

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Temporary preload test: raising the legs shifts venous blood toward the heart. A measurable rise in stroke volume or cardiac output suggests fluid responsiveness.

Reassess promptly: use a real-time flow measurement when available. Blood pressure alone is less reliable; responsiveness does not automatically mean fluid is needed.

Nursing role: follow the ordered technique and consider injuries or positioning restrictions. This is a reversible assessment, not definitive treatment for shock.

17 / 74Intubation and mechanical ventilation

Open full size

Intubation and mechanical ventilation

Pre-intubation nursing actions

Monitor ECG, SaO2, breath sounds, and skin color

Preoxygenate with 100% oxygen

Assist with ventilation with manual resuscitation bag and mask

Suction as needed

Sedation and paralysis will be usually required (Ex: propofol and succinylcholine)

Postintubation nursing actions

Assess bilateral breath sounds, symmetrical chest movement

End-tidal CO2 monitoring and O2 saturation

CXR to confirm placement of tube in trachea

Secure ETT to avoid dislodgment

Post procedural sedation and analgesia needed (never allow paralysis alone)

Monitor all ventilator alarms (low pressure and high pressure)

18 / 74Hypovolemic Shock (fluid/blood loss)

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Hypovolemic Shock (fluid/blood loss)

Most common type

Decreased intravascular volume

Decreased CVP

15-30% reduction in volume

750 to 1500 mL of blood (in 70kg person)

Excessive fluid loss from burns, diuresis, vomiting or diarrhea, or blood loss secondary to surgery, trauma, gynecologic/obstetric causes

Remember the symptoms and signs! (BP, HR, RR, skin color, urine output?)

19 / 74Expected findings in hypovolemic shock

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

BP and heart rate: falling pressure or a narrowing pulse pressure with tachycardia; compensation can initially maintain BP.

Breathing and skin: tachypnea, cool pale clammy skin, weak pulses and delayed capillary refill.

Perfusion: decreasing urine output, thirst, anxiety, dizziness or altered mental status. Trend findings together rather than waiting for profound hypotension.

20 / 74 Hypovolemic Shock management

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Hypovolemic Shock management

Treat the underlying condition resulting in fluid loss!

Restore the fluids and stop the fluid loss concurrently

Fluid and/or blood replacement- isotonic crystalloids and/or colloids, blood products

Medications

Fill the tank first! (rehydrate with IV fluids and blood products if bleeding)

Replace fluid volume before using vasopressors/medications

Vasoactive meds result in constricting arterioles, helping to maintain systemic BP

Meds given to reverse the underlying cause

Nursing Management

Oxygen

Administration of blood and fluids

23 / 74Mr. Lopez: priority findings

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Suspect internal hemorrhage: blunt abdominal trauma, a firm abdomen and periumbilical bruising suggest abdominal injury and concealed blood loss.

Perfusion is threatened: BP 90/54, pulse 120/min and positional dizziness are concerning even though he is oriented.

Act: activate the trauma response; reassess airway, breathing, circulation and bleeding. Keep him supine as tolerated, monitor closely, establish large-bore IV access and prepare blood and urgent source control.

25 / 74Mr. Lopez: why these orders?

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

NS and blood: crystalloid supports circulating volume; transfusion restores oxygen-carrying capacity and replaces hemorrhagic losses. Verify compatibility and monitor for reactions.

CBC/electrolytes: assess anemia, platelets and electrolyte changes; an initial hemoglobin may not show the full acute loss. Oxygen supports delivery while perfusion is restored.

Imaging: investigate the injury. SIDE NOTE: NS at 125 mL/hour and oxygen at 2 L/min may be inadequate for unstable hemorrhage. Escalate; FAST, CT when stable, or immediate intervention may be needed rather than relying on a plain abdominal X-ray.

27 / 74Goals for hypovolemic shock

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Stop the loss: locate and control bleeding or the cause of fluid loss.

Restore circulation and oxygen delivery: improve mentation, pulses, BP trend, capillary refill and urine output while avoiding hypothermia.

Prevent organ injury: reassess response frequently; follow lactate, hemoglobin, electrolytes and coagulation studies as ordered. Escalate persistent hypoperfusion.

28 / 74Cardiogenic Shock (Pump failure)

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Cardiogenic Shock (Pump failure)

The heart's ability to contract and to pump blood is impaired due to a direct cardiac cause (the pump is failing!)

MI, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, dysrhythmias, and valvular rupture/stenosis

70% mortality rate- highest mortality of all types of shock since not easily reversible

Difficult to treat

Client cannot tolerate a fluid bolus to raise blood pressure

Results in lack of forward flow of blood (CO) and back-flow of blood into lungs

Inotropes (dopamine, dobutamine) can be used to stimulate HR and contractility

Vasopressors (norepinephrine) can be used to raise BP by increasing vascular resistance

Oxygen, morphine

Nitroglycerin (works in conjunction with dobutamine and dopamine)

31 / 74Why these cardiogenic-shock tests?

Open full size
Read slide text and study notes

Troponin/cardiac enzymes: identify myocardial injury. BNP reflects ventricular stretch and supports evaluation of heart failure; neither result stands alone.

12-lead ECG: detect ischemia/infarction or a dysrhythmia that may impair pumping. Echocardiography assesses ventricular function, valves and mechanical complications.

Cardiac catheterization: define coronary obstruction and permit revascularization when indicated. SIDE NOTE: transthoracic echo is TTE; transesophageal echo is TEE. The source slide mixes these abbreviations.

    33 / 74Cardiogenic Shock Medications

    Open full size
    Read slide text and study notes

    Cardiogenic Shock Medications

    Inotropic agents (milrinone, dobutamine)

    Strengthens cardiac contraction and increases CO while decreasing afterload

    Given IV, constant hemodynamic monitoring, titrate to prescribed parameters, often combined with a vasopressor

    Can result in dysrhythmias and rapid heart rates which may need lowering/stopping of medication

    Phentolamine is reversal agent if extravasates into tissue

    Vasopressors (dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine)

    Increases vascular resistance by constricting arterioles thereby helping increase BP

    Raises BP by vasoconstricting which helps in keeping the blood on the arterial side

    Increases kidney perfusion at low doses; decreases kidney perfusion at high doses

    Given IV, constant hemodynamic monitoring, titrate to prescribed parameters, monitor UOP, administer through a central line to prevent extravasation

    Phentolamine is reversal agent if vasopressor extravasates into tissue

    Vasodilators (nitroglycerin)

    Low doses- venous vasodilator and reduces preload

    Higher doses- arterial vasodilation and reduces afterload, increases CO (in combination with dobutamine), minimizes cardiac workload, enhances blood flow to myocardium

    Clinical side note

    34 / 74Side note: dopamine and kidney protection

    Open full size
    Read slide text and study notes

    The original medication slide is retained. Low-dose dopamine is not recommended to prevent or treat acute kidney injury.

    Manage perfusion and the cause of shock; monitor urine output and renal function. Vasopressors are selected and titrated to the clinical plan, not prescribed as “renal protection.”

    Phentolamine is used for certain vasopressor extravasation events under protocol; report pain, pallor or swelling around an infusion immediately.

    35 / 74Distributive Shock (leaky blood vessels)

    Open full size
    Read slide text and study notes

    Distributive Shock (leaky blood vessels)

    Intravascular fluid leaks out of peripheral vessels

    Not enough blood returns to the heart

    Leads to inadequate tissue perfusion from loss of fluid into interstitial space

    Three types:

    Septic shock: circulating bacteria cause inflammation/vasodilation, causing leaky blood vessels (low BP)

    Neurogenic shock: lack of sympathetic NS causes vasodilation, and bradycardia (low BP)

    Anaphylactic shock: allergen causes inflammation, vasodilation, and leaky blood vessels (low BP)

    37 / 74Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS)

    Open full size
    Read slide text and study notes

    Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS)

    Results from a clinical insult that initiates a systemic inflammatory response

    Clients with SIRS may be exhibiting protective inflammatory response to the initial insult or exhibiting a response to infection, which may lead to sepsis

    Monitor common causes:

    Multiple trauma

    Infection (sepsis)

    Criteria

    Only need 2/4

    Temp >38.3°C (101°F) or <36°C (96.8°F)

    Tachycardia >90

    Tachypnea >20

    WBC > 12,000 cells/mm3 or < 4000 cells/mm3 or >10% bands or 10% of immature bands

    Can you make yourself have SIRS right now?

    It's an indication that "something" is occurring but not specific yet- more monitoring and testing needed

    Monitoring may help "declare" the cause of the SIRS (sepsis? pneumonia? appendicitis?)

    Completed answer

    38 / 74Can exercise “give you SIRS”?

    Open full size
    Read slide text and study notes

    Teaching point: exercise can briefly raise heart rate and respiratory rate enough to meet two old SIRS thresholds.

    Interpretation: these numbers are nonspecific. Exercise is not the same as pathologic systemic inflammation, and SIRS alone does not establish infection or sepsis.

    Sepsis assessment: evaluate suspected infection with organ dysfunction and the whole clinical picture; normal temperature does not rule out serious infection.

    THE SEQUENTIAL ORGAN FAILURE ASSESSMENT (SOFA)

    Identifies clients at higher risk for mortality from their suspected infection

    Measure of organ dysfunction

    If score decreases by 2 or more from baseline, this is suggestive of organ dysfunction

    This is a tool that is part of a hospital toolkit for sepsis surveillance

    Clinical side note

    40 / 74Side note: direction of the SOFA score

    Open full size
    Read slide text and study notes

    Correction: an acute INCREASE of 2 or more SOFA points from baseline supports organ dysfunction associated with suspected infection.

    The source says “decreases.” That direction is reversed: a lower score generally reflects less organ dysfunction.

    Sepsis does not require positive blood cultures or organisms circulating in the bloodstream; infection may begin at a localized site.

    41 / 74Septic Shock

    Open full size
    Read slide text and study notes

    Septic Shock

    Most common type of distributive shock

    Widespread infection (usually bacterial) that has entered the bloodstream

    Endotoxins and other mediators causing massive vasodilation and inflammation

    The deadliest type is gram-negative bacteria (UTI? Pneumonia? Cholecystitis?)

    40% mortality rate

    One of the most common causes of death in ICUs

    Continues to rise, so important to notice SIRS criteria initially

    Age extremes

    Malnourishment

    43 / 74Hour-1 Sepsis Bundle, 2021 Sepsis guidelines (AACN, 2021)

    Open full size

    Hour-1 Sepsis Bundle, 2021 Sepsis guidelines (AACN, 2021)

    Measure lactate and remeasure if initial lactate is > 2 mmol/L

    Obtain blood cultures before administering antibiotics

    Administer broad-spectrum antibiotics

    Begin rapid administration of crystalloid (LR is recommended) for hypotension or lactate ≥ 4mmol/L

    Start vasopressors if hypotensive during or after fluid resuscitation to maintain MAP ≥ 65 mmHg

    45 / 74Why choose lactated Ringer’s?

    Open full size
    Read slide text and study notes

    LR is a balanced crystalloid: its electrolyte composition and buffer reduce the chloride load compared with 0.9% saline.

    Large saline volumes can contribute to hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis. Balanced solutions are suggested for many adults with sepsis; the choice remains individualized.

    Reassess after fluid: watch perfusion, oxygenation, lung sounds and fluid balance. Heart/kidney disease or brain injury may change the plan; more fluid is not always better.

    Open full size

    Neurogenic Shock

    Medical Management:

    Stabilization of SCI with neck immobilization, rigid backboard or firm mattress

    Proper positioning, avoid movements unless proper logrolling for assessment

    BP needs maintaining with vasopressor, bradycardia may require atropine

    Nursing Management:

    Ensure proper positioning

    HOB at least 30 degrees with spinal anesthesia

    Immobilize SCI pts to prevent further damage

    Prevention of thrombus formation (LMWH SC or heparin SC)

    Monitor for skin breakdown and skin ulcers

    49 / 74Anaphylactic Shock

    Open full size
    Read slide text and study notes

    Anaphylactic Shock

    Caused by a widespread hypersensitivity reaction to an allergen (initial exposure sensitizes system)

    Subsequent exposure to an allergen- severe allergic reaction causing massive vasodilation, SVR falls, blood return to the heart is decreased dramatically (preload)

    Anaphylaxis occurs rapidly following exposure

    Life-threatening reaction: think airway (swelling), breathing (short of breath, wheezing), circulation (hypotension, tachycardia), skin (urticaria), GI (nausea and vomiting)

    Risk Factors: (can potentially be any allergen)

    Contrast media

    Transfusion reaction

    Bee stings, snake bites

    Latex sensitivity

    Severe allergy to food (peanuts) or medications (penicillin)

    Obstructive shock (Something is blocking pump)

    Cardiac pump failure d/t an indirect cardiac factor (all result in hypotension)

    Pulmonary embolus (blocks pulmonary artery)

    Cardiac tamponade (surrounding fluid in pericardium compresses heart chambers)

    Tension pneumothorax (collapsed lung under pressure compresses heart chambers)

    51 / 74Needle decompression and chest tube insertion

    Open full size
    Read slide text and study notes

    Needle decompression and chest tube insertion

    Procedure is used to relieve pressure from a tension pneumothorax

    Nursing actions

    Monitor ECG, SaO2, breath sounds, and color

    Sedate as needed, apply high flow oxygen

    Needle decompression immediately removes the pressure from pleural space

    Set up water seal chest-drainage system and attach it to suction

    Apply an airtight dressing to chest-tube insertion site

    54 / 74Chest drainage: bubbling and tidaling

    Open full size
    Read slide text and study notes

    Wet suction-control chamber: gentle continuous bubbling is expected when wet suction is operating. A dry-suction system does not need this bubbling.

    Water seal: intermittent bubbling can occur as air exits a pneumothorax; continuous bubbling suggests an air leak requiring assessment.

    Tidaling: the water-seal level may move with breathing. Absent tidaling can mean re-expansion or obstruction; assess the patient and tubing. Keep the unit upright below the chest and avoid routine clamping.

    Used when fluid therapy alone does not maintain MAP

    60 / 74Multiple Organ Dysfunction Syndrome (MODS)

    Open full size
    Read slide text and study notes

    Multiple Organ Dysfunction Syndrome (MODS)

    Altered organ function that requires medical intervention to support continued organ function

    Occurs from the release of toxic metabolites and destructive enzymes in response to inadequate oxygenation

    Can develop from inadequate tissue perfusion and reperfusion of ischemic cells, causing further tissue injury

    High mortality rate

    60% when more than 4 organs fail

    Prevention and aggressive treatment of shock is key to avoiding MODS

    Case Study: highlight priority findings

    Mr. Smith is a 77-year-old male patient admitted from a nursing home to the intensive care unit with septic shock secondary to urosepsis. The patient has an indwelling catheter in place from the nursing home with cloudy greenish, yellow-colored urine with sediments. The nurse removes the catheter after obtaining a urine culture and replaces it with a condom catheter attached to a drainage bag. The patient has a history of urinary and bowel incontinence. The patient is confused, afebrile, and hypotensive with a blood pressure of 82/44 mm Hg. His respiratory rate is 28 breaths/min. The pulse oximeter reading is at 88% room air, so the healthcare provider prescribed 2 to 4 L/min of oxygen per nasal cannula titrated to keep SaO2 greater than 92%. The patient responded to 2 L/min of oxygen per nasal cannula with an SaO2 of 93%. The patient has diarrhea. His blood glucose level is elevated at 160 mg/dL. The white blood count is 15,000, and the C-reactive protein, a marker for inflammation, is elevated. The patient is treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics and norepinephrine beginning at 2 mcg/min and titrated to keep systolic blood pressure greater than 100 mm Hg. A central venous access device was inserted and verified by chest x-ray for correct placement. An arterial line was placed in the right radial artery to closely monitor the patient’s blood pressure during the vasopressor therapy.

      70 / 74Norepinephrine: calculate the pump rate

      Open full size
      Read slide text and study notes

      Convert and find concentration: 16 mg = 16,000 mcg. 16,000 mcg ÷ 250 mL = 64 mcg/mL.

      Calculate: (2 mcg/min × 60 min/hour) ÷ 64 mcg/mL = 1.875 mL/hour, approximately 1.9 mL/hour if rounding to tenths.

      Safety: independently verify the concentration and smart-pump setting per policy. Titrate to the actual order with continuous hemodynamic monitoring; use the ordered line and assess the site.

          Completed answer

          74 / 74Why nutrition matters during shock

          Open full size
          Read slide text and study notes

          Stress is catabolic: inflammation and stress hormones increase energy needs and break down muscle/protein. Nutrition supports healing and recovery.

          After stabilization: use enteral nutrition when the gut is usable and the patient can tolerate feeding; coordinate with the dietitian and critical-care team.

          Safety: uncontrolled shock or worsening perfusion may require withholding or adjusting feeds. Monitor tolerance, glucose, fluid balance and electrolytes.

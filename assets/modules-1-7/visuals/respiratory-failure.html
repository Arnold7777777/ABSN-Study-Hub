Oxygen in. Carbon dioxide out.

Respiratory failure can involve oxygenation, ventilation, or both. Assess the child as well as the monitor.

Oxygenation · Type I pattern

Too little oxygen in blood

Hypoxemic respiratory failure means inadequate blood oxygen. Carbon dioxide may be normal or low.

Impaired oxygen transfer can have several causes. A single alveolus illustration does not show every mechanism.

NHLBI: Diagnosing respiratory failure · NHLBI: Respiratory failure symptoms

Ventilation · Type II pattern

Carbon dioxide builds up

Hypercapnic respiratory failure means inadequate carbon dioxide removal. Acute CO₂ retention often lowers pH; chronic compensation changes the blood-gas pattern.

Low oxygen can occur at the same time. The two patterns can overlap.

NHLBI: Diagnosing respiratory failure

Read the measurements

SpO₂ does not measure CO₂

Pulse oximetry estimates oxygen saturation. Arterial blood gases provide information about oxygen, carbon dioxide and acid–base status.

A reassuring saturation can coexist with poor ventilation, especially during supplemental oxygen. Interpret trends and clinical findings together.

FDA: Pulse oximeters · AAP/AAPD: Pediatric sedation monitoring

Observe the child

Effort, air entry, alertness

Look for retractions, nasal flaring, grunting, abnormal rate and reduced air entry. A tired child may show less effort while getting worse.

Drowsiness, apnea, poor respiratory effort or bradycardia demand urgent escalation. There is no fixed order in which warning signs always appear.

Earliest sign of low oxygen: restlessness, anxiety and confusion. Act on them; don’t wait for the saturation to fall.

Side note (Codex): the two types can overlap, and the ABG numbers are a definition, not a reason to wait. A child who is tiring or drowsy needs help now.

Act on deterioration. Get urgent pediatric/emergency help; assess and support airway and breathing. Give oxygen when indicated and assist ventilation when breathing is inadequate, within training and protocol. Do not wait for a blood-gas threshold before responding to a deteriorating child.

Royal Children’s Hospital: The seriously unwell child

Try it before you reveal it

SpO₂ looks reassuring on oxygen, but breathing is shallow and the child is drowsy. Reassured?

No. Oxygen saturation does not establish adequate ventilation. Reassess urgently, escalate and support airway/breathing as indicated; evaluate ventilation and CO₂ with the clinical team.

Retractions become less visible as a child grows exhausted. Is this necessarily improvement?

No. Less visible effort may reflect fatigue and inadequate breathing. Assess air entry, rate, mental status and the whole clinical picture immediately.

Can oxygenation failure and ventilation failure happen together?

Yes. Low blood oxygen and CO₂ retention can coexist. The treatment plan responds to the underlying problem and current clinical status.

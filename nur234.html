Use the nursing process on every topic

The comprehensive final covers Modules 1–13. Use the full Exam 1–4 topic lists below, including topics omitted from the shorter final outline.

  1. Recognize cues: collect history, vital signs, symptoms, laboratory results, fetal/newborn findings and the family’s concerns.
  2. Analyze cues: explain the pathophysiology; distinguish expected adaptation from deterioration.
  3. Prioritize hypotheses: identify the greatest immediate threat and justify the priority.
  4. Generate solutions: plan nursing care, consultation, treatment preparation and teaching that fit the person’s preferences and circumstances.
  5. Take action: implement the indicated care, communicate urgent findings and coordinate transitions.
  6. Evaluate outcomes: compare trends with measurable goals and revise the plan.

Use a qualified interpreter when needed, ask about cultural and family preferences, protect informed choice and confirm understanding with teach-back. Agree on follow-up and access to help rather than assuming teaching alone resolves barriers.

Dosage calculations: work the units, then check the patient

Oxytocin infusion example

Order: 2 milliunits/min. Bag: 10 units in 1,000 mL.

10 × 1,000 = 10,000 milliunits; 10,000 ÷ 1,000 = 10 milliunits/mL. Rate = (2 ÷ 10) × 60 = 12 mL/hour.

Magnesium infusion example

Order: 4 g in 100 mL over 30 minutes.

30 minutes = 0.5 hour. Rate = 100 ÷ 0.5 = 200 mL/hour.

These are arithmetic practice examples, not standing doses. Verify the actual order, concentration, pump settings, compatibility, required independent check and maternal/fetal monitoring. Reassess the response rather than stopping at the calculation.

🧭 The counseling pathway

  1. Ask about pregnancy goals and preferences.
  2. Assess health, medicines and practical access.
  3. Compare options together.
  4. Use teach-back and make a follow-up plan.

📣 Professor’s focus

  • Decisions about if, when and how many children to have.
  • Physical, emotional, ethical, religious, cultural, financial and legal influences on decisions.
  • Counseling that respects autonomy and supports emergency contraception or termination discussions as appropriate.

🛡️ Method families

  • Fertility awareness / behavioral: cycle-based methods, abstinence and lactational amenorrhea when its criteria are met.
  • Barrier: condoms, diaphragm, cervical cap and spermicides.
  • Hormonal: pills, patch, ring, injection and implant.
  • IUD: copper or hormonal.
  • Permanent: vasectomy or tubal surgery.
  • Emergency: pills or copper IUD.

🎯 What to be ready to explain

  • Match the method to the client’s needs and health status.
  • Recognize risks that can limit estrogen-containing methods, including relevant DVT/PE history.
  • Teach STI prevention: condoms reduce risk; most contraceptives do not protect against STIs.
  • Apply advantages, disadvantages and patient education in a case scenario.
🧠 Does an effective pregnancy-prevention method automatically prevent STIs?

No. Discuss STI prevention separately, including condoms, screening and safer-sex practices.

  • Amniotic membranes / fluid volumes

Practice the module objectives
  • Describe the growth and development of the fetus by gestational age in weeks.
  • Identify fetal growth and development through maternal and pregnancy landmarks.
  • Describe nursing care to help ensure both a safe fetal environment and a safe pregnancy outcome.

Use the clinical-judgment prompts to move from cues to actions and measurable outcomes.

Organ origins and gestational landmarks

Germ layers provide the starting tissues: ectoderm forms skin and the nervous system; mesoderm forms muscle, bone, blood and much of the cardiovascular and urinary systems; endoderm forms much of the digestive and respiratory lining. Label the clock: gestational age starts with the last menstrual period and is about two weeks ahead of age since conception. At gestational weeks 5–10, major structures are forming. Later pregnancy adds growth and functional maturation: movement becomes noticeable around midpregnancy; airways and surfactant continue developing in the late second and third trimesters. Organ formation does not mean an organ is ready for life outside the uterus.

Read more: openstax.org · medlineplus.gov

AFP, CVS and amniocentesis

AFP is a maternal blood screening test, commonly offered at 15–20 weeks; an abnormal result signals a need for further evaluation and is not a diagnosis. CVS samples placental tissue, usually at 10–13 weeks, to diagnose selected chromosome/genetic disorders; it does not assess every birth defect. Amniocentesis samples amniotic fluid, usually at 15–20 weeks, for diagnostic testing. Explain each test’s purpose, possible results and procedure risks, support informed choice, and follow ordered pre/postprocedure assessments.

Follow development from fertilization to fetal growth, then explain how the placenta and fetal shunts support oxygen delivery.

DevelopmentThree shuntsBirth transition
📍 Locate the tube and uterine lining · labeled diagram
External anatomyPelvic side viewMale anatomy

🌱 Development in sequence

  1. Gametes → fertilization → zygote
  2. Cell division → implantation
  3. Embryonic development and organ formation
  4. Fetal growth and organ maturation

📣 Professor’s focus

  • Gametogenesis, fertilization, implantation, embryo and fetus.
  • Placenta, amniotic fluid and umbilical cord: identify each support role.
  • Review quickening, heartbeat detection, surfactant and gestational-age milestones.
  • Connect fundal height, movement counting, NST and BPP to fetal assessment.

🩺 Therapeutic communication

  • Ask about physical and emotional concerns.
  • Explore personal or cultural beliefs and available support.
  • Use the e-book to make flashcards, notes and study guides.
  • The wrap-up referred to an exam-review email; check the current course calendar for actual dates.

🫀 Three fetal shunts — simplified route map

1 · Ductus venosus

  • Umbilical vein → ductus venosus → inferior vena cava.
  • Diverts part of placental blood past liver circulation.

2 · Foramen ovale

  • Right atrium → left atrium.
  • Atrial route that helps bypass the high-resistance fetal lungs.

3 · Ductus arteriosus

  • Pulmonary artery → descending aorta.
  • Diverts much of right-ventricular output away from the lungs.

At birth

  • Breathing lowers pulmonary vascular resistance; cord clamping removes placental circulation.
  • Atrial pressures shift, and oxygen/prostaglandin changes promote closure over time.

This is a route guide, not the complete circulation. Some blood still reaches the fetal lungs and liver.

🧠 Which shunt connects the right atrium to the left atrium?

The foramen ovale. The ductus arteriosus connects the pulmonary artery to the aorta.

🔎 Study clarifications added

These notes clarify the supplied wrap-up; they are not quotations from Professor Buhler.

  • Distinguish post-fertilization age from gestational age dated from LMP. The fetal period starts after 8 completed weeks after fertilization—about 10 weeks gestational age. Organs continue developing and maturing.
  • Milestones have ranges. Doppler detection depends on timing and conditions; viability is not one guaranteed week or weight.
  • “Lungs off” is a memory aid: the placenta performs gas exchange, but the lungs develop and receive some blood. Shunts do not all anatomically close instantly.

🤰PROFESSOR BUHLER · WEEK 3 / MODULE 3Pregnancy changes: mind & body

Condensed from Professor Buhler’s weekly wrap-up supplied by the student. Chapter 10 · Psychological and Physiological Changes. Added study aids and clinical clarifications are identified below.

Recognize expected adaptations, support emotional adjustment and distinguish suggestive findings from definitive pregnancy evidence.

Trimester tasksPregnancy signsBody systems

🧠 The trimester tasks

  1. 1st trimester: accepting the pregnancy
  2. 2nd trimester: accepting the fetus
  3. 3rd trimester: preparing for birth

📣 Psychological focus

  • Readiness, relationships, partner involvement, family, culture and stress shape the experience.
  • Ambivalence and changes in attention, mood or sexual desire may occur.
  • Ask about depression, anxiety, coping and support; do not assume one emotional response fits everyone.
  • Couvade describes pregnancy-like symptoms in some partners.

🔍 Three kinds of pregnancy signs

  • Presumptive: subjective changes such as amenorrhea, nausea, fatigue, breast changes or quickening.
  • Probable: objective but not definitive findings, including hCG testing and Goodell, Chadwick or Hegar signs.
  • Positive: fetal cardiac activity, ultrasound evidence, or fetal movement felt by an examiner.

❤️ Circulation, kidneys & metabolism

  • Blood volume and cardiac workload increase; renal filtration rises.
  • Urinary frequency is common, particularly early and late in pregnancy.
  • Placental hormones increase insulin resistance as pregnancy progresses.
  • Supine uterine pressure can reduce venous return and cause symptoms.

🌿 Other adaptations

  • Uterine/breast changes, nausea, reflux, constipation and back discomfort.
  • Lordosis, skin pigmentation, linea nigra and striae.
  • Promote early prenatal care, prescribed prenatal vitamins and folic acid.
  • Fundal height helps assess growth; use gestational dating and the clinical context.
🧠 Is a positive home pregnancy test a “positive sign” in this nursing classification?

It is classified as a probable sign. The category name refers to evidence of a fetus, not whether a test result reads positive.

🔎 Study clarifications added

These notes clarify the supplied wrap-up; they are not quotations from Professor Buhler.

  • Trimester tasks are a teaching framework, not required emotional stages. Persistent mood symptoms deserve assessment.
  • Pregnancy involves complex immune adaptation; avoid treating it as a universal “IgG down / immunity off” rule.
  • New severe headache, visual change, chest pain or difficulty breathing should not be dismissed as a normal discomfort.

ACOG: prenatal care · CDC: urgent maternal warning signs

Module 4: Assessment of a Pregnant Family

  • Prenatal care/screening
  • Terms related to pregnancy status
  • Gestational classification/history / GTPAL
  • Fundal height and fetal heart tones
  • Pelvic anatomy / pelvic exam
  • Laboratory screenings
  • Danger signs during pregnancy
Practice the module objectives
  • Describe the areas of health assessment commonly included in prenatal visits.
  • Identify the readiness for parenthood and the health status of a pregnant woman and her family.

Use the clinical-judgment prompts to move from cues to actions and measurable outcomes.

Assess the family as well as the pregnancy

Ask about the person’s goals, feelings about pregnancy, coping, mental health, available support, relationship safety, language needs, cultural preferences, food/housing security and access to visits. Screen for intimate partner violence privately. Agree on practical referrals and use teach-back. Readiness for parenthood is an assessment conversation, not a judgment based on age or family structure.

Read more: openstax.org

Pelvic anatomy and examination

Follow the passage from pelvic inlet through the midpelvis to the outlet; the ischial spines are the reference for fetal station. When indicated, a pelvic assessment may include inspection of external genitalia, a speculum examination of the vagina/cervix, and bimanual assessment of pelvic organs. Explain the purpose, obtain consent, protect privacy, offer support and stop if consent is withdrawn. Pelvic shape labels alone do not determine whether vaginal birth is possible.

Read more: openstax.org

📣 Assessment priorities

  • Maternal baseline, gestational age, fetal growth and risk factors.
  • Nutrition, activity, substance use, mental health, support and private screening for intimate partner violence.
  • Anticipatory teaching for pregnancy, lactation and newborn care.
  • Monitor blood pressure and symptoms at prenatal visits.

🗓️ Dating & measurements

  • Naegele’s rule: LMP + 7 days − 3 months + 1 year; assumes a typical 28-day cycle.
  • Ultrasound and clinical dating may refine the estimated due date.
  • Fundus is around the umbilicus near 20 weeks; fundal height is a growth screen, not a stand-alone exact dating test.
  • Practice gestational-age, GTPAL and dosage calculations.

🧪 Screening toolbox

  • CBC, blood type/Rh and antibody screen, urine testing, HIV and other indicated infection tests.
  • Glucose screening and follow-up diagnostic testing as indicated.
  • AFP and first-trimester markers such as PAPP-A answer different screening questions.
  • Know the purpose and timing of Rh immune globulin and GBS screening.

⚠️ Recognize danger signs

  • Bleeding, persistent vomiting, fever or decreased fetal movement.
  • Severe headache, visual changes, sudden concerning swelling or upper abdominal pain need prompt evaluation.
  • A BP ≥140/90 mm Hg needs assessment in context; severe-range values require urgent response.
🧠 Do twins add two to T or P?

No. T and P count pregnancy outcomes/deliveries. L counts the currently living children.

🔎 Study clarifications added

These notes clarify the supplied wrap-up; they are not quotations from Professor Buhler.

  • For this GTPAL convention, term begins at 37 weeks 0 days; preterm is 20 weeks 0 days through 36 weeks 6 days. The 20-week documentation cutoff is not a guarantee of survival.
  • Routine GBS culture is at 36 weeks 0 days–37 weeks 6 days. Rh immune globulin is for eligible unsensitized Rh-negative patients, including around 28 weeks and after birth when indicated by infant Rh status, plus other sensitizing events.
  • AFP can suggest open neural-tube-defect risk but is not diagnostic. PAPP-A contributes to aneuploidy screening; it is not a neural-tube-defect test.
  • Pelvic-shape labels are historical teaching categories. Shape alone does not predict delivery outcome; pelvic examination components are performed when indicated.

Soft signs that labor may be approaching

Lightening means the presenting part descends lower into the pelvis; breathing may feel easier while pelvic pressure increases. 📣 Lifestyle & self-care

  • Hygiene, oral care, comfortable breast support and normal leukorrhea.
  • Moderate activity, rest and positions that avoid symptomatic supine compression.
  • Travel and work require attention to hydration, movement, lifting and exposures.
  • Discuss sex, exercise and work restrictions for the individual pregnancy.

⚠️ Exposure prevention

  • Review medicines and supplements before use. Avoid alcohol, tobacco and other harmful exposures.
  • Know infection risks, including the TORCH teaching group.
  • Live vaccines are generally avoided during pregnancy; assess recommended vaccines individually.
  • Avoid overheating and evaluate occupational or environmental hazards.

🍽️ Nutrition priorities

  • Varied foods with adequate protein, iron, calcium and folate; prescribed prenatal supplements.
  • Avoid raw/undercooked high-risk foods, unpasteurized products and high-mercury fish; follow pregnancy caffeine guidance.
  • Assess vomiting, inability to maintain intake, pica and barriers to food access.
  • Maternal PKU needs specialist phenylalanine control before and during pregnancy.

🕒 Discomforts & labor clues

  • Early: nausea, fatigue, breast tenderness and urinary frequency.
  • Later: reflux, constipation, back discomfort, ankle edema and Braxton Hicks contractions.
  • Review lightening, cervical change, contraction patterns, fluid leakage and bloody show.
  • Separate expected discomforts from severe or persistent symptoms.
🧠 Is rupture of membranes required before true labor begins?

No. True labor involves contractions with progressive cervical change. Membranes may rupture before or during labor.

🔎 Study clarifications added

These notes clarify the supplied wrap-up; they are not quotations from Professor Buhler.

  • Sex is often safe in an uncomplicated pregnancy, but ruptured membranes are not the only reason a clinician may recommend avoiding intercourse. Follow individual restrictions.
  • TORCH is a grouping of infections, not a universal routine panel for every pregnant patient.
  • PKU and maple syrup urine disease are different disorders. Maternal high phenylalanine can harm fetal development even when the fetus does not have PKU.
  • Bleeding, leaking fluid, persistent severe pain or reduced fetal movement should prompt assessment, rather than waiting for a textbook labor pattern.

Maternal response to labor

Contractions and pushing temporarily increase cardiovascular workload; pulse, blood pressure and breathing may rise during effort. Gastric emptying slows, so nausea may occur. Support rest, positioning, hydration as appropriate, bladder emptying and coping, and compare trends between contractions. Persistent tachycardia, hypotension, fever, low oxygen saturation or severe unexpected pain requires evaluation rather than being labeled normal labor.

Read more: openstax.org

Fetal response and recovery

Contractions briefly reduce uteroplacental blood flow. A fetus with adequate reserve normally tolerates these interruptions and recovers during relaxation. Excessively frequent contractions, maternal hypotension or placental disease can reduce that reserve. Interpret baseline, variability, accelerations, decelerations and recovery together with maternal status; a pattern alone is not a complete diagnosis.

Read more: openstax.org

📣 The four P’s

  • Passage: pelvis, cervix, vagina and soft tissues.
  • Passenger: fetus, head, presentation and molding.
  • Powers: uterine contractions and pushing.
  • Psyche: emotional response, coping and support.
  • Maternal position also affects comfort and labor mechanics.

🧭 Describe the fetus

  • Lie: relationship of fetal and maternal long axes.
  • Attitude: flexion or extension.
  • Presentation: the part entering first.
  • Position: presenting-part landmark relative to maternal pelvis, such as LOA.
  • Station: presenting part relative to ischial spines; 0 station is at the spines.

⭕ Dilation and effacement measure different changes

1 cm
4 cm
6 cm
10 cm

Dilation: opening, 0–10 cm. Effacement: thinning, 0–100%. Circles illustrate relative change and are not life-size.

🔄 Cardinal movements

  1. Engagement → descent
  2. Flexion → internal rotation
  3. Extension → external rotation
  4. Expulsion

⏱️ Stages of labor

  1. 1: labor onset with cervical change → full dilation
  2. 2: full dilation → birth
  3. 3: birth → placental delivery
  4. 4: immediate postpartum recovery and close monitoring

📊 Assess the pattern

  • Contractions: frequency, duration and intensity.
  • Cervix: dilation and effacement; fetal descent and station.
  • FHR baseline commonly 110–160 bpm; interpret variability, accelerations and decelerations together.
  • Early decelerations: often head compression. Late: uteroplacental concern. Variable: often cord compression.

💧 Membranes & support

  • Document rupture time, fluid color, odor and amount; assess FHR promptly.
  • Watch for cord prolapse and other causes of fetal compromise.
  • Promote mobility and positions when safe, continuous support and individualized comfort measures.
  • Skin-to-skin and early feeding are supported when parent and newborn are stable.
🧠 Does 0 station mean the cervix is fully dilated?

No. Station describes descent relative to the ischial spines. Dilation describes the cervical opening.

🔎 Study clarifications added

These notes clarify the supplied wrap-up; they are not quotations from Professor Buhler.

  • Labor duration varies widely. Active-phase management uses 6 cm as the start of active labor; the first stage is not a fixed 12 hours.
  • Nitrazine is one piece of membrane assessment and can give false-positive results; blue alone does not prove rupture.
  • A normal FHR immediately after rupture does not by itself exclude every cord problem. Evaluate the entire tracing and clinical situation.
  • Meconium or a single deceleration is not interpreted in isolation. Fetal oxygen saturation is not a routine direct measurement during standard monitoring.

ACOG: first and second stage labor management · ACOG: rupture-of-membranes evaluation

🤲PROFESSOR BUHLER · WEEK 7 / MODULE 7Comfort measures during labor & birth

From Professor Buhler’s Week 7 wrap-up supplied by the student. Chapter 16 · Comfort Measures During Labor and Birth. Added study aids and clinical clarifications are identified below.

Pain in labor is subjective and shaped by physical, emotional and cultural factors. Match comfort measures to the client, the stage of labor and the birth plan, and know when each medication is safe.

NonpharmacologicAnalgesia vs anesthesiaTiming + safety

🤲 Nonpharmacologic comfort

  • Effleurage, counter-pressure, hydrotherapy and guided imagery.
  • Position changes such as hands-and-knees and squatting; movement helps fetal descent and comfort.
  • Counter-pressure for back pain from an occiput posterior position.
  • Doulas and birth partners enhance comfort and a positive birth experience.

🧠 Why it works

  • Gate Control Theory: touch and distraction close the “gate”, so less pain reaches the brain.
  • Endorphins are the body’s own pain relief.
  • Client-centered care: tailor strategies to individual needs, cultural beliefs and the birth plan.
  • Pain is subjective. Use appropriate scales and validate the client’s experience.

💊 Analgesia or anesthesia?

Analgesics

Reduce the sensation of pain for a limited time; they do not eliminate it.

  • Butorphanol (Stadol)
  • Nalbuphine (Nubain)
  • Morphine sulfate
  • Fentanyl (Sublimaze)

Anesthesia

Reduces all sensation, not just pain, for a set length of time.

  • Regional: epidural, spinal
  • Local: local infiltration, pudendal block
  • General: emergencies only

⏱️ The timing rule

Narcotic analgesics cause maternal respiratory depression and fetal CNS and respiratory depression, so they should not be given within one hour of delivery. Always assess cervical dilation before giving an analgesic.

🩻 Epidural

  • Anesthetic (e.g. bupivacaine) plus an analgesic (morphine or fentanyl) into the epidural space at the 4th or 5th vertebra.
  • Helpful for clients with heart disease, pulmonary disease, diabetes and sometimes severe gestational hypertension. Preserves her energy.
  • Before it is placed: an IV bolus of 500–1000 mL to offset maternal hypotension.

💉 Spinal

  • Local anesthetic into the subarachnoid space (spinal fluid) at the 3rd, 4th or 5th lumbar interspace.
  • If a spinal headache occurs, the client returns to the hospital for evaluation by anesthesia.
  • Be vigilant for spinal headache and know the follow-up care.

📍 Local anesthesia

Reduces the ability of local nerve fibers to conduct pain.

  • Local infiltration: lidocaine into the superficial nerves of the perineum along the vulva.
  • Pudendal block: lidocaine or bupivacaine given trans-vaginally in front of the pudendal nerve; numbs the perineum, vulva and rectal area for delivery, episiotomy and repair.

🚨 General anesthesia

Rarely used for vaginal or cesarean births without complications. Used only for a delivery complication or emergency, or when nerve-block analgesia or anesthesia is contraindicated.

💡 Key concepts

  1. Pick pain relief by labor stage and client preference.
  2. Know epidural and spinal indications, contraindications and pre-procedure hydration.
  3. Watch for spinal headache after an epidural or spinal.
  4. Counter-pressure for occiput posterior.
  5. After any analgesic: monitor for respiratory depression and know when it is safe to give relative to delivery.
  6. Assess cervical dilation before analgesics.
  7. Comfort measures should be safe, effective and empowering; nurses educate, support and advocate.
🧠 Delivery is expected within the hour. Can you give the ordered opioid?

No. Opioids given within one hour of delivery can cause neonatal respiratory depression. Check cervical dilation first and report to the provider.

🧠 What must happen before an epidural is placed?

An IV fluid bolus of 500–1000 mL, to offset maternal hypotension.

Pregnancy Complications

Follow each condition from recognition to nursing priorities, teaching and reassessment. Open a topic to study its explanation and any paired graphics.

Practice the module objectives
  • Define high-risk pregnancy, including preexisting factors that contribute to its development such as diabetes mellitus or cardiovascular disease.
  • Describe the effect of a preexisting or newly acquired illness on pregnancy.
  • Identify expected outcomes to help ensure a safe pregnancy.

Use the clinical-judgment prompts to move from cues to actions and measurable outcomes.

  • Identifying a high-risk pregnancy
  • Cardiovascular disorders and pregnancy (left sided and right sided failure)
  • Chronic hypertensive vascular disease
  • Venous thromboembolic disease
  • Renal / Urinary disorders of pregnancy (UTI’s)
  • Endocrine disorders and pregnancy (Diabetes mellitus)
Identifying a high-risk pregnancy

Risk can exist before conception or develop during pregnancy. Review cardiac, renal and endocrine disease; chronic hypertension; prior pregnancy complications; medications and substance exposure; age, multifetal pregnancy and barriers to care. Establish baseline findings, reconcile medicines and arrange the appropriate obstetric or maternal–fetal medicine team. A useful outcome is a specific follow-up plan the client can explain, with warning signs and a clear route for urgent help.

Cardiovascular disorders: left- and right-sided failure

Pregnancy increases circulating volume and cardiac workload; postpartum fluid shifts can also precipitate decompensation. Left-sided failure tends to produce pulmonary congestion: worsening dyspnea, orthopnea and crackles. Right-sided failure tends to produce systemic venous congestion: dependent edema, neck-vein distention and hepatic congestion; both patterns can coexist. New breathlessness at rest, chest pain, syncope or hypoxemia warrants urgent evaluation. Trend oxygenation, lung sounds, perfusion, fluid balance and fetal status; coordinate a cardiac–obstetric care plan instead of assuming all edema or fatigue is normal pregnancy.

Chronic hypertensive vascular disease

Hypertension present before pregnancy or detected before 20 weeks suggests chronic hypertension. Compare new findings with baseline blood pressure, renal function and urine protein; a change may indicate superimposed preeclampsia. Teach medication adherence and reporting of severe headache, visual symptoms, upper abdominal pain or dyspnea. Prescribed treatment, fetal growth surveillance and postpartum follow-up reduce risk; antihypertensive selection must account for pregnancy, and ACE inhibitors/ARBs are generally avoided.

Venous thromboembolic disease

Pregnancy and especially the postpartum period increase venous clot risk. Unilateral painful swelling, warmth or redness suggests DVT; sudden unexplained dyspnea, chest pain, hemoptysis or collapse raises concern for pulmonary embolism. Escalate promptly and anticipate objective imaging and prescribed anticoagulation; do not massage the affected leg or rely on Homan’s sign. Heparin-based treatment is commonly used during pregnancy. Teach the individualized plan for injections, bleeding precautions and when to call the birth team because anticoagulation affects labor and neuraxial-anesthesia planning.

Renal / urinary disorders: bacteriuria, cystitis and pyelonephritis

Urinary stasis increases infection risk. Asymptomatic bacteriuria can be found on early prenatal urine culture; cystitis causes dysuria, urgency or suprapubic discomfort. Fever with flank pain or costovertebral-angle tenderness suggests pyelonephritis, which can lead to sepsis and preterm labor and is initially managed in hospital. Obtain the ordered urine studies, monitor maternal vital signs and fetal status as appropriate, and administer culture-directed antibiotics. Teach completing treatment and returning urgently for fever, worsening pain, vomiting or contractions.

Endocrine disorders: preexisting diabetes and gestational diabetes

Distinguish diabetes present before pregnancy from gestational diabetes detected during pregnancy. Hyperglycemia around organ formation raises congenital-anomaly risk; later hyperglycemia promotes excessive fetal growth and neonatal hypoglycemia after birth. Care combines individualized food planning, activity, glucose monitoring and prescribed medication, often insulin. Teach recognition and treatment of hypoglycemia and the sick-day plan; insulin requirements can change rapidly after birth. Assess fetal growth and arrange postpartum glucose follow-up for gestational diabetes.

Read more: publications.smfm.org · nhlbi.nih.gov · niddk.nih.gov · rcog.org.uk · acog.org

Evaluate outcomes: stable maternal perfusion, controlled glucose/BP, appropriate fetal growth, completed follow-up and a client who can explain when to seek help.

Exam 3 · Module 9

Sudden Pregnancy Complications

Follow each condition from recognition to nursing priorities, teaching and reassessment. Open a topic to study its explanation and any paired graphics.

Practice the module objectives
  • Identify sudden complications of pregnancy that place a pregnant woman and her fetus at risk.
  • Identify expected outcomes to minimize risk and manage seamless transitions across differing healthcare settings.
  • Identify the sequence of events that occur when clients have preeclampsia, cardiac disease, diabetes, and any sudden pregnancy complications.

Use the clinical-judgment prompts to move from cues to actions and measurable outcomes.

  • Spontaneous abortions
  • Ectopic pregnancy
  • Gestational trophoblastic disease
  • Premature cervical dilation
  • Placenta previa
  • Placental abruption
  • Disseminated intravascular coagulation
  • Preterm labor
  • Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy
  • HELLP syndrome
  • Multiple gestation
  • Amniotic fluid volume
  • Isoimmunization/Rhogam
Spontaneous abortion / miscarriage

Recognize bleeding, cramping, tissue passage and changes in the cervix, but establish viability and location with the prescribed examination, ultrasound and serial hCG. Threatened loss has bleeding with a closed cervix; incomplete loss leaves retained tissue, whereas complete loss has passed the pregnancy tissue. Heavy bleeding, syncope, fever or worsening pain requires urgent assessment. Management may be expectant, medication-based or procedural depending on stability, findings and preference. Use sensitive language, assess grief and explain follow-up; routine bed rest does not prevent miscarriage.

Ectopic pregnancy

Implantation outside the uterine cavity can cause unilateral pelvic pain and bleeding; rupture can produce shoulder-tip pain, syncope and shock with little visible bleeding. Assess stability first and urgently escalate suspected rupture. Ultrasound and serial hCG guide evaluation; an empty uterus alone does not establish an ectopic pregnancy. Selected stable patients may receive methotrexate, while others need surgery. Teach serial hCG follow-up, urgent return for worsening symptoms and medication-specific precautions.

Gestational trophoblastic disease / molar pregnancy

Abnormal trophoblastic growth may cause bleeding, unusually high hCG, marked nausea and a uterus larger than expected; partial moles may contain fetal tissue. Ultrasound and pathology establish the diagnosis. Following evacuation, serial hCG detects persistent disease, and reliable contraception prevents a new pregnancy from obscuring that monitoring. Follow-up duration depends on the type of mole, hCG response, treatment and specialist protocol; do not apply a universal one-year rule. Support grief as well as adherence to follow-up.

Premature cervical dilation / cervical insufficiency

Cervical insufficiency is typically painless cervical shortening or dilation in the second trimester, unlike labor driven by contractions with cervical change. Review prior losses or preterm births and the transvaginal cervical-length findings. Progesterone or cerclage may be indicated for selected patients; eligibility is based on history and current findings. Teach reporting of pressure, bleeding, fluid leakage, fever or contractions and the individualized plan for follow-up or stitch removal.

Placenta previa

The placenta overlies the cervical opening; bleeding is classically painless, but symptoms alone cannot confirm the diagnosis. Assess maternal circulation and fetal status and obtain the ordered ultrasound. Avoid a digital vaginal examination until previa has been excluded. Prepare for the obstetric plan based on bleeding, gestational age, placental location and maternal/fetal stability.

Placental abruption

Premature placental separation often produces abdominal or back pain, uterine tenderness or rigidity and fetal compromise. Bleeding may be concealed, so a small amount of visible blood does not rule out major hemorrhage. Activate urgent assessment, quantify bleeding, trend perfusion and fetal status, and anticipate IV access, blood studies and blood products or urgent birth. Assess for DIC. Unlike previa, this is separation of a normally implanted placenta; blood color alone is not a dependable discriminator.

Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

Widespread coagulation consumes platelets and clotting factors, producing both microvascular thrombosis and bleeding. Obstetric triggers include abruption, severe hypertensive disease, sepsis, major hemorrhage and amniotic fluid embolism. Watch for oozing at IV sites, bruising, persistent bleeding and worsening perfusion; platelets and fibrinogen may fall while coagulation times prolong. Treat the cause urgently and support oxygenation/circulation with the team’s blood-product and hemorrhage protocol. Reassess trends rather than a single laboratory value.

Preterm labor

Labor before 37 weeks requires contractions with cervical change; pressure, cramps, backache or leaking fluid should prompt evaluation. Assess gestational age, membranes, infection, bleeding and fetal status. Corticosteroids support fetal maturation when indicated; short-term tocolysis may allow time for treatment or transfer, while magnesium may be used for fetal neuroprotection at early gestations. These medicines have different purposes. Suspected infection, hemorrhage or maternal/fetal compromise may make delaying birth inappropriate; coordinate transport to a suitable neonatal-care setting.

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy

Gestational hypertension develops after 20 weeks without the findings that establish preeclampsia. Preeclampsia is new hypertension with proteinuria OR specified organ dysfunction; proteinuria is not required when qualifying severe features are present. Severe-range pressure (systolic at least 160 or diastolic at least 110), neurologic symptoms, pulmonary edema, low platelets, renal injury or liver involvement needs prompt escalation. Magnesium prevents/treats seizures; a separate antihypertensive treats severe blood pressure. During magnesium therapy monitor breathing, reflexes, urine output and mental status together; suspected toxicity requires stopping the infusion, calling for help and supporting breathing, with calcium gluconate per order.

HELLP syndrome

HELLP means hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes and low platelets. RUQ/epigastric pain, nausea, malaise or headache may accompany the laboratory abnormalities, and severe hypertension or proteinuria may be absent. Escalate suspected HELLP, assess bleeding and organ perfusion, and anticipate serial blood counts, liver/renal tests and coagulation studies. Stabilization and the timing of delivery require obstetric specialist management; continue surveillance postpartum.

Multiple gestation

Early ultrasound identifies the number of fetuses and whether they share a placenta or amniotic sac. Risks include preterm birth, growth problems, hypertensive disease, anemia and uterine overdistention; shared placentas introduce additional risks such as twin-to-twin transfusion. Individualize nutrition, growth and fluid surveillance and the birth plan. Teach preterm-labor and preeclampsia warning signs and arrange appropriate maternal and neonatal resources.

Amniotic fluid volume: oligohydramnios and polyhydramnios

Oligohydramnios is reduced fluid and may accompany membrane rupture, placental insufficiency or fetal renal/urinary problems; cord compression is a concern. Polyhydramnios is excessive fluid and may accompany diabetes, impaired fetal swallowing or multiple gestation; overdistention increases preterm-labor and postpartum-atony risk. Ultrasound quantifies fluid; use gestation and the clinical cause to interpret the result. Assess membranes, fetal growth and wellbeing and plan surveillance or treatment with the team; a fluid-volume label alone does not identify the cause.

Isoimmunization / Rho(D) immune globulin

An Rh-negative person can form antibodies after exposure to Rh-positive fetal red cells; those antibodies can affect an Rh-positive fetus. Check blood type and the antibody screen. Rh immune globulin prevents sensitization in eligible unsensitized patients—it does not reverse established sensitization. Routine prophylaxis is commonly given around 28 weeks and within 72 hours after birth when the infant is Rh-positive, with additional doses for indicated sensitizing events. Follow current gestation-specific guidance for early loss; a blanket rule after every loss is not appropriate. A positive antibody screen requires evaluation and, when indicated, fetal surveillance.

Read more: acog.org · nice.org.uk · acog.org · acog.org · acog.org · rcog.org.uk

Exam 3 · Module 10

Complications of Labor and Birth / Surgical Interventions

Follow each condition from recognition to nursing priorities, teaching and reassessment. Open a topic to study its explanation and any paired graphics.

Practice the module objectives
  • Describe common deviations in the power, passage or passenger which can cause complications during labor or birth.
  • Identify the usual indications for surgical interventions such as amniotomy, episiotomy and cesarean birth.
  • Discuss surgical interventions for labor including preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative management.

Use the clinical-judgment prompts to move from cues to actions and measurable outcomes.

  • Dysfunctional labor
  • Group B Streptococcus Aureus (GBS)
  • Induction / Augmentation of labor
  • Cervical ripening/Bishop score
  • Uterine rupture/uterine inversion
  • Amniotic fluid embolism
  • Umbilical cord prolapse
  • Posterior/breech position
  • Macrosomia
  • Shoulder dystocia
  • Inlet/outlet contraction
  • External cephalic version
  • Forceps/Vacuum-assisted delivery
  • Velamentous insertion
  • Vasa-previa
  • Placenta accreta/increta/percreta
  • Amniotomy
  • Episiotomy
  • Internal monitoring
  • Cesarean birth
Dysfunctional labor

Evaluate powers, passenger, passage and the person’s coping/support: contraction pattern, cervical change, fetal position, descent, bladder and maternal/fetal condition. Weak contractions, malposition and obstruction require different responses; more oxytocin is not the answer to every slow labor. Assess adequate time and progress using the current labor-management protocol rather than a fixed 1 cm/hour rule. Reassess after interventions and escalate an abnormal fetal tracing, bleeding or instability.

Group B streptococcus (GBS)

GBS is Streptococcus agalactiae; the review document’s wording “Streptococcus Aureus” is a naming error. Screening is performed late in each pregnancy, generally at 36–37 weeks. A positive screen usually calls for IV antibiotics during labor to reduce early newborn infection; prior affected infant or GBS bacteriuria can also establish an indication. Verify allergies, document the antibiotic and timing, and communicate with the newborn team. GBS colonization is not a sexually transmitted infection.

Induction / augmentation of labor

Induction starts labor; augmentation strengthens labor that has already begun. Confirm the indication, gestational age, fetal presentation, cervical readiness, consent and contraindications to vaginal birth. Monitor fetal heart rate, contraction activity and maternal response during oxytocin or cervical ripening. Tachysystole means more than five contractions in 10 minutes averaged over 30 minutes; reduce/stop oxytocin and follow the response protocol when indicated. Correct maternal hypotension and reposition as appropriate; routine oxygen is not recommended solely for an abnormal tracing when maternal oxygenation is normal.

Cervical ripening / Bishop score

The Bishop score combines cervical dilation, effacement, consistency, position and fetal station. A higher score indicates a more favorable cervix; it predicts readiness for induction, not the onset of spontaneous labor. An unfavorable cervix may need a mechanical balloon or a prescribed medication before oxytocin. Method selection accounts for uterine scars and other contraindications; monitor for excess contractions and fetal intolerance. Explain the sequence and reassess readiness after ripening.

Uterine rupture / uterine inversion

Rupture is disruption of the uterine wall, with fetal bradycardia, pain, bleeding, loss of station or maternal shock; symptoms vary and contractions need not stop. Activate the emergency response, stop oxytocin and prepare for urgent surgery with resuscitation. Inversion is the uterus turning inside out, usually after birth, and may cause hemorrhage, severe pain and shock. Call for immediate obstetric help, support circulation and prepare for prompt replacement by the trained clinician; avoid pulling on the cord or attempting placental removal independently.

Amniotic fluid embolism

Suspect AFE with sudden respiratory and cardiovascular collapse during labor or shortly after birth, often followed by coagulopathy. It is a clinical diagnosis; no single laboratory test confirms it. Activate the resuscitation and obstetric teams, support oxygenation/ventilation, and begin high-quality CPR if pulseless. Coordinate emergency delivery when indicated and early management of hemorrhage/DIC, avoiding indiscriminate fluid overload. This response is simultaneous team care, not a slow checklist.

Umbilical cord prolapse

The cord descends beside or below the presenting part and may become compressed, often after membrane rupture with a high presenting part. Sudden fetal bradycardia or prolonged deceleration requires immediate assessment. Call for help, stop oxytocin, relieve pressure by elevating the presenting part as trained, position to reduce compression and prepare urgent birth. Continue pressure relief until effective handover or birth; avoid unnecessary handling of the cord and never push it back into the uterus.

Posterior / breech position

Occiput posterior is a head-down fetus facing toward the maternal abdomen and may cause back pain, slow rotation and prolonged labor. Support movement/position changes and reassess rotation and progress with the team. Breech means buttocks or feet present first; confirm with ultrasound and plan individualized birth care. Persistent malpresentation increases risks such as cord prolapse and can require cesarean birth; it is not corrected by simply increasing contractions.

Macrosomia

A large estimated fetal size increases the risk of difficult birth, shoulder dystocia and maternal trauma, but ultrasound weight estimates are imprecise. Review diabetes control, growth and the obstetric birth plan. Prepare for neonatal assessment, including glucose monitoring when indicated. Do not assume every large fetus will have dystocia, or that a smaller fetus cannot have it.

Shoulder dystocia

After the head is born, an impacted shoulder prevents the body from delivering; the head may retract against the perineum. Call the emergency team, record times and assist with the designated maneuvers. McRoberts positioning and suprapubic pressure are common initial measures; do not apply fundal pressure or excessive traction on the head. After birth assess maternal injury/hemorrhage and the infant for hypoxia or trauma, then explain and debrief the event.

Inlet / outlet contraction

A narrowed pelvic inlet can interfere with engagement; a restricted outlet can impede descent or delivery. The relevant question is the fit and progress of this fetus through this pelvis, including size and position, not a pelvic label alone. Monitor descent, cervical change, contractions and fetal condition. Persistent obstruction despite adequate labor requires obstetric reassessment and may require cesarean birth; oxytocin must not be used to force a fetus through an obstructed passage.

External cephalic version (ECV)

A trained clinician attempts to turn a breech fetus to a head-down presentation by external abdominal manipulation. Confirm eligibility, consent, presentation, placental location and fetal wellbeing, with ultrasound and fetal monitoring before/after the attempt. ECV is performed where urgent cesarean delivery can be provided if needed; a tocolytic may be prescribed. Assess Rh status and provide Rh immune globulin if indicated. Teach reporting of bleeding, pain, contractions, reduced movement or leaking fluid after discharge.

Forceps / vacuum-assisted delivery

An operative vaginal birth may help when the second stage is prolonged or birth needs to be expedited and prerequisites are met. Confirm full dilation, ruptured membranes, known position, engaged head, an emptied bladder, adequate analgesia and informed consent, with a backup plan. Assist the trained operator and monitor fetal status. After birth assess for maternal lacerations or hematoma and newborn scalp, facial or nerve injury; concerning swelling or neurologic change needs escalation.

Velamentous insertion

The cord inserts into the membranes, so fetal vessels travel without the usual protection of Wharton’s jelly before reaching the placenta. Those vessels are vulnerable to compression or rupture. Ultrasound assessment of the insertion and vessel course helps determine whether vasa previa is also present. Communicate the finding and follow the individualized surveillance and birth plan; velamentous insertion and vasa previa are related but are not interchangeable terms.

Vasa previa

Unprotected fetal vessels pass over or close to the cervical opening. Vessel rupture with labor or membrane rupture can cause rapid fetal blood loss, even while the mother remains relatively stable. Antenatal ultrasound diagnosis permits a planned birth before labor/rupture; the timing is individualized by the specialist team. Bleeding with sudden fetal compromise after rupture is an emergency. Avoid amniotomy when vasa previa is suspected or known.

Placenta accreta / increta / percreta

Placenta accreta spectrum describes abnormally adherent or invasive placentation: accreta attaches to the myometrium, increta invades it and percreta extends through the uterine wall and sometimes into nearby organs. Prior cesarean birth plus previa raises concern. The main danger is massive hemorrhage during attempted placental separation. Plan birth with an experienced multidisciplinary team and blood-product capability; do not forcibly remove the placenta. Teach the individualized surgical plan, which may include hysterectomy.

Amniotomy

Artificial rupture of membranes may induce or augment labor or permit selected internal monitoring. Verify the order, presentation and engagement, and exclude placental/vessel contraindications. Assess fetal heart rate before and immediately after rupture because cord prolapse or compression may occur. Document time and fluid color, odor and amount; continue surveillance for infection and minimize unnecessary vaginal examinations after rupture.

Episiotomy

An episiotomy is a surgical incision of the perineum to enlarge the vaginal opening for selected birth circumstances; routine use is not recommended. Explain the indication and provide appropriate analgesia. After repair assess pain, swelling, wound approximation, hematoma and infection; teach perineal care, bowel comfort and when to report worsening symptoms. Severe persistent pain or pressure can suggest a hematoma rather than ordinary soreness.

Internal monitoring

A fetal scalp electrode measures fetal heart rate directly; an intrauterine pressure catheter measures contraction pressure and resting tone. These answer different questions. Placement requires ruptured membranes and appropriate access, with assessment for contraindications such as relevant maternal infection or fetal bleeding risk. Maintain aseptic technique and watch for infection or trauma; compare the data with the complete maternal/fetal assessment. An internal monitor does not replace clinical judgment.

Cesarean birth: preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative care

Before surgery, confirm identity, consent and indication; assess maternal/fetal status, allergies, laboratory results and ordered IV access, antibiotics and thrombosis prevention. During surgery assist positioning and anesthesia monitoring, maintain safety checks, quantify blood loss and support the parent and newborn. Afterward trend circulation, fundus/lochia, incision, urine output, pain and respiratory status; encourage appropriate mobility and feeding support. Teach wound care and urgent symptoms such as heavy bleeding, chest pain, dyspnea, fever or a painful swollen leg. Evaluate recovery and provide a clear discharge/follow-up plan.

During the puerperium, retrogressive changes reverse pregnancy adaptations, including uterine and vaginal involution. After birth, the cervix gradually narrows and firms; after vaginal birth its external opening usually remains a transverse slit. Vaginal swelling resolves as injured tissues heal.

💉 Immunizations

Live vs inactivated is the distinction that decides who can be vaccinated, and it drives almost every question on the topic.

⚖️ Live vs inactivated

This one distinction answers most immunization questions.

Live vaccines contain a weakened organism, so they are withheld from anyone who cannot fight even a weakened infection.

 LIVE attenuatedInactivated / subunit
ExamplesMMR, Varicella, Rotavirus, intranasal flu, BCGDTaP, Hib, IPV, Hep A & B, PCV, HPV, injectable flu, Tdap
Give to immunocompromised?NOYes
Give in pregnancy?NOYes — Tdap is recommended
SpacingGive together or 4 weeks apartNo spacing rule

🚨 Withhold live vaccines when

  • Immunocompromised — chemotherapy, high-dose steroids, HIV with low CD4, transplant
  • Pregnant
  • Recently had IVIG or a blood product — wait about 11 months; the antibodies block the response
  • Severe febrile illness — postpone until recovered

A mild cold with a low fever is NOT a reason to postpone. Withholding vaccines for minor illness is the single commonest missed opportunity.

The three anchors worth memorizing: MMR and varicella are not given before 12 months, because maternal antibodies interfere before then. Tdap, HPV and MenACWY are the adolescent set at 11–12. And MenACWY comes back at 16 — the dose most often missed, because people read 11–12 and think the job is done.

🧠 True contraindications

  • Anaphylaxis to a previous dose or a vaccine component
  • Severe egg allergy — care with some influenza formulations
  • Encephalopathy within 7 days of a pertussis-containing vaccine → use DT instead of DTaP
  • Live vaccines in the situations above

Not contraindications: mild illness, low fever, current antibiotics, family history of reactions, prematurity (vaccinate by chronological age), or breastfeeding.

🎓 The adolescent and the 18-year-old

🚨 The dose that gets missed is the one at 16. MenACWY is given at 11–12 and again at 16. An 18-year-old who had “their meningitis shot” at twelve and nothing since is not up to date, and that is exactly how the question is written.

🏫 Heading to college

  • MenACWY — the 16-year booster; many schools require it for dormitory living
  • MenB — 16–23, shared clinical decision-making. Not routine for everyone, and that wording is the answer
  • HPV catch-up through age 26 — starting now means the 3-dose schedule
  • Influenza every year, and Td/Tdap every 10 years from here on
  • Check MMR, varicella and hepatitis B series are actually complete

🔄 Catching up: never restart a series. However long the gap, you carry on from where it stopped — count the doses already given and give what is missing. Restarting is a wrong answer.

🩸 Tetanus after a wound — which product, by age

⭐ The answer is chosen by age, not by the wound. The cut-off is the 7th birthday — the same cut-off that separates DTaP from Td and Tdap in the routine schedule. Everything else in the question is a distractor built on that one line.

AgeFor wound prophylaxisWhy not the others
Under 7DT (or DTaP if the child is also due routine doses) Full-strength pediatric formulation. Tdap is not licensed under 10, and the adult Td is not the childhood product.
7 and olderTd — the adult tetanus booster DTaP stops at the 7th birthday. Tdap is for completing the pertussis series or as a one-time adolescent dose — not the wound-prophylaxis product.
18 and olderTd every 10 years from here on The 10-year booster clock is what an exam means by “up to date” in an adult.

✅ When TIG is added

Tetanus immune globulin is for a dirty or deep wound in someone who is not up to date — fewer than three documented doses, or an unknown history. A child who is up to date does not need it, however deep the puncture.

TIG and the tetanus toxoid may be given at the same visit, but never mixed in one syringe. Separate syringes, and a different anatomical site.

🤔 The 7-to-10 gap

A child aged 7 to 10 who never finished the DTaP series gets Tdap to catch up on pertussis — that is a catch-up decision, not a wound decision. For the wound itself, the 7-and-older answer is still Td.

🩹 The wound itself — home care

The tetanus decision is only half of it. The other half is the wound, and a puncture is a worse wound than it looks: a small opening, a deep track, and bacteria carried in and then sealed under the skin.

  • 💧 Let it bleed briefly — that flushes the track — then wash with soap and running water for several minutes. Do not scrub; scrubbing adds tissue injury.
  • 🧹 Do not dig for something embedded. A retained fragment is a reason to be seen, not a reason to probe at home.
  • 🩹 Cover it, and check it daily.

A puncture through a shoe is its own problem. The sole and the foam lining carry Pseudomonas into the foot, and the classic complication is osteomyelitis appearing 1–4 weeks later — often after the skin looked healed. Foot pain and swelling weeks after a nail through a sneaker is that, until proven otherwise.

Seek care for: spreading redness or warmth after 2–3 days, pus, fever, a wound that will not close, anything retained, a bite, a dirty or deep wound in a child who is not up to date on tetanus — and any puncture through footwear.

🩺 Giving them well

✅ Technique

  • Vastus lateralis for infants — the largest muscle they have
  • Deltoid once walking well and the muscle is developed (usually 3+ years)
  • Give the most painful injection last
  • Different sites for different vaccines; document site, lot number and expiry
  • Comfort: swaddle, breastfeed or give sucrose to infants; hold upright afterwards

🏠 Aftercare teaching

  • Expected: soreness, redness, mild swelling, low-grade fever, fussiness for a day or two
  • Acetaminophen or ibuprofen for comfort — not routinely before, as it may blunt the response
  • Cool compress to the site; move the limb normally
  • MMR and varicella reactions come late — a rash or fever 7–14 days afterwards is normal

Report a fever over 40C, a seizure, persistent inconsolable crying over 3 hours, or any breathing difficulty.

💬 Talking with a hesitant parent

Listen first, do not lecture. Ask what specifically worries them and answer that.

  • Acknowledge the concern as reasonable rather than dismissing it
  • Give concrete local information — what these diseases actually do
  • Hib vaccine all but eliminated epiglottitis; that is a vivid, true example
  • Document the discussion and any refusal; keep the door open for next visit

🎯 NCLEX traps

  • Live = MMR, varicella, rotavirus, nasal flu — not for immunocompromised or pregnant
  • Mild illness is not a reason to postpone
  • MMR/varicella not before 12 months
  • Vastus lateralis in infants
  • MMR reaction appears 7–14 days later
  • After IVIG, delay live vaccines about 11 months

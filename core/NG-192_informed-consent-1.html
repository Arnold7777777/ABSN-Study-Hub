Informed Consent ✍️

Voluntary · Informed · Competent — and the one distinction NCLEX tests every single time

NG-192 NURSING CORE Series: Legal & Ethics 3 of 5 ADHD-friendly visual edition

Informed consent is required before any invasive procedure or surgery. It's the paperwork proof of autonomy (page 1) — and skipping it is legally battery (page 2). The single most-tested fact on this whole topic: the provider obtains consent — the nurse witnesses the signature and confirms the client actually understands.

VALID CONSENT — 3 REQUIREMENTS

STEP 1 · WHAT MAKES IT VALID

Miss any one of these three and the "signed" consent isn't legally valid.

✅ Voluntary + Informed + Competent = valid consent

🧠 "VIC gives consent." V(oluntary) — I(nformed) — C(ompetent). No VIC, no valid signature.

🕊️ Voluntary

Given freely, without coercion, pressure, or undue influence — from staff, family, or anyone else.

🧠 A client who signs only because a family member is standing over them insisting has not given truly voluntary consent.

📋 Informed

The provider must disclose: the diagnosis, the nature and purpose of the treatment, the risks and benefits, and the alternatives — including the alternative of no treatment at all.

🧠 If any of these pieces is missing, the consent is not truly "informed" — even with a signature on the form.

🧠 Competent (capacity)

The client must be a legal adult (or emancipated minor), alert and oriented, and not impaired by sedating medications, illicit substances, or acute intoxication at the time of signing.

🧠 Pre-op sedation is the classic trap: once meds are given, that client can no longer legally sign — consent must be obtained before sedation.
👥

WHO CAN — AND CAN'T — CONSENT

STEP 2 · IDENTIFY THE RIGHT SIGNER

When the client can't sign for themselves, the law has a specific order of who steps in.

❌ Who generally cannot consent for themselves

  • 🧒 Minors — unless legally emancipated (state-dependent)
  • 😵 Clients who are unconscious or have severely impaired cognition
  • 💉 Clients who are sedated or acutely intoxicated
  • ⚖️ Clients under a court-appointed guardianship for decision-making
🧠 In these cases, consent shifts to a legal guardian, healthcare proxy/durable power of attorney for healthcare, or — in a true emergency — the emergency-treatment doctrine.

🚨 Involuntary psychiatric admission ≠ loss of consent rights

Clients admitted involuntarily still have the right to informed consent for their general medical treatment. An involuntary hold addresses safety — it does not strip away every other legal right.

🧠 Classic ATI trap: students assume "involuntary" means "no rights." Wrong — see page 5 (AMA & Mandatory Reporting) for the full right-to-refuse discussion.

🌳 Decision tree: client can't sign — now what?

🧠 Telephone consent for an emergent surgery must be witnessed by 2 authorized individuals (commonly two staff on separate extensions/lines) — a single witness is not enough.

THE NURSE'S ROLE

STEP 3 · THE #1 NCLEX DISTINCTION

The provider explains and obtains; the nurse verifies and witnesses. Confusing these two is the most common wrong answer.

🚫 The provider obtains consent — the nurse is NOT the one explaining risks/benefits

🧠 "Provider persuades, nurse notices." If a question asks who should explain surgical risks, the answer is almost never "the nurse" — it's the surgeon/provider performing the procedure.

🔄 Client can withdraw consent at any time

Consent isn't a one-way door. If the client expresses doubt, asks a new question, or says they've changed their mind — even minutes before the procedure — the nurse must stop, notify the provider, and hold the procedure.

🧠 A signed form does not lock a client in. "I don't want this anymore" always overrides a signature.

🏥 Ethics committee & ANA

An ethics committee is a healthcare team that provides guidance during an ethical dilemma — it gives recommendations, and does NOT impose a decision on the care team or family.

The ANA (American Nurses Association) sets the ethical standards for the whole nursing profession — first covered on page 1 of this series.

🧠 If a stem asks "who makes the final call in an ethical dilemma," it's never the ethics committee — they advise, the care team/family/client still decide.

NCLEX TRAPS

STEP 4 · DON'T GET FOOLED

Real exam-style stems, straight from the source material.

🚨 SATA trap: intentional torts

"A nurse applies restraints without a prescription on a client who threatens to leave AMA. Select all that apply — which are intentional torts?"

Correct: Battery, False imprisonment. Not correct: Malpractice and Negligence are unintentional torts, and HIPAA is a federal privacy law, not itself an intentional tort category.

🧠 Sort by the word first — "intentional" rules out negligence and malpractice immediately.

🚨 Break-room gossip scenario

"Two nurses overhear a coworker say she believes a married HCP is in a relationship with a client."

This is slander — a spoken, harmful (and unverified) statement about someone's reputation.

🧠 Say it out loud = slander. Write it down = libel.

⭐ Emergency surgery, can't sign

"Client needs emergency surgery but cannot sign the operative consent form due to sedation. Priority action?"

Obtain telephone consent from a family member, witnessed by 2 authorized individuals.

🧠 "Cannot sign" ≠ "skip consent." The team pivots to the next legally valid pathway — it doesn't disappear.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: Who is legally responsible for explaining the risks and benefits of a surgical procedure?
The provider/HCP performing the procedure — not the nurse. The nurse witnesses the signature and verifies understanding.
Q2: What are the 3 requirements for valid consent?
Voluntary, Informed, and Competent (capacity).
Q3: A client who was involuntarily admitted for psychiatric treatment needs an appendectomy. Do they still have the right to informed consent?
Yes — involuntary admission does not remove the right to informed consent for general medical treatment.
Q4: Emergency surgery is needed and the client cannot sign due to sedation, with no representative physically present. What's the priority action?
Obtain telephone consent from a family member, witnessed by 2 authorized individuals.

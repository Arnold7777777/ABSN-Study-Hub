PULSE POINTS

STEP 1 · FIND THE BEAT

Same seven sites you saw on NG-079 — here's the exact anatomy and the one you never press on both sides at once.

📍 The seven peripheral pulse points

🧠 "CBR-FPD-T" top to bottom: Carotid, Brachial, Radial, Femoral, Popliteal, Dorsalis pedis, Tibialis (posterior). Walk down the body and you hit every site in order.

⚠️ Caution: carotid pulse

Never palpate both carotid pulses at the same time — bilateral compression can slow the heart rate (vagal response) or reduce blood flow to the brain.

🧠 "One side of the neck at a time — always."

✅ Circulation checks — distal to the pulse

  • Capillary refill: press nail bed, should return <3 sec
  • Skin turgor: pinch skin, should return <3 sec
  • Temperature should be equal bilaterally in all extremities

LUNG AUSCULTATION

STEP 2 · WALK THE LADDER

A systematic zig-zag down the chest, comparing side to side at every stop — never straight down one side then the other.

🪜 The auscultation ladder — anterior & posterior

  1. Start top-right, above the clavicle — listen for a full inhale + exhale
  2. Move to the opposite side, same spot — compare quality, depth, extra sounds
  3. Move down one interspace at a time, comparing side-to-side every stop
  4. Continue through the 6th ICS at the midaxillary line (armpit) — this covers the lower lobes
  5. Flip to posterior: client leans forward, hands in lap to separate the scapulae for a clean listen
🧠 "HF = Heavy Fluid." The lung bases are the best place to catch crackles from fluid overload / heart failure. "A is for Armpit" — the 6th ICS midaxillary line is right at the armpit and picks up the lower lobes.

🫱 Tactile fremitus

Use the ulnar and palmar surfaces of your hands against the chest wall while the client says "ninety-nine" — vibration should feel equal bilaterally.

🧠 Increased fremitus = consolidation (e.g., pneumonia). Decreased = air/fluid blocking transmission (e.g., pneumothorax, effusion).

🗣️ Charting the subjective symptom: dyspnea

Correct client language for shortness of breath: "It's hard for me to breathe and I feel short winded all the time."

Dys-PNEA = Difficult breathing — a PRIORITY symptom.

🚨 Priority interventions — "breathing problems"

  • Ensure the airway is open
  • Monitor O2 saturation
  • Place client in High Fowler's
  • Evaluate effectiveness of respiratory effort after intervening
🧠 Most important objective data confirming hypoxia: ABGs (arterial blood gases) — not just the SpO2 number.

👀 Cyanosis assessment sites

Bluish discoloration from hypoxia is checked at:

  • Nailbeds — peripheral cyanosis
  • Conjunctiva — central/mucosal cyanosis, more reliable in darker skin tones
  • Earlobes
🧠 Also watch for: clubbing of the fingernail beds (chronic hypoxia), vertigo/dizziness, and increased pulse rate as signs of longer-term hypoxia.

HEART & ABDOMEN

STEP 3 · THE OTHER TWO STOPS

Five points for the heart, four quadrants for the belly — and one order you can't mix up.

🫀 Heart auscultation — 5 points, "All Pigs Eat Too Much"

🧠 "All Pigs Eat Too Much" — Aortic, Pulmonic, Erb's point, Tricuspid, Mitral, top to bottom down the left sternal border, ending at the apex (mitral/apical — the same spot you count the apical pulse from NG-079).

🎵 Heart sounds — S1, S2, S3, S4

  • S1 “lub” — the mitral and tricuspid (AV) valves closing at the start of systole. Loudest at the apex.
  • S2 “dub” — the aortic and pulmonic (semilunar) valves closing at the end of systole. Loudest at the base.
  • S3 — an extra sound just after S2, early diastole: blood rushing into a volume-overloaded ventricle. Heart failure or fluid overload in an older adult; can be normal in children, young adults and late pregnancy.
  • S4 — an extra sound just before S1, late diastole: the atria pushing blood into a stiff ventricle. Hypertension, MI, aortic stenosis.
🧠 S3 = “Ken-TUC-ky” (S1-S2-S3: the extra beat comes last) — think “sloshing in”, too much volume.
🧠 S4 = “TEN-nes-see” (S4-S1-S2: the extra beat comes first) — think “a stiff wall”.
🧠 “Lub” = S1 = Systole starts, so it lines up with the carotid pulse — feel the pulse while you listen to tell S1 from S2.

🩻 Bowel sounds — four quadrants

Listen for sounds in all four quadrants. If absent, listen for a full 5 minutes before charting "absent."

🧠 Chart as active, hyperactive, or hypoactive — never just "present."

🚨 The order you can't mix up: IAPP

1️⃣ Inspect
▼
2️⃣ Auscultate
▼
3️⃣ Percuss
▼
4️⃣ Palpate

The abdomen is the ONE exception to the usual inspect→palpate→percuss→auscultate order — palpating or percussing first can artificially stimulate bowel sounds and give a false reading.

🧠 "Look, Listen, THEN Touch" for the belly — auscultate before you ever press on it.

🫃 Normal abdominal shape

Belly should be flat, non-pulsating, and even/symmetric. Ask about pain with palpation — a rigid, board-like abdomen with fever and tachycardia is peritonitis until proven otherwise and is a priority to report.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: Why should you never palpate both carotid pulses at once?
Bilateral pressure can slow the heart rate via a vagal response or reduce cerebral blood flow.
Q2: Where is the best place to auscultate for fluid overload in the lungs?
The posterior lung bases — listen for crackles ("HF = Heavy Fluid").
Q3: What's the correct order of abdominal assessment, and why is it different from other systems?
Inspect, Auscultate, Percuss, Palpate — auscultate before touching so you don't artificially stimulate bowel sounds.
Q4: List the five heart auscultation points in order.
Aortic (2nd R ICS) → Pulmonic (2nd L ICS) → Erb's point (3rd L ICS) → Tricuspid (4th L ICS) → Mitral (5th L ICS, midclavicular) — "All Pigs Eat Too Much."

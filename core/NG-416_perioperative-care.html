🧵 Perioperative Care

Before, during and after a procedure. Most of the exam points live in the before and the waking up — the checklist that has to be complete, and the airway that has to come back.

⭐ The one idea

Every perioperative question is really asking “what is the nurse responsible for in this phase?”

1 · Preoperative Verify. Consent, identity, allergies, NPO, baseline vitals, teaching. This is the phase with the checklist, and the phase most questions come from.
2 · Intraoperative Protect. Positioning, sterile field, counts, normothermia. The client cannot advocate for themselves, so the nurse does.
3 · Postoperative Watch. Airway first, then bleeding, then pain. Complications announce themselves in the vital signs before anyone feels unwell.

📋 Before the procedure: the checklist

  • Signed consent on the chart — and the provider obtains it. The nurse witnesses the signature and confirms she understands; the nurse does not explain the procedure.
  • Allergies, and say the awkward ones out loud: iodine, contrast, shellfish, latex.
  • Baseline vital signs, recorded. Without a baseline nothing afterwards means anything.
  • How long she has actually been NPO — not whether she was told to be.
  • Site marked, jewellery, dentures and prosthetics removed, bladder emptied.

The identification band stays on. It is not in the way — it is the last check before anything irreversible happens.

⚠️ NPO means nothing by mouth. Not a light breakfast, not a sip of coffee. “I can have a light breakfast” is the answer that shows teaching has not landed.

💬 What teaching has to cover

Four things, and a client who can say them back has understood:

  • Nothing to eat or drink after midnight
  • Moderate sedation, not general anaesthesia, for most scopes — drowsy and comfortable, not unconscious
  • Someone else drives her home. No driving for 24 hours after sedation
  • After a bronchoscopy or an EGD, nothing by mouth until the gag reflex returns — the throat was numbed, and swallowing is not safe until it wears off

🛍️ PACU: waking up

Airway first, every time. Then the numbers, then comfort.

  • Vital signs every 5 to 15 minutes until stable
  • NPO until the gag reflex returns
  • Warm blankets — theatres are cold and anaesthesia blunts thermoregulation, so hypothermia is expected and shivering raises oxygen demand
  • Position to protect the airway; suction within reach
  • Pain treated on a scale, not on stoicism

She does not eat, drink or walk the hallway in PACU. Clear liquids and ambulation belong to the unit, after she has met discharge criteria.

📊 The Aldrete score

Five parameters, each scored 0, 1 or 2 — out of 10. It answers one question: is she recovered enough to leave PACU? Most units want 9 or better, or back to her pre-procedure baseline.

🥋
Activity

Moves four extremities, two, or none — voluntarily or on command.

🫁
Respiration

Breathes deeply and coughs freely, or is dyspneic and shallow, or apneic.

🧡
Circulation

Blood pressure within 20% of pre-anaesthetic level, or 20–50% off, or further.

🧠
Consciousness

Fully awake, rousable on calling, or not responding.

🟨
Oxygen saturation

Above 92% on room air, needs oxygen to hold 90%, or below 90% even with oxygen.

Bowel sounds are not on the Aldrete score. They matter later, on the unit. The score is about waking up safely: moving, breathing, perfusing, conscious, oxygenating.

💊 Get ahead of the pain

Preemptive analgesia means giving the dose before the thing that hurts, not after she reports pain.

Physiotherapy at 10:00 and a PRN oral opioid available → give it at about 09:15 to 09:30. An oral opioid needs 30 to 45 minutes to work, so a dose given when she starts hurting arrives after the session is already ruined.

Waiting for her to report pain, shortening the session, or holding the opioid “so she can participate” all cost her the therapy. Pain is what stops her moving; treating it is what lets the knee bend.

🩸 After a bowel or bariatric operation

An anastomosis is a surgical join between two tubular structures — two ends of bowel, a vessel to a vessel, a duct to a duct. Not an opening onto the abdominal wall — that is a stoma.

Where there is a join, there can be a leak, and a leak spills bowel contents into the abdomen.

🚨 Unexplained tachycardia is the earliest sign of an anastomotic leak — often the only sign, and it shows up before the fever, before the rigid abdomen, before the pain gets bad.

Day 2 after a sleeve gastrectomy, a heart rate of 128 with only mild pain is not expected. Do not document it as normal, medicate it, or walk it off as gas pain — each of those wastes the hours in which a leak is still fixable. Call the provider.

⭐ High yield

  • The provider obtains consent; the nurse witnesses it
  • The ID band stays on
  • Ask about iodine, contrast, shellfish and latex by name
  • Check how long she has actually been NPO
  • PACU: airway first, vitals q5–15 min, warm blankets, NPO until the gag reflex returns
  • Aldrete = activity, respiration, circulation, consciousness, oxygen saturation — not bowel sounds. Out of 10, usually 9 to discharge
  • Preemptive analgesia: 30 to 45 minutes before therapy
  • Anastomosis = a join; stoma = an opening
  • Unexplained tachycardia after GI surgery = anastomotic leak until proven otherwise
  • No driving for 24 hours after sedation
Sources. Written from AORN perioperative practice guidance, ASPAN perianesthesia standards and MedlinePlus: Surgery. The Aldrete score is the modified (oxygen-saturation) version in standard PACU use.

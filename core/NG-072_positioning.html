Positioning 🛏️

Therapeutic Patient Positions & Their Indications

NG-072 Nursing Core ADHD-friendly visual edition

Every position is a tool, not a default. The nurse picks a position to ensure comfort and safety while preventing complications related to the client's condition, treatment, or procedure. Pick the wrong one and you can drop a blood pressure, aspirate a patient, or blow a fresh surgical line.

⬇️ TrendelenburgHead down, feet up — for shock / hypotension & central line work.
⬆️ Semi/High-Fowler'sHead up 30–90° — for SOB, cardiac issues, NG tube feeding.
🤰 Sims'Semi-prone, top knee to chest — for enemas & perineal exams.
🚨 Aspiration riskNever feed a patient flat — HOB up 30–45° during & after meals/tube feeds.

WHY POSITION

STEP 1 · THE PURPOSE

Positioning is never random — every position serves comfort, safety, a procedure, or a physiologic goal.

🎯 Purpose of therapeutic positioning

To ensure client comfort and safety while preventing complications related to the client's condition, treatment, or procedure.

  • 🫁 Respiratory support — upright positions ease breathing & maximize lung expansion.
  • ❤️ Hemodynamic support — head-down positions increase venous return in shock.
  • 🩹 Post-procedure protection — the right position protects a fresh surgical site or line.
  • 🛡️ Skin integrity — repositioning at least every 2 hours prevents pressure injury.
  • 🚽 Access for a procedure — enemas, pelvic exams, and childbirth all need a specific position.
🧠 Ask "why this position?" every time. If you can't name the reason, you don't know the position yet — Fowler's isn't "just how patients sit," it's the answer to SOB, cardiac load, and aspiration risk.

🦴 Body alignment matters in every position

Whatever position you choose, keep the spine neutral, support joints in slight flexion, and use pillows/wedges to prevent foot drop, shoulder subluxation, and skin shear.

🧠 Alignment first, position second. A "correct" position with a dangling foot or twisted neck is still a bad position.

⚠️ Reposition schedule & pressure injury

Bedbound clients: reposition at least every 2 hours. Chair-bound: shift weight every 15 minutes, full reposition every 1 hour.

🧠 "2 in bed, 1 in the chair." Bed = every 2 hours; chair = every 1 hour (with small shifts every 15 min).

THE POSITIONS

STEP 2 · KNOW EACH ONE

Seven named positions — picture each one, memorize the one-line indication.

🧎 Supine — "on your spine"

Indication: Abdominal, facial & extremity surgeries; the default "at rest" position.

🧠 Supine = spine. You're flat on your spine, face up.

🙃 Prone — "on your tummy"

Indication: Drainage of the mouth after oral/neck surgery; allows full extension of hip & knee joints.

🧠 Prone = face-down on the ground. Good for drainage — gravity pulls secretions out of the mouth.

↔️ Lateral (side-lying)

Indication: GI issues, rectal surgery, and pressure-injury prevention (alternate sides).

🧠 Right lateral = right side down. Name the side that's touching the mattress.

🤰 Sims' — semi-prone

Indication: Administering enemas, perineal exams, and comfort in pregnancy.

🧠 Sims' = "S" for enema. Upper knee climbs toward the chest, upper arm flexes at the elbow.

🪑 Fowler's family — head & trunk up 30–90°

Indication: Cardiac issues, shortness of breath (SOB), or NG tube feeding — gravity keeps the diaphragm free & secretions out of the airway.

🧠 Low → high: Semi-Fowler's 30–40° → Fowler's 45–60° → High-Fowler's 90°. Higher angle = more respiratory/cardiac relief.

⬇️ Trendelenburg — "upside down"

Indication: Hypotension/shock (increases venous return), GYN & abdominal hernia surgery, central line placement/removal, and treatment of venous air embolism (traps air away from the pulmonary outflow tract).

🧠 Trendelenburg = treat the pressure that's TOO LOW. Feet up sends blood back to the heart and brain.

⬆️ Reverse Trendelenburg

Indication: Promotes perfusion during surgery in obese patients; helps prevent aspiration.

🧠 Reverse = the opposite problem. Same tilted-bed idea as Trendelenburg, flipped — head high instead of head low.

🦵 Lithotomy

Indication: Gynecological procedures & childbirth — most commonly seen in OB.

🧠 Lithotomy = "L" for labor and delivery. Stirrups + legs up = the classic OB/GYN table position.

🚨 Position ↔ Indication mismatch = NCLEX trap

NURSING CARE

STEP 3 · SAFE POSITIONING

Assess before you move, support what you place, and reassess after.

✅ Before repositioning — CHECK

  • Assess hemodynamic stability (a hypotensive patient can drop further with rapid position changes — reposition slowly).
  • Assess lines, drains, and dressings before and after the move.
  • Assess skin at every position change for redness/breakdown.
  • Check for orders/contraindications (spinal precautions, recent surgery, ICP).
🧠 CHECK before you ACT. Robo's study map: Step → Check → Act — assess first, every time.

⭐ Supportive devices for every position

  • 🛏️ Pillows/wedges under joints to maintain neutral alignment.
  • 🦶 Footboards or high-top sneakers to prevent foot drop.
  • 🌀 Trochanter rolls to prevent hip external rotation (supine).
  • 🎈 Pressure-redistribution mattress for high pressure-injury risk.
🧠 Support the gaps. Anywhere there's an empty space between the body and the bed, put a pillow there.

🚨 The one you must never forget

Never leave a patient flat during or right after a tube feeding or oral meal — keep HOB elevated 30–45° for at least 30–60 minutes after to prevent aspiration.

🧠 "Flat feeds fail." Flat + feeding tube = aspiration pneumonia risk.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: A hypotensive post-op patient needs a BP boost fast — which position?
Trendelenburg — head down, feet up increases venous return to the heart.
Q2: A patient with SOB and a new NG tube feeding — which position and why?
Semi- or high-Fowler's (30–90°) — eases breathing and prevents aspiration during the feed.
Q3: What's the difference between lateral and Sims' position?
Lateral is simple side-lying; Sims' is semi-prone with the upper leg flexed toward the chest and upper arm flexed — used for enemas and perineal exams.
Q4: How often must a bedbound patient be repositioned to prevent pressure injury?
At least every 2 hours in bed; chair-bound patients need a weight shift every 15 minutes and full reposition every 1 hour.

Tort Law ⚖️

Negligence, malpractice, and the intentional torts that show up on every NCLEX legal question

NG-172 NURSING CORE Series: Legal & Ethics 2 of 5 ADHD-friendly visual edition

A tort is a wrongful act or violation of someone's rights that creates legal liability. Nursing torts split into two families: unintentional (negligence, malpractice — you didn't mean to cause harm, but you did) and intentional (assault, battery, false imprisonment, defamation — willful acts that violate a client's rights). The burden of proof always sits with the accuser, not the nurse being accused.

THE BASICS

STEP 1 · DEFINE THE TERM

One umbrella word — tort — covers everything from an honest mistake to a deliberate violation.

⚖️ Tort = a wrongful act that creates legal liability

🧠 "Innocent until proven, and it's not your job to prove it." Whoever brings the claim (the accuser) has to prove the nurse was liable — the nurse doesn't have to prove innocence.

🏛️ Civil vs. Criminal

CivilCriminal
Crime against an individualCrime against the state
Most nursing torts land hereMore serious — can involve licensure loss, jail
🧠 Most malpractice suits are civil — but abuse, patient abandonment with harm, or falsifying records can escalate to criminal.

📏 The "reasonable, prudent nurse" test

Used to determine the standard of care in every negligence/malpractice case: "What would a reasonable, prudent nurse — with similar training and experience, in a similar setting — have done in this same situation?"

🧠 It's not "what would a perfect nurse do" — it's what a reasonably careful peer would do. This test comes back on the Informed Consent page too.
⚖️

NEGLIGENCE & MALPRACTICE

STEP 2 · UNINTENTIONAL TORTS

Same family, different license — malpractice is negligence committed by a licensed professional.

🪜 The 4 elements of negligence — ALL FOUR must be present

Example: A nurse doesn't wash hands before inserting a Foley catheter (breach — a duty existed) and the client develops a catheter-associated UTI (causation + damages). All four elements present = negligence.

🧠 "DBCD — Don't Break the Client's Duty." Duty → Breach → Causation → Damages. Miss any one link and there's no negligence claim, no matter how careless the nurse was.

⚠️ Negligence — the definition

The failure to provide adequate care that another reasonable, prudent nurse would provide in a similar situation.

Example: A nurse identifies an absent peripheral pulse in a client's casted extremity in the early morning — but doesn't report it to the HCP until the early afternoon. The delay is the breach.

🧠 Withholding informed consent can itself be negligence — if a provider doesn't obtain proper consent, that provider can be found negligent.

🩺 Malpractice — negligence with a license attached

Illegal, improper, or negligent actions by a licensed professional — practice that falls below the accepted standard of care.

Example: An RN fails to check the dosage printed on a medication vial, and the client is harmed as a result.

🧠 Malpractice = negligence + a license. Every malpractice case is negligence, but not every negligence case is malpractice — malpractice specifically requires the person to be a licensed professional acting within their scope.

🚪 Abandonment — a special unintentional tort

Desertion of a client by anyone who has already assumed responsibility for their care — for example, leaving an assignment or ending a nurse-client relationship without properly transferring care or giving adequate notice.

🧠 Once a nurse accepts an assignment (duty exists), walking away without a proper hand-off is abandonment — even if the intent was reasonable (e.g., leaving because the assignment felt unsafe). Always notify the charge nurse/supervisor first.

INTENTIONAL TORTS

STEP 3 · WILLFUL ACTS

These are deliberate — no accident excuse. Assault and battery are the two NCLEX loves most.

🆚 Assault (the threat) vs. Battery (the act) — A comes before B

🧠 "A & B, in order — Assault before Battery." The threat (A) doesn't require contact; the act (B) does. Both can happen in one scenario: threatening an NG tube (assault) then inserting it anyway over refusal (battery).

🔒 False imprisonment

Using physical restraints, seclusion, or a chemical restraint to keep someone against their will — without a valid order/indication.

Example: Using restraints on a competent client just to prevent them from leaving the facility. Chemically restraining a competent client able to make their own decisions is both false imprisonment and assault/battery.

🧠 A restraint used for the wrong reason (convenience, punishment, "they might leave AMA") is false imprisonment — see page 5 for the correct AMA process.

📚 Defamation of character — Libel vs. Slander

Making false, rude, or insulting remarks that harm a client's (or coworker's) reputation.

LibelSlander
WrittenSpoken
🧠 "LIBEL lives in the LIBRARY" — both start with L, and a library holds written words. Example: break-room gossip about a coworker's alleged affair with a client = slander (spoken).

🕵️ Invasion of privacy

Violating a client's confidentiality rights — including HIPAA violations.

Example: Discussing a client's diagnosis where others can overhear, or accessing a chart with no professional reason to.

🧠 Confidentiality violations sit at the intersection of tort law and HIPAA — same act, two different legal exposures.

🚨 Highest-yield fact: treatment without consent = battery

This single fact links Tort Law directly to the next page in this series. Any invasive procedure, medication, or treatment given without valid consent — even if medically appropriate — is legally battery.

🧠 See NG-192 · Informed Consent for exactly how consent is obtained, witnessed, and withdrawn.

⭐ Highest-yield fact: burden of proof

In any tort claim, the accuser must prove all required elements. A nurse is not required to prove they did nothing wrong — the plaintiff must prove the nurse did.

🧠 This is why thorough, honest documentation matters so much — it's the nurse's best defense if an accusation is ever made.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: A nurse skips hand hygiene before a Foley insertion and the client develops a UTI. Which 2 elements of negligence does this satisfy at minimum?
Breach (skipped standard technique) and causation/damages (the UTI resulted) — plus duty, since a nurse-client relationship existed. All four elements are present.
Q2: A nurse says "I'll restrain you if you get up again" but never actually restrains the client. What tort is this?
Assault — the threat of harm, without any actual contact.
Q3: A competent client is placed in restraints solely to stop them from leaving the unit. What tort(s)?
False imprisonment (and potentially battery/assault depending on how it was carried out).
Q4: What's the difference between negligence and malpractice?
Malpractice is negligence committed specifically by a licensed professional acting below the standard of care — negligence is the broader, general term.

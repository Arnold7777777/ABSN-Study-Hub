Ethical Key Terms 🧭

The 6 ethical principles every legal/ethics NCLEX question is built on

NG-133 NURSING CORE Series: Legal & Ethics 1 of 5 ADHD-friendly visual edition

Every ethics/legal NCLEX question — informed consent, restraints, refusing treatment, AMA discharge, advance directives — is secretly testing one of six words: autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice, fidelity, and veracity. Learn these six cold first — the rest of this series (Tort Law, Informed Consent, Advance Directives, AMA & Mandatory Reporting) is just these six words showing up in different clinical costumes.

🅰️ AutonomyCompetent client's right to choose — even if the nurse disagrees.
🤝 FidelityKeep the promise — follow through on what you said you'd do.
🗣️ VeracityTell the truth — report the error even with zero harm done.
⚖️ JusticeFair & equitable care for every client, every time.
📖

THE SIX TERMS

STEP 1 · KNOW THE WORDS

Six pillars hold up every ethical decision a nurse makes — memorize the word, the one-line gloss, and one real example each.

🏛️ The six pillars of nursing ethics

🧠 "A Big Nurse Just Feels Very (caring)." A(utonomy) — B(eneficence) — N(onmaleficence) — J(ustice) — F(idelity) — V(eracity). Say the sentence, tap one pillar per word.

🅰️ Autonomy — "Always in control"

The client's right to make their own healthcare decisions, as long as they are a competent adult who has been informed of the risks.

Example: Respecting a competent client's right to refuse a treatment — even a Jehovah's Witness refusing a life-saving blood transfusion, or a client signing out AMA.

🧠 NCLEX trap: the nurse's job is to educate on the risks, not to override the choice. Ignoring a competent refusal = a violation of autonomy (and can become battery — see Tort Law, page 2 of this series).

💙 Beneficence — "Benefit"

Doing and promoting good for the client — actively acting in their best interest, not just avoiding harm.

Example: Calling a client's family to tell them their loved one is "critically ill and we are caring for their needs" so the family isn't left in the dark.

🧠 Beneficence = doing the good thing. Nonmaleficence (next card) is its quieter twin — not doing the bad thing.

🚫 Nonmaleficence — "No mal-intent"

= "first, do no harm" To avoid causing harm to the client, intentionally or through carelessness.

Example: Double-checking a drug dosage with another nurse before giving a high-alert medication.

🧠 "Mal" = bad (think "malicious"). Non-mal-eficence = actively steering away from the bad outcome.

⚖️ Justice — "Fair"

Treating clients fairly and equally — resources, time, and care distributed equitably regardless of race, gender, religion, culture, insurance status, or ability to pay.

Example: Providing the same standard of care to every client on the unit, not just the ones who tip better or complain loudest.

🧠 Justice ≠ identical. True justice is equitable — the client who needs more gets more; the point is no one is shorted because of who they are.

🤝 Fidelity — "Faithful"

Staying loyal to the client and following through on your responsibilities and promises — even the small ones.

Example: Client reports 8/10 pain; the nurse states she will return with pain medication — and does.

🧠 Fidelity = "I do what I said I'd do." Every broken small promise ("I'll be right back") chips away at trust — and is a fidelity violation, even if nothing bad happens medically.

🗣️ Veracity — "Very honest"

To tell the truth — full, accurate information, even when it's uncomfortable.

Example: A medication error occurs with NO adverse effect on the client — the nurse still reports it, and still tells the client/family it happened.

🧠 Veracity is tested by the "no harm done" trap. If the stem says "no harm resulted," students often think "so don't bother reporting." Wrong — veracity says report it anyway.

🛡️ Advocacy — the bonus 7th term

Not always grouped with the classic 6, but shows up constantly on ethics questions: to protect the client's rights, health, and safety — speaking up on their behalf.

  • Example: Reporting a critical change in the client's condition to the HCP right away.
  • Example: Relaying a client's wish (e.g., about code status or a treatment refusal) to the care team when they cannot speak for themselves.
🧠 Advocacy is the action arm of autonomy + beneficence combined — you protect the client's right to choose (autonomy) and their wellbeing (beneficence) at the same time.

WHEN PRINCIPLES COLLIDE

STEP 3 · PRIORITIZE

The hardest ethics questions pit two good principles against each other — you have to know which one wins, and why.

🪢 The classic tug-of-war: Autonomy vs. Beneficence

If the client is competent, informed, and adult, their right to choose (even to refuse) outranks what the nurse or family thinks is "best." The nurse's job shifts to educate, document, and support — not override.

🧠 "Competent wins." The single fastest way to answer an autonomy-vs-beneficence question: ask "is this client competent?" If yes — autonomy wins almost every time.

🚧 When autonomy does NOT automatically win

  • 🧒 Client is a minor without emancipated status
  • 🧠 Client lacks decision-making capacity — unconscious, severely impaired, actively intoxicated
  • 🚨 True emergency, life/limb at risk, no surrogate reachable
  • ⚠️ Client is a danger to self or others (may trigger a legal hold — varies by state)
  • 📋 Mandatory reporting laws override confidentiality/autonomy (abuse, certain communicable diseases)
🧠 These five exceptions come back in full on page 4 (Advance Directives) and page 5 (AMA & Mandatory Reporting) of this series.

🔀 Nonmaleficence vs. Beneficence — the subtle one

Nonmaleficence (avoid harm) is usually the floor; beneficence (do good) is the ceiling. A nurse must always clear the floor, and should reach for the ceiling when possible — but never sacrifice "do no harm" chasing "do more good."

Example: Restraining an agitated client "for their own good" (beneficence reasoning) without meeting the legal criteria can cross into harm — false imprisonment (see Tort Law, next page).

🧠 "First, do no harm" outranks "do more good." If an intervention risks harm, that's a nonmaleficence red flag even if the intent was beneficent.

NCLEX TRAPS

STEP 4 · DON'T GET FOOLED

These six words are the connective tissue for the rest of this legal & ethics series — here's where they trip students up.

🚨 Trap: "the nurse personally disagrees"

If a competent client refuses treatment and the nurse personally disagrees with the choice, the correct action is still to respect autonomy — document the refusal, ensure the client is informed, and notify the HCP. Overriding it can become battery.

🧠 The nurse's opinion is not a legal override. Feelings ≠ authority.

⭐ Trap: "no harm was done, so why report it?"

A med error with zero adverse effect must still be reported — that's veracity, not overkill. Reporting also protects the next client and feeds facility safety data.

🧠 "No harm" changes the outcome, not the obligation to be honest.

✅ Foundation: the ANA Code of Ethics

The American Nurses Association (ANA) establishes the ethical standards for the entire nursing profession — every one of these six terms traces back to that Code.

🧠 If a question asks "who sets nursing's ethical standards," the answer is the ANA — not a hospital's individual policy.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: A client with 8/10 pain is told by the nurse "I'll be back with your medication," and the nurse returns as promised. Which principle?
Fidelity — following through on a promise/responsibility.
Q2: A medication error occurs but causes zero harm. Should the nurse report it?
Yes — veracity requires honesty regardless of outcome.
Q3: A competent adult refuses a blood transfusion the nurse believes is life-saving. What wins?
Autonomy — for a competent, informed adult, their choice outranks the nurse's or family's opinion of what's "best" (beneficence).
Q4: Who sets the ethical standards for the nursing profession?
The American Nurses Association (ANA), via its Code of Ethics for Nurses.

🌎 Community Health: Outbreaks, Surveillance & Herd Immunity

How a disease gets from one person to many, how public health notices and investigates it, and why vaccinating most people protects the few who cannot be vaccinated.

⭐ If you read nothing else

📈 Outbreak = more cases than expected, in one time, place and population.
👥 Direct spread = person to person. 🥤 Indirect = a shared vehicle (air, food, water, needles) or a vector (mosquito, tick).
🔍 Investigation starts by proving the outbreak is real — attack rate = new cases ÷ people at risk.
📡 Passive surveillance = reports that already come in. Active = going out to look for cases.
🛡️ Herd immunity protects newborns and immunocompromised people — measles needs about 94% immune.

📈 1 · What makes it an outbreak

An outbreak is a sudden increase in a disease in a specific time, place and population. Example: norovirus gastroenteritis spreading from one contaminated meal.

How an outbreak behaves depends on the germ — above all, on how it is transmitted. That is why the next section matters for everything after it.

🦠 2 · How it spreads

Transmission = a pathogen passing from an infected host to another person or group. Which route depends on the organism.

👥 Direct — person to person
💑
One to onee.g. a sexually transmitted infection through intercourse
🤧
One to manye.g. influenza from one violent sneeze in a crowded room
🥤 Indirect — through something in between
🚰
Common vehiclecontaminated air, water or food supply, shared needles
🦟
Vectora mosquito or a tick carries it

✅ Nursing tie-in: the route decides the precautions. Know the route, and you know what breaks the chain — see isolation precautions.

🔍 3 · Investigating an outbreak — 5 steps

1 · Prove it is realCount the cases (numerator) and the people at risk (denominator). Are there more cases than expected? Work out the attack rate.
2 · Look at time, person, placeWhen did people get sick, who are they, where were they — and do those overlap?
3 · Develop hypothesesUse what is already known about the disease plus what this investigation found.
4 · Test themAnalyze the data: a case-control study, or lab tests such as chemical or immunological fingerprinting.
5 · Control and preventRemove the source, set environmental controls to interrupt spread, improve sanitation, immunize susceptible people.

🧮 Attack rate — the share of a disease-free group that gets sick.

attack rate = new cases ÷ people at risk

It is a proportion because the sick people (numerator) are also counted in the whole group (denominator). Worked example: 30 sick ÷ 120 guests = 0.25 = 25%.

📡 4 · Disease surveillance

Surveillance is public health's early-warning system — systematic collection, analysis and interpretation of disease data to predict, spot and shrink outbreaks.

  • 📈 Changes in how often a disease occurs or kills
  • 🏭 Changes in risk factors in the environment
  • 💉 How complete vaccination coverage is
  • 💊 How common drug-resistant organisms are

📥 Passive — uses reports that already come in (reportable diseases such as anthrax, cholera, gonorrhea).

✅ Cheap and easy to set up; international comparisons quickly show where help is urgent.

❌ Reporting is not the reporter’s main job, so local outbreaks may be missed.

🚶 Active — runs its own program: field visits to clinics, hospitals, communities.

✅ More accurate (staff are there for surveillance); local outbreaks are more likely caught.

❌ More expensive to build and keep running.

📝 Where you fit: when a nurse or provider reports a reportable disease to the health department, that report is passive surveillance. A missed report is a gap in the system.

⚠️ Why data can be unreliable in low-income areas → under-reporting: places are hard to reach, communication with central authorities is difficult, and diagnostic labs are not always available.

🛡️ 5 · Vaccination & herd immunity

Herd immunity: when a critical percentage of a population is immune, the whole population is indirectly protected. Immunity can be innate, from vaccination, or from recovering from the infection.

📊 The threshold depends on how contagious the disease is. Measles is highly communicable, so about 94% must be immune.

Because of herd immunity, a vaccine program does not need 100% coverage — immunizing the critical percentage protects everyone.

👶 Who depends on it — people who cannot be vaccinated or cannot build immunity:

  • Newborn infants
  • People with HIV/AIDS
  • People with cancer or on cancer treatment

📋 Herd immunity works only when all four are true:

  • The host is a single species (humans)
  • The germ spreads by direct contact
  • No reservoir outside the human host
  • Infection leaves solid immunity

✅ Quick check — tap to reveal

Twelve people at a wedding of 60 get norovirus from the same dish. What is the attack rate?

12 ÷ 60 = 0.20 → 20%. New cases divided by everyone who was at risk.

Malaria carried by mosquitoes — direct or indirect?

Indirect, by a vector. Direct means person to person.

Which kind of surveillance is more likely to catch a small local outbreak?

Active — staff go looking for cases. Passive relies on routine reports, and local outbreaks can slip through.

Why does a 3-week-old infant depend on the people around them being vaccinated?

The infant is too young to be vaccinated, so the infant is protected only if enough people nearby are immune: herd immunity.

What is the first step of an outbreak investigation?

Prove the outbreak is real: count the cases and the population at risk and check that there are more cases than expected.

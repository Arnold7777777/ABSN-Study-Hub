NG-423

🦠 Ebola & the Filoviruses

A rare, often fatal viral hemorrhagic fever. In the community it spreads by direct contact with blood and body fluids; in the hospital your textbook puts viral hemorrhagic fevers under airborne + contact precautions. Nursing care is about isolation, perfect PPE, fluids and watching for bleeding and shock.

⭐ If you read nothing else

🦠 Filoviruses = Ebola and Marburg: enveloped RNA viruses that cause viral hemorrhagic fever.
🩻 Spread by direct contact with blood or body fluids of a sick or dead person (or infected animals, often fruit bats and apes) — and contaminated objects.
⏳ Incubation 2–21 days (usually 6–12). Not contagious until symptoms start.
💧 Fever and flu-like illness → massive watery diarrhea (up to 10 L) and vomiting → bleeding, DIC, shock and organ failure.
🛡️ Isolate with airborne + contact precautions (your textbook’s rule for viral hemorrhagic fevers), full PPE with a trained observer, aggressive fluid and electrolyte replacement, report to public health.

🦍

🧑 Human → human
🩸
Blood and body fluidsof someone who is ill — or who has died of it
🪴
Contaminated objectsneedles, linens, surfaces
💑
Sex with a survivorthe virus can stay in semen and other fluids for months after it leaves the blood

Highest risk: health-care workers without proper PPE or training, unsafe handling of bodies and waste, travel to an outbreak area, and contact with wild animals.

📈 2 · How the illness unfolds

Why it bleeds and leaks. Infected macrophages make tissue factor → the clotting cascade fires everywhere → DIC. A flood of cytokines dilates and leaks vessels → shock. The virus also kills off lymphocytes, so bacterial sepsis can follow.

🔴

🩸

🧠

⬇️

⏳

⚖️

Pregnancy: spontaneous abortion and vaginal bleeding; third-trimester infection is almost always fatal for mother and baby. Overall death rate 40% to 90%, depending on the species.

🔍 3 · Suspect it, then test

Think Ebola when fever comes with an exposure: travel to an outbreak area or contact with a sick person within the last 21 days.

RT-PCR on blood confirms it; the virus is usually detectable within 3 days of symptom onset. A rapid antigen test (ReEBOV) also exists. Isolate first and call infection prevention — don’t wait for the result.

🩺 4 · Treatment and nursing care

There is no cure — care is supportive. Two monoclonal-antibody drugs are now approved for the Zaire species, and a vaccine exists for outbreak responders, but the exam focuses on the nursing care below.

🧠 5 · Quick check

How does Ebola usually spread, and which precautions does your textbook want in the hospital?

It spreads by direct contact with blood, body fluids or contaminated objects. In the hospital, Brunner lists viral hemorrhagic fevers under airborne + contact precautions — use both.

A febrile client returned from an outbreak area 10 days ago. First action?

Isolate in a private negative-pressure room with airborne + contact precautions and notify infection prevention, then do the rest of the work-up.

Which electrolyte is high in Ebola, and why does it matter?

Potassium — watch the cardiac monitor. Sodium and calcium run low.

Why avoid IM injections?

DIC and low platelets make bleeding likely; every puncture is also an exposure risk for staff.

When is a person with Ebola contagious?

Once symptoms start — not during incubation. Bodies of people who died remain highly infectious.

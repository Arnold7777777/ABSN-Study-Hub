AMA & Mandatory Reporting 🚪

The right to refuse and leave — and the legal duty to speak up for someone who can't

LEAVING AMA

STEP 1 · THE RIGHT TO REFUSE

A competent client can walk out — the nurse's job is to inform, document, and never obstruct.

✅ Right to refuse — even if involuntarily admitted

Clients have the right to refuse medications or other therapies if they are competent to do so — even if they were involuntarily admitted (except in true emergency situations where there is imminent danger).

Example: a client who is Alert & Oriented x4 and a Jehovah's Witness refusing blood products — even if their partner (who is not a Jehovah's Witness) wants them to receive it. The nurse follows the competent client's wishes, not the family's.

🧠 This is autonomy in its purest form — the family's opinion does not override a competent adult's own choice.

🪜 The AMA discharge process — 6 steps

🧠 "INDS-SR" won't roll off the tongue, so picture it as a checklist you read left to right: inform → notify → no security → discuss → sign → remove lines. Step 3 is the single most-tested step — calling security to physically stop a competent client can itself become false imprisonment.

⭐ Assessing the risk before they walk out

  • Was the client properly educated on why staying matters?
  • Are they competent to make this decision right now?
  • Do they have a legal hold in place?
🧠 Three quick questions, asked in order, decide the whole scenario.

🔄 If the client refuses to sign the AMA form

The nurse still documents the refusal to sign — a client cannot be forced to sign anything, but the discharge (and the fact that risks were explained) is still documented in the chart.

🧠 Refusing to sign the AMA form does not equal being forced to stay.
🔒

WHO CANNOT LEAVE AMA

STEP 2 · CAPACITY CHECK

The right to leave only applies to someone who can actually understand the choice they're making.

❌ Cannot safely/legally sign out AMA

  • 🍺 Acutely intoxicated — e.g., drinking heavily for 12 hours and unable to walk safely
  • 🗣️ Actively psychotic with command hallucinations to harm self or others (e.g., voices telling them to hurt someone)
  • 👑 Severe impairment of reality testing that removes capacity to understand the decision (e.g., a client who believes they are a historical figure)
  • ⚖️ Client is under an active legal/involuntary hold for danger to self or others (specific hold names/timeframes vary by state)
🧠 The common thread isn't the diagnosis — it's whether the client can currently understand and appreciate the risk of leaving. Compare all three named in the source scenario: intoxicated-and-can't-walk, command hallucinations to harm a child, and believing she's Marie Antoinette — each removes the "competent" checkbox.

🏥 Voluntary psychiatric admission, wants to leave

A client admitted voluntarily for an anxiety disorder demands release. Initial nursing action: notify the client's primary HCP — the client generally retains the right to leave unless they meet criteria for an emergency hold.

🧠 Voluntary admission ≠ locked in. The HCP evaluates whether a hold is legally justified; the nurse doesn't unilaterally block the door.

🗣️ Client refuses a medication

"You don't have to take the medication if you don't want to" is the most therapeutic nursing response to a competent client's refusal.

🧠 Respecting the refusal — not arguing, bargaining, or threatening — is the correct action.

MANDATORY REPORTING

STEP 3 · PROTECT THE VULNERABLE

When someone can't advocate for themselves, the law requires the nurse to.

🛡️ 4-step response to suspected abuse

🧠 "PICS" — Protect · Interview (separately) · Collect (evidence) · Safety plan. Do them in that order — safety always comes before evidence-gathering.

⚠️ Report only after a full assessment

Suspected abuse is reported only after a detailed assessment and full history — not on a hunch alone. Once that threshold of reasonable suspicion is met, reporting is mandatory, not optional.

🧠 "Report only after assessment" ≠ "wait and see." A completed assessment that raises suspicion still legally obligates the report.

📋 What's typically mandated (varies by state/facility)

  • 🧒 Suspected child abuse or neglect
  • 👴 Suspected elder or dependent-adult abuse/neglect
  • 🦠 Certain communicable diseases to public health authorities
  • 🔫 Gunshot or stab wounds, in jurisdictions that require it
🧠 Exact reportable conditions and timeframes are set by state law and facility policy — always verify locally rather than assuming one national rule.

NCLEX TRAPS

STEP 4 · DON'T GET FOOLED

This closes out the whole 5-page series — every trap here reaches back to an earlier page.

🚨 Trap: "Call security to stop them"

Calling security to physically prevent a competent client from leaving is never the correct AMA response — it risks false imprisonment (see page 2, Tort Law).

🧠 The nurse's tools here are words and documentation, not force.

⭐ Trap: family overrides a competent client

A family member's wishes do not override a competent client's own informed refusal — this is autonomy (page 1) at its strongest test.

🧠 If the stem pits family against a competent client, the client's voice wins.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: A competent client wants to leave AMA and staff are worried. What should the nurse NOT do?
Call security to physically stop them — this risks false imprisonment. Instead: inform of risks, notify the provider, discuss risks, get the AMA form signed (or document refusal), and remove lines/tubes.
Q2: An intoxicated client who cannot walk wants to leave. Can they sign out AMA?
No — acute intoxication that impairs capacity means they cannot legally/safely be allowed to sign out AMA at that time.
Q3: What is the first step when abuse is suspected?
Protect the client from immediate harm, then interview them separately from the suspected abuser, collect/prepare evidence, and provide a safety plan with shelter resources.
Q4: A Jehovah's Witness client refuses blood products; their partner (not a Jehovah's Witness) wants them to receive it. Whose wishes does the nurse follow?
The competent client's own wishes — autonomy overrides a family member's differing opinion.

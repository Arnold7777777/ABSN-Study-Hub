🌡️ Temperature

~98.6°F / 37°C is the commonly-cited average — real "normal" is a range, roughly 97.8–99.1°F, and it shifts with time of day, age, and site measured.

  • Febrile = has a fever
  • Afebrile = no fever
  • Sites, most → least invasive-accurate: rectal ≈ temporal > oral > axillary
🧠 Cross-ref: hypothermia is core temp <95°F (35°C) — full pathophysiology on NG-041 Hypothermia.

💓 Pulse

60–100 bpm at rest. Always note rate, rhythm, AND quality — three data points, one vital sign.

  • 0 = absent
  • 1+ = weak / thready
  • 2+ = normal
  • 3+ = increased / full
  • 4+ = bounding
🧠 Irregular rhythm? Count the apical pulse for a full 60 seconds — never a 15-second count ×4.

🫁 Respirations

12–20 breaths/min, counted for a full minute without the client knowing (they'll change their pattern if they notice you counting).

  • Eupnea = normal, unlabored
  • Tachypnea = fast, >20
  • Bradypnea = slow, <12
  • Apnea = absent breathing
🧠 "Count the chest, not the story." Keep your fingers on the pulse site while you covertly watch the chest rise — the client relaxes their breathing when they think you're still counting pulse.

🩸 Blood pressure — systole vs diastole

Systole = max contraction of the left ventricle (top number). Diastole = pressure of the resting ventricles (bottom number).

🧠 "Systole = Squeeze." Both start with S — the squeeze makes the top number.

🌬️ SpO2 — pulse oximetry & hypoxia levels

95–100% is the typical adult reference range. Below that, hypoxia is commonly taught in tiers:

🧠 "85 = call for backup." Under 85% is severe hypoxia — that's an emergency escalation, not a "recheck in an hour."

😖 Pain — the 5th (or 6th) vital sign

Pain is assessed at every vitals check using a 0–10 numeric scale (or a validated behavioral/faces scale for nonverbal clients).

  • It's subjective — the client's report is the data
  • Ask location, quality, timing, aggravating/relieving factors
  • Reassess after every intervention
🧠 "Pain is what the patient says it is." Never substitute your own judgment of how much pain "looks right."

💓 Pulse quality + the pattern trap

A pulse can be the "right number" and still be abnormal — rate is only 1 of 3 things you must chart: rate, rhythm, quality.

🧠 "Number's fine, pattern's not." A rate of 88 with a thready, irregular, weak quality is NOT a normal finding — always chart all three.

PRIORITY ACTIONS

STEP 3 · ACT ON IT

Vital signs exist to trigger an action — know what each red-flag pattern means you do next.

🚨 Airway → Breathing → Circulation — the universal priority order

1
🫁 Airway — is it open/patent? Position, suction, anticipate advanced airway if needed
2
💨 Breathing — rate, effort, SpO2, adventitious sounds
3
🫀 Circulation — pulse, BP, cap refill, cardiac monitor if unstable
🧠 ABC never changes. Whatever the diagnosis, the priority framework for an unstable vital sign pattern is always Airway → Breathing → Circulation.

✅ When one abnormal vital = escalate

  • SpO2 <85% — severe hypoxia
  • New irregular pulse — get a full apical minute + notify
  • Temp <95°F — hypothermia protocol
  • Rigid, board-like abdomen + fever + tachycardia — think peritonitis

⚠️ NCLEX trap: "normal" ≠ "stable"

A single normal-range vital sign doesn't rule out deterioration — always compare to the client's own trend, not just the textbook range.

🧠 "Trend beats snapshot." A BP of 110/70 is reassuring for most people — but it's a red flag if their baseline is 160/95.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: A client's pulse is irregular. How should the nurse count it?
Count the apical pulse for a full 60 seconds — never estimate an irregular rhythm from a partial count.
Q2: SpO2 reads 83%. What tier of hypoxia is this and what's the priority?
Severe hypoxia (typically taught as <85%) — this is an emergency; assess airway/breathing immediately and escalate, don't just recheck later.
Q3: What three things must be charted for a pulse, not just the number?
Rate, rhythm, and quality (e.g., 0 absent to 4+ bounding).
Q4: Why is pain called the "5th (or 6th) vital sign"?
Because it's assessed at every vitals check just like temp/pulse/RR/BP/SpO2 — and because it's subjective, the client's report is treated as the data.

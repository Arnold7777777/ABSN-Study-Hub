Sterile Technique 🧫

Surgical Asepsis · Sterile Field Setup · Sterile Gloving

NG-074 Nursing Core ADHD-friendly visual edition

Sterile technique is performed to drastically reduce — and ideally eliminate — the chance of bacteria entering a wound, catheter site, or sterile body cavity, cutting the risk of a nosocomial (hospital-acquired) infection. Four rules protect the entire field: the 1-inch border is contaminated, anything below waist level is contaminated, reaching over the field contaminates it, and wet = contaminated.

BEFORE

STEP 1 · PURPOSE, SUPPLIES, ASSESS

Know why you're doing this, gather everything within reach, and screen the patient before you ever open a kit.

🎯 Purpose & when sterile technique is used

Surgical procedures, catheter insertion/changes, chest tube care, cervical checks, and any time you're working on a body part that wouldn't normally contact bacteria — a sterile body cavity, a fresh wound bed, the bloodstream. Surgical asepsis ("sterile technique") aims to eliminate organisms entirely — a much higher bar than the "reduce" goal of medical/clean asepsis (see the Asepsis & Handwashing page).

🧠 "If it doesn't normally meet germs, it gets sterile technique." Skin meets germs all day — a fine needle aspirate into a joint space does not.

🧰 Supplies

  • Sterile kit (pre-packaged, unexpired)
  • Running water and soap
  • Sterile gloves — sometimes packaged inside the kit
  • A clean, dry surface for setup
  • Clean paper towels
🧠 Check the expiration date before you even open the outer wrapper — an expired kit is treated as non-sterile.

⚠️ Assessment before you start

  • Confirm the need for the procedure
  • Assess the site for existing infection
  • Screen for latex, iodine, or adhesive allergies
  • Assess pain level — give analgesia 30–45 min before the procedure for comfort
🧠 "LIA before you begin" — Latex, Iodine, Adhesive. Ask about all three before you open a single package.

🩺 Nursing diagnoses & risks to anticipate

  • Risk for infection
  • Risk for impaired tissue integrity
  • Risk for pain
  • Risk for hypersensitivity reaction — think latex/iodine/adhesive
🧠 Same four risks apply to nearly every sterile procedure — infection, tissue damage, pain, and allergy — build your care plan around these before you even touch the kit.
🧤

DURING

STEP 2 · FIELD SETUP & STERILE GLOVING

Four rules govern the entire field. Break one, and the whole field — or your glove — is contaminated.

📏 The four rules of a sterile field

  • 📏 The outer 1-inch border of any sterile drape or package is always contaminated — nothing sterile may cross it
  • ⬇️ Anything held below waist level, or out of your direct line of sight, is contaminated
  • 🙅 Never reach over a sterile field — go around it instead
  • 💧 Wet = contaminated — moisture wicks bacteria through a barrier by capillary action ("strike-through")
🧠 "Border, Below, oveR, dRip" — the four rules all start with the idea of a boundary: the 1-inch edge, the waistline, reaching, and moisture all create an invisible line bacteria can cross.

🧾 Setting up the sterile field — step by step

1
🧼 Check the expiration date on the package and perform hand hygiene
2
📦 Open the kit using the special flap so the first flap opens away from you
3
🤏 Pinch the remaining flaps on their outside corners only and pull them back gently
4
🚫 Do not touch the inside — everything inside is sterile except the 1-inch border
5
🗑️ Throw the outer wrapper away
6
🧤 Get sterile gloves ready on a clean, dry surface next to the field
🧠 "Flap away, pinch the corners, hands stay out." The whole setup is really one rule repeated: your bare, ungloved hands never cross into the sterile interior.

🧤 Donning sterile gloves — closed-glove logic

1
🧼 Wash hands the same thorough way as before; dry with a clean paper towel
2
📦 Open the glove wrapper so the gloves lie in front of you — do not touch them yet
3
✋ With your dominant hand, grab the other glove by the folded wrist cuff only
4
🧤 Slide that glove on, leaving the cuff folded — don't let the outside touch your skin
5
🤲 Slide the fingers of your gloved hand under the cuff of the second glove to lift it — glove that hand keeping fingers flat, thumb away from skin
6
🔄 Reach under both cuffs and unroll them up toward your elbows
🧠 "Skin never touches sterile, sterile never touches skin." Bare hand only ever touches the folded cuff (contaminated zone by design); gloved hand only ever touches the outside of the other glove (sterile zone).

🚨 Break in technique — what to do

If a gloved hand touches anything outside the sterile field — a rail, your own skin, an unsterile surface — remove the gloves, wash hands again, and redo the entire gloving sequence with a brand-new pair. Once gloved, touch only your sterile supplies.

🧠 There is no "just this once." Any break in sterile technique means starting the gloving sequence completely over — there's no partial fix.
📋

AFTER

STEP 3 · DOCUMENT & TEACH

The procedure isn't done when the field comes down — document it and prep the patient to protect the site themselves.

📋 Documentation

  • Date and time of the procedure
  • Type of procedure performed
  • Fluid/exudate from the site — color, amount, odor
  • Follow hospital policy for dating, timing & initialing dressings, specimens, or catheter sites
  • Document the client's tolerance of the procedure
🧠 "CTFO" — Color, amount (Total), odor, Fluid type — describe drainage the same four ways every time.

🗣️ Patient education

  • Practice good personal hygiene
  • Ask for analgesia before pain becomes unbearable — don't wait it out
  • Know the signs & symptoms of infection and when to notify the HCP
🧠 Teach the same infection red flags every time: increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pain, purulent drainage, or fever — call the HCP if any appear.

🚨 Signs of infection to report

🌡️Fever>100.3°F
🔴Increasing rednessaround the site
🥵Warmth& swelling
🤕Increasing painnot resolving
🧪Purulent drainagefoul odor
🧠 Any one of these after a sterile procedure is enough to notify the HCP — don't wait for all of them to appear together.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: How much of a sterile drape's border is considered contaminated?
The outer 1 inch — everything inside that border is sterile, the border itself is not.
Q2: A nurse's sterile glove brushes the bed rail. What now?
Remove the gloves, wash hands again, and redo the entire sterile gloving sequence with a new pair — there's no partial fix for a break in technique.
Q3: Why is a wet sterile drape considered contaminated?
Moisture allows bacteria to wick through the barrier by capillary action ("strike-through") even if nothing visibly touched it.
Q4: When should analgesia be given before a sterile procedure, and why teach patients to ask early?
30–45 minutes before the procedure for comfort; patients are taught to ask before pain becomes unbearable rather than waiting it out.

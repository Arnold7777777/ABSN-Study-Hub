Crystalloid agents: which IV fluid?

Crystalloid means water containing small dissolved substances, such as salts or glucose. Common resuscitation examples are 0.9% normal saline, lactated Ringer’s and Plasma-Lyte. These are clear solutions; read the bag label to identify the fluid.

Compare the crystalloid agents

0.9% sodium chloride · NS

  • Isotonic: expands extracellular fluid, including the circulation.
  • Contains sodium and chloride; no potassium or buffer.
  • Large volumes can raise chloride and contribute to metabolic acidosis. Monitor fluid status and electrolytes. [2]

Lactated Ringer’s · LR

  • Balanced crystalloid: commonly grouped with isotonic resuscitation fluids.
  • Contains sodium, chloride, potassium, calcium and lactate, a bicarbonate precursor.
  • Check medication and blood-product compatibility; its calcium matters. Do not assume every IV fluid can share a line. [4]

Plasma-Lyte

  • Balanced isotonic crystalloid: another resuscitation option.
  • Formulations such as Plasma-Lyte A contain sodium, chloride, potassium, magnesium, acetate and gluconate.
  • Read the exact product label and assess renal function and electrolytes. [5]

0.45% sodium chloride · half NS

  • Hypotonic: promotes movement of water into cells.
  • Used for selected water-replacement needs; not an initial shock bolus.
  • Watch sodium, neurologic status and circulation. [3]

3% sodium chloride

  • Hypertonic: draws water out of cells.
  • Reserved for specific indications, such as severe symptomatic hyponatremia, under a monitored protocol.
  • Frequent sodium checks are essential; overly rapid correction can cause serious neurologic harm. [3]

D5W · 5% dextrose in water

  • Near-isotonic in the bag; after glucose is metabolized, it supplies free water.
  • Its physiologic effect differs from NS or LR.
  • Not for initial shock resuscitation. Monitor glucose and sodium. [3]

Crystalloid ≠ colloid. Albumin is a colloid containing large protein molecules. Blood products are a separate category. [1]

The sepsis-question connection

When a question says “IV bolus of a crystalloid agent,” think an ordered isotonic fluid such as NS or LR to support circulating volume and tissue perfusion.

  • Give the prescribed bolus promptly and reassess its effect. A continuous infusion afterward still needs an individualized order and ongoing review.
  • Monitor perfusion, mental status, urine output, blood pressure, breathing and ordered lactate trends.
  • Fluids accompany urgent infection treatment; they do not replace antibiotics or source control. [1]
Guideline side note: adult sepsis fluids

The 2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign suggests balanced crystalloids over normal saline. For sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock, it suggests at least 30 mL/kg within the first 3 hours; this is a weak recommendation based on low-quality evidence. Ongoing decisions require repeated assessment, including dynamic measures when available. It is not an automatic dose for every suspected infection or a standing instruction for unrestricted fluids. Follow the prescribed plan and local protocol. [1]

Three checks to remember

  1. BAG: verify the order, solution, volume, rate, IV patency and compatibility.
  2. BODY: reassess the clinical response; track intake/output, renal function and electrolytes.
  3. BREATHING: new breathlessness, crackles, falling oxygen saturation or edema needs prompt assessment for overload and escalation. Reassess before giving more fluid. [2]

Fluid physics: isotonic crystalloid distributes through extracellular fluid. Only part remains in the bloodstream; some enters tissue space. It does not stay entirely inside vessels. [6]

🦵 Endometriosis

Tissue like the lining of the uterus, growing where it does not belong — bleeding every cycle with nowhere to drain. It is the commonest reason a young woman is told her crippling periods are normal.

⭐ The one idea

Tissue similar to the uterine lining grows outside the uterus — on the ovaries, the ligaments behind the uterus, the tubes, the bowel or the bladder. It still answers to estrogen, so it builds up and bleeds with every cycle. But there is no way out.

Blood that cannot leave becomes inflammation, and inflammation becomes scar tissue and adhesions. That is the whole disease: pain, organs stuck to each other, and trouble getting pregnant.

It is not an infection. Nothing about it is treated with antibiotics.

⚠️ Not the same as endometritis

Endometri​osis and endometr​itis are a letter apart and share nothing but the word root. Mixing them up is the commonest mistake on this topic — and they turn up in the same courses, so the test knows it.

📏 The four stages

Staged at laparoscopy, on a point score built from how many implants there are, how deep they go, where they sit, and how much scarring ties things together. Four stages come out of it.

I
Minimal

A few small, shallow implants. Little or no scar tissue.

II
Mild

More implants, and deeper ones. Minor adhesions starting to form.

III
Moderate

Many deep implants, endometriomas — blood-filled ovarian cysts, the “chocolate cysts” — and significant adhesions.

IV
Severe

Widespread deep implants, large endometriomas, and dense adhesions binding organs together.

⚠️ The stage does not predict the pain. A stage I can be agonizing and a stage IV almost silent. The score describes what the surgeon sees, not what she feels — so never use a low stage to talk a client out of her symptoms. Stage mostly informs the fertility conversation and the surgical plan.

📍 Where it lands

  • Ovaries — the classic site, and where endometriomas form
  • Behind the uterus: the cul-de-sac and the uterosacral ligaments, which is why a pelvic exam can feel tender nodules there
  • Fallopian tubes — scarring here is a route to infertility and to ectopic pregnancy
  • Bowel and bladder — giving cyclic bowel or urinary symptoms that flare with her period
  • Rarely further afield, including old surgical scars

🔥 What she reports

The classic triad. Three kinds of pain, and all three track the cycle.

DysmenorrheaPainful periods that are secondary — they started later in life, not at menarche — and get worse over the years. Pain often begins 1–2 days before the flow.
DyspareuniaDeep pain with intercourse, from implants on the ligaments behind the uterus.
DyscheziaPainful bowel movements, worst during the period.

And alongside them:

  • Chronic pelvic pain between periods
  • Heavy or irregular bleeding
  • Infertility — often the reason she is finally investigated
  • Bloating, nausea, fatigue
  • Depression and anxiety, from years of pain and of being disbelieved

Some women have no symptoms at all and are found incidentally at surgery.

🔍 How it is diagnosed

There is no blood test. The work-up is:

  • History — the cyclic pattern of the pain is the strongest clue
  • Pelvic exam — tender nodules on the uterosacral ligaments, a fixed, retroverted uterus
  • Ultrasound or MRI — can show an endometrioma, but a normal scan does not rule it out
  • Laparoscopy with direct visualization ± biopsy — the definitive diagnosis and the moment it is staged

Years pass before most women are diagnosed. The pain is cyclic, the exam is often normal, and “bad periods” gets normalized. Believing her is a nursing intervention.

💊 Treatment, least invasive first

Nothing cures it. Everything below either quiets the inflammation or takes the estrogen away, because the disease is estrogen-dependent. It settles at menopause — usually.

StepWhat it isWhat she needs to know
1. NSAIDsIbuprofen, naproxenStart before the pain does — a day ahead of the flow, on a schedule. Take with food.
2. Combined hormonal contraceptionPills, patch, ring — often taken continuously to skip periodsFewer periods means less bleeding into the implants. Not for a smoker over 35.
3. ProgestinsNorethindrone, depot medroxyprogesterone, the levonorgestrel IUDBreakthrough spotting is common early. Depot can thin bone with long use.
4. GnRH agonists / antagonistsLeuprolide, elagolixShuts estrogen down — a medical menopause. Hot flashes, vaginal dryness, bone loss. Expect add-back hormones, a limited course, calcium, vitamin D and weight-bearing exercise.
5. DanazolAn older androgenAcne, hirsutism, weight gain, deepening voice — the voice change does not reverse. Teratogenic: she needs reliable non-hormonal contraception on it.
6. Conservative surgeryLaparoscopic excision or ablation of implants, lysis of adhesionsPreserves fertility. Relief is real but the disease can come back.
7. Definitive surgeryHysterectomy, usually with removal of both ovariesEnds fertility and causes surgical menopause immediately. A last resort, and never a quiet decision.

“Just get pregnant, it will fix it.” Pregnancy suppresses symptoms while it lasts; it is not a treatment and it is not advice. Many of these clients are struggling to conceive in the first place.

🩸 After a laparoscopy

Expected — teach her it is coming:

  • Shoulder and neck pain from the CO2 used to inflate the abdomen. It is referred pain, it is normal, and walking plus a heating pad and lying on the left side move it along. It clears in a day or two.
  • Some bloating, and a small amount of vaginal spotting

Discharge teaching: no heavy lifting and nothing in the vagina for about 2 weeks — no tampons, no douching, no intercourse. Warm compresses for comfort. Shower, do not soak.

🚨 Call the provider for: fever, heavy vaginal bleeding, worsening or severe abdominal pain, a rigid abdomen, redness or drainage at a port site, or trouble passing urine. Severe pain after a laparoscopy is not the gas pain — it is bleeding or an injured bowel or bladder until someone proves otherwise.

🧘 Nursing care

Pain

  • A scheduled plan around the cycle beats chasing the pain
  • Heat to the lower abdomen or back
  • Regular exercise; rest positions that ease pelvic pressure
  • Track symptoms against the calendar — it makes the pattern visible to her and to the provider

The rest of her

  • Fertility counselling early, before decisions are urgent
  • Screen for depression at every visit
  • Name the disbelief she has met, and validate the pain
  • Support groups and reliable information

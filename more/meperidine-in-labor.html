Medication safety · Exam connection

Meperidine in labor

Opioid exposure near birth can affect newborn breathing. Your question’s answer is B: assess the newborn for respiratory depression.

🎯 Keep the course answer

B — assess the newborn for respiratory depression.

Reflex checks, maternal bradycardia and newborn withdrawal are not the best response to this specific item.

Read it in small chunks

💊

01 Why the newborn is the focus

Meperidine is an opioid that crosses the placenta.

Near birth, exposure may cause excess sedation and weak or slow breathing. The question states the dose was given 1 hour before entering the second stage—not necessarily 1 hour before delivery.

🫁

02 Assess

Watch breathing rate and effort.

Also assess heart rate, oxygenation, tone and responsiveness as part of newborn assessment. Poor breathing requires prompt evaluation and support.

📣

03 Prepare & respond

Tell the birth team the drug and administration time.

Be ready for neonatal support. If breathing is ineffective, summon the neonatal team and support ventilation according to neonatal resuscitation protocol.

🩺

04 Monitor the mother too

Check respirations and sedation.

Maternal opioid effects also matter. Continue the prescribed maternal and fetal monitoring.

↔

05 Acute effect versus withdrawal

A recent labor dose points to an immediate breathing concern.

Possible withdrawal is associated with prolonged prenatal opioid exposure. It is a different concern from the acute respiratory depression emphasized in this question.

Clinical clarification: naloxone

The screenshot’s statement that Narcan is ineffective should not become a general rule. The current meperidine label describes opioid-antagonist reversal of opioid-induced neonatal respiratory depression.

That does not make naloxone the first response to a newborn who is not breathing effectively. AHA/AAP neonatal guidance prioritizes effective ventilation. Medication decisions belong to the neonatal team and protocol.

The poster preserves answer B without adding a universal medication-timing cutoff or guaranteed peak-effect time.

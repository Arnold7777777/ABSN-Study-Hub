Withdrawal Meds 🩹

Alcohol vs. opioid withdrawal — different drugs, different priorities, one nurse watching vitals

NG-277 PHARM ADHD-friendly visual edition

Withdrawal management is not one protocol — it's two very different physiologic pictures. Alcohol withdrawal can kill via seizures and delirium tremens, so benzodiazepines are the safety net. Opioid withdrawal is miserable but rarely lethal on its own, so care focuses on symptom control and medication-assisted treatment (MAT).

ALCOHOL WITHDRAWAL

STEP 1 · THE ONE THAT CAN KILL

Untreated alcohol withdrawal can progress to seizures and delirium tremens — benzodiazepines are the life-saving drug class here.

🚨 Benzodiazepines — prevent seizures & DTs

EXAM TIP Benzodiazepines (e.g., chlordiazepoxide, diazepam, lorazepam) are first-line for alcohol withdrawal because they cross-tolerate with alcohol at the GABA receptor — they prevent the two most dangerous complications: withdrawal seizures and delirium tremens (DTs).

🧠 "No benzo, no brake." Benzodiazepines are the only class on this page that directly prevents a withdrawal death — everything else is symptom comfort or long-term relapse prevention.

📊 CIWA-Ar — symptom-triggered dosing

Many units dose benzodiazepines using the CIWA-Ar (Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol, revised) — a structured symptom score (tremor, sweating, anxiety, hallucinations, orientation, etc.). A higher score → more medication; exact cutoffs vary by facility protocol.

🧠 "Score drives the dose." CIWA-Ar lets the nurse titrate benzodiazepines to the client's actual symptom burden instead of a fixed schedule.

🚫 Disulfiram (Antabuse) — the "no alcohol, anywhere" drug

Disulfiram deters drinking by causing a severe reaction (flushing, nausea/vomiting, palpitations, hypotension) if alcohol is consumed. Teach patients to avoid all alcohol sources — not just drinks.

🧠 "ARTS & CRAFTS caution." Rubbing alcohol, mouthwash, cooking wine, and alcohol-based hobby/craft products (glues, paints, hand sanitizer) can all trigger a reaction — teach clients to check labels broadly, not just beverages.
💉

OPIOID WITHDRAWAL

STEP 2 · SYMPTOM CONTROL + MAT

Rarely life-threatening on its own, but three very different tools are used together: full agonist, partial agonist, and non-opioid symptom control.

💊 Three tools, three jobs

🧠 Full details on clonidine's withdrawal role are on NG-243 · Clonidine — this page places it alongside methadone/buprenorphine so you can see the whole picture at once.

🚨 Methadone — toxicity red flags

  • 🌡️ Long half-life — can accumulate; effects may lag behind dosing
  • 🤢 Early signs of toxicity: nausea/vomiting, lethargy, frequent emesis
  • 📉 Monitor: prolonged QT interval on ECG
  • 😴 Client "falls asleep easily" — a red flag for over-sedation/toxicity, not just fatigue
🧠 "Sleepy and sick = stop and check." A methadone client who is unusually drowsy and vomiting needs an urgent reassessment, not just reassurance.

🧪 Methadone — O2 sat threshold

🧠 <90% on a methadone client is not "borderline" — treat it as an early sign of opioid over-sedation until proven otherwise.

⭐ Buprenorphine — the ceiling-effect drug

As a partial agonist, buprenorphine has a ceiling effect on respiratory depression — a safety advantage over full agonists like methadone at typical doses. It's frequently combined with naloxone (an opioid antagonist) to deter misuse, since naloxone has little effect orally/sublingually but blocks euphoria if the product is misused by injection.

🧠 "Partial credit, full safety net." Buprenorphine won't fully activate the receptor the way methadone does, which is exactly why its overdose ceiling is safer.

SAFETY & MONITORING

STEP 3 · CROSS-CUTTING PRIORITIES

Different drugs, same nursing job underneath: watch vitals, watch sedation level, and never assume "just withdrawal" explains a symptom.

✅ Universal monitoring checklist

1
🩺 Vital signs frequently — HR, BP, RR, temp, SpO₂
2
😴 Sedation/LOC checks — especially with methadone or clonidine on board
3
📉 ECG for QT if on methadone
4
🧮 Symptom scoring (e.g., CIWA-Ar) to guide alcohol withdrawal dosing
5
🗣️ Full medication reconciliation — disulfiram + hidden alcohol sources, other CNS depressants
🧠 The tools differ by substance, but the underlying nursing job is the same: catch deterioration early, before it becomes a code.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: Why are benzodiazepines the priority drug class in alcohol withdrawal?
They cross-tolerate with alcohol at the GABA receptor and prevent the two most dangerous complications — withdrawal seizures and delirium tremens (DTs).
Q2: What's the key difference between methadone and buprenorphine?
Methadone is a full opioid agonist; buprenorphine is a partial agonist with a ceiling effect on respiratory depression, giving it a wider safety margin. Both are used for MAT in opioid use disorder.
Q3: A client on methadone becomes unusually drowsy and starts vomiting repeatedly. What should the nurse do?
Recognize these as early signs of methadone toxicity — reassess urgently, check SpO2 and consider an ECG for QT prolongation, and notify the provider.
Q4: A client on disulfiram uses rubbing alcohol during an arts-and-crafts activity. Why does this matter?
Disulfiram reacts with any alcohol exposure, not just drinking — rubbing alcohol, mouthwash, and other alcohol-based products can trigger flushing, nausea/vomiting, palpitations, and hypotension.

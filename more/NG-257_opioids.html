Opioids 💊

Mu-Receptor Agonists for Severe Pain

NG-257 Pharmacology ADHD-friendly visual edition

Opioids bind mu (μ) receptors in the brain, spinal cord, and gut to block pain signals — used for severe pain, not inflammation or fever. The whole topic is one trade: great pain relief for a predictable list of side effects, and respiratory depression is always the #1 priority.

WHAT IT DOES

STEP 1 · THE MECHANISM

One receptor family explains the drug class, the effects, AND the antidote.

🔑 Opioids are the KEY, mu (μ) receptors are the LOCK

🧠 "Same lock, whole-body effects." Because mu receptors sit in the brain (pain + sedation), the brainstem (breathing), and the gut (motility), one drug class explains almost every side effect on this page.

🎯 Indication: severe pain only

Opioids treat severe acute or chronic pain — post-op, trauma, cancer, MI. They are not anti-inflammatory and not antipyretic (that's NSAIDs/acetaminophen's job).

🧠 If the stem says "reduces fever" or "anti-inflammatory," it is NOT an opioid — cross it off.

⭐ Class review — where opioids fit

CLASS: Opioid / narcotic analgesic, Schedule II controlled substance (most agents).

ACTION: Agonist at mu (and kappa/delta) opioid receptors → blocks pain transmission & perception.

🧠 Robo's map: Class → Action → Watch. If you can say what family a drug is in and what it changes, you can predict everything it watches for.

WATCH FOR

STEP 2 · LOW & SLOW

Every adverse effect is a body system slowing down — memorize them as "low and slow."

🧍 The opioid body map — "LOW & SLOW"

🧠 "LOW & SLOW" — Low RR, Low BP, Low brain (sedation) + Slow gut (constipation), Slow everything else. If you can name the mu-receptor locations, you already know this list.

🚨 Respiratory depression — the #1 priority

🧠 Assess RR BEFORE every dose — count for a full 60 seconds in a sedated patient. Depth matters too: shallow, irregular breathing is a red flag even above 12.

😴 CNS sedation — the earliest warning sign

  • Easily falls asleep mid-conversation — earlier & more sensitive than RR alone
  • Unarousable = medical emergency, do not give another dose
  • Use a validated sedation scale (e.g., POSS) — sedation always precedes respiratory arrest
🧠 Sedation is the smoke detector; apnea is the fire. Catch a rising sedation score before RR ever drops.

🩸 Orthostatic hypotension

  • Opioids cause vasodilation → drop in BP on standing
  • Dizzy / lightheaded on standing → assist to a seated position immediately
  • Never let the patient get up unassisted
🧠 "Rise slow, or you'll fall low." Change positions slowly; dangle legs before standing.

🤢 N/V and 🌸 pruritus

N/V: opioids stimulate the chemoreceptor trigger zone — give antiemetic PRN, especially with first doses.

Pruritus: caused by histamine release from mast cells — not necessarily a true allergy. Treat with antihistamine; document carefully so "allergy" isn't charted incorrectly.

🧠 Itching after morphine ≠ automatic allergy label — assess for hives, swelling, or anaphylaxis before charting "allergic."

REVERSE & TEACH

STEP 3 · NALOXONE + PREVENTION

One antidote reverses the emergency; one bowel plan prevents the everyday problem.

💉 Naloxone (Narcan) — the antidote

Mechanism: competitive mu-receptor antagonist — knocks the opioid off the receptor and reverses sedation, respiratory depression, and pupil changes.

Naloxone's effect lasts roughly 30–90 minutes — usually shorter than the opioid it is reversing. The patient can re-sedate ("renarcotize") once it wears off, so frequent reassessment and repeat dosing must continue well past the first response.

🧠 "Narcan naps first." Naloxone falls asleep before the opioid does — never assume one dose is the end of monitoring.

✅ Naloxone response — the priority order

1
🅰️ Assess ABCs — airway, breathing, circulation first
2
💨 Oxygen — support ventilation/oxygenation
3
💉 Give naloxone per order/protocol, titrate to breathing — not full alertness
4
📞 Notify HCP and prepare for a repeat dose if sedation/RR drop again
🧠 "ABC before Narcan." Airway/breathing/oxygen come first — naloxone doesn't help a completely obstructed airway.

⚠️ Naloxone nursing considerations

  • Can precipitate acute withdrawal in opioid-dependent patients: agitation, pain surge, hypertension, GI cramping
  • Give in small, titrated amounts when the goal is adequate breathing, not full pain return
  • Reassess sedation & RR frequently — do not leave the room after one good reading
  • Available IV, IM, subcutaneous, and intranasal (community/OD-reversal kits)
🧠 Reversing too fast = a wide-awake patient in severe pain and withdrawal. Titrate to breathing, not to a smile.

💩 Opioid-induced constipation — prevent, don't wait

Start PRN/scheduled stool softener + stimulant laxative on day one of opioid therapy — do not wait for symptoms. Unlike sedation or nausea, constipation does not build tolerance and will persist for the entire course of therapy.

🧠 KEY TERM: CONSTIPATION. If a care plan lists opioid teaching and stool softeners aren't on it, the plan is incomplete.

✅ Patient teaching essentials

  • ❌ No driving or operating machinery until effects are known
  • ❌ Avoid alcohol and other CNS depressants (sedative-additive, respiratory arrest risk)
  • ⚠️ Fall risk — call for help before getting up, especially first 24–48h
  • 📋 Take a proactive bowel regimen for the entire duration of therapy
  • 📞 Report new confusion, RR change, or extreme drowsiness right away
🧠 "Slow down, don't drive, don't drink, don't stand alone." Four rules cover almost every opioid safety NCLEX stem.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What is the #1 priority adverse effect of opioids, and the hold parameter?
Respiratory depression — hold the dose if RR is below 12/min, stay with the patient, and notify the HCP.
Q2: What is the antidote for opioid overdose, and why can't you walk away after one dose?
Naloxone (Narcan), a mu-receptor antagonist. Its effect (~30–90 min) is usually shorter than the opioid's, so the patient can renarcotize — reassess frequently and be ready to repeat the dose.
Q3: Which opioid side effect never develops tolerance and must be prevented from day one?
Constipation — start a scheduled stool softener plus stimulant laxative, fluids, fiber, and ambulation before symptoms start.
Q4: A patient on morphine develops generalized itching with no hives or respiratory symptoms. What's happening?
Likely histamine release, not a true allergy. Treat with an antihistamine and document carefully rather than automatically charting a drug allergy.

PORTAL HYPERTENSION — THE ROOT OF EVERYTHING

STEP 1 · CAUSE

One blocked organ, four escape routes, and every escape route is a complication.

🪨 What the scar actually does to the blood flow

Cirrhosis replaces soft, spongy liver tissue with fibrous scar and regenerative nodules. Scar tissue cannot be squeezed through, so the pressure in the portal vein climbs.

🧠 “The liver turns to rock.” Nothing flows through rock — so the river floods backwards.

🗺️ Where the blocked blood escapes to

🧠 “Water finds a way.” Oesophagus, umbilicus, rectum, spleen — the four escape routes, the four classic signs.

🧪 What portal hypertension IS

The portal vein normally runs at a low pressure. In cirrhosis the pressure rises; a portal pressure gradient above roughly 10 mmHg is the level at which the classic complications start to appear, and higher pressures carry a higher bleeding risk.

Blood that cannot get through the liver is forced into small collateral veins that were never built to carry that volume — so they stretch, thin out, and eventually tear.

🧠 “Dam the river, flood the side streets.” Collaterals are the flooded side streets.

👀 What you can see at the bedside

  • Distended, tense abdomen with a fluid wave and shifting dullness (ascites).
  • Caput medusae — dilated veins radiating from the navel.
  • Splenomegaly — you may see it on imaging and the platelet count reflects it.
  • Haemorrhoids that are new or worsening.
  • Weight gain and increasing abdominal girth without eating more.
🧠 Measure girth at the SAME marked spot every day, or the number means nothing.

🩸 Splenomegaly → low platelets

Blood dams back into the spleen and it enlarges. An enlarged spleen sequesters and destroys platelets, so the platelet count falls.

Now stack that on top of a liver that cannot make clotting factors: fewer platelets AND fewer clotting factors at the same time. That is why a cirrhotic patient bruises from a blood-pressure cuff.

LINK NG-153 · WBC & coagulation panel
🧠 “Big spleen eats platelets.” Two bleeding problems for the price of one.

⚠️ Splenomegaly also means a fragile spleen

An enlarged spleen sits below the left ribs and is easier to injure.

  • Avoid vigorous abdominal palpation over the left upper quadrant.
  • Teach the patient to avoid contact sports and heavy lifting.
  • Sudden severe left upper quadrant pain with hypotension is an emergency.
🧠 “Handle the left upper quadrant gently.”

ASCITES — THE BELLY FULL OF FLUID

STEP 2 · CLUES

Three mechanisms at once. Patients say they look pregnant; the exam calls it third spacing.

💧 Why the fluid collects — and where

🧠 A-A: Ascites = Abdominal fluid. And remember the pair that follows it: no NG tube, no straining.

👐 How ascites is assessed

📏
Abdominal girth daily, tape at a spot marked on the skin, measured at the same point in the breathing cycle.
⚖️
Daily weight — same scale, same time, after voiding. 1 kg = 1 L.
🥁
Percussion — dull over fluid, tympanic over the floating bowel; dullness shifts when the patient rolls.
🌊
Fluid wave — a tap on one flank transmits a wave to the other hand.
LINK NG-059 · Fluid balance III has the full measuring toolkit.
🧠 Girth and weight, every single day. Trends beat single measurements.

🫁 Why a big belly becomes a breathing problem

Litres of fluid push the diaphragm up and stop the lungs expanding.

  • Shortness of breath, especially lying flat; the patient will not want to lie down.
  • Rapid, shallow breathing; falling oxygen saturation.
  • Position: semi-Fowler's or high Fowler's to let the diaphragm drop.

Never lie a patient with tense ascites flat for comfort — it makes the breathing worse.

🧠 “Big belly, sit them up.” Positioning is a real intervention here, not a comfort measure.

🦠 Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)

Ascitic fluid is a warm pool of protein sitting in the abdomen. It can become infected without any obvious source — no perforation, no surgery, nothing.

  • Fever, sometimes low-grade or absent in a very sick liver patient.
  • Abdominal pain or tenderness, rebound tenderness.
  • New or worsening confusion — often the first thing the nurse notices.
  • Worsening ascites that suddenly stops responding to treatment.

Diagnosis is by sampling the ascitic fluid; treatment is antibiotics per culture and protocol.

🧠 New confusion + fever + a big belly = think SBP. Report it, do not wait for the morning round.

✅ Nursing priorities for the ascitic abdomen

  • Position semi- to high Fowler's for breathing.
  • Daily weight, girth and strict I&O.
  • Low-sodium diet, and a fluid restriction if one is ordered.
  • Skin care — stretched, oedematous abdominal skin breaks down and weeps.
  • Support the abdomen when the patient moves; teach them to splint when coughing.
  • Monitor for SBP — temperature, abdominal tenderness, mental status.

🧠 Sit up · weigh · measure · watch the skin · watch the temperature.

OESOPHAGEAL VARICES — THE EMERGENCY

STEP 3 · THE KILLER

This is the complication that kills people quickly, and the one every exam asks about.

💣 What varices are, and why they rupture

🧠 “A ticking time bomb of blood.” Thin-walled veins with the pressure of a whole blocked circulation behind them, sitting on top of the airway.

🚨 FIRST ACTION when the patient vomits blood

A
AIRWAY. Turn the patient side-lying so blood drains out of the mouth instead of into the lungs. Suction at the bedside, oxygen on.
▼
B
BREATHING. Oxygen, monitor saturation, watch for aspiration.
▼
C
CIRCULATION. Vital signs, large-bore IV access, blood work and type and cross, prepare for volume and blood products.
▼
D
Call for help and prepare for urgent endoscopy. Keep the patient NPO.

Some questions ask “what is the FIRST action?” and the safest answer is the one that protects the airway; others ask “what would the nurse do first” in a stable-sounding scenario, where obtaining vital signs is the assessment answer. Read whether the patient is actively bleeding into their mouth right now.

🧠 Blood in the mouth → turn them on their side. You cannot resuscitate a blocked airway.

❌ The two orders you QUESTION

  • A new nasogastric tube in a patient with known or suspected varices — a tube passed blindly down the oesophagus can tear a varix open. Question the order and speak to the provider.
  • Anything that causes straining — enemas that require bearing down, hard suppositories, and any instruction to push.

Also question aspirin and NSAIDs unless specifically ordered, and any invasive oral or oesophageal device.

🧠 “No tube down the pipe.” If the exam gives you an NG tube order in a varices patient, that is the answer.

🚽 Why straining is so dangerous

Bearing down (the Valsalva manoeuvre) raises intra-abdominal and intrathoracic pressure. That pressure is transmitted straight to the already-stretched varices.

  • Teach: do not strain with bowel movements, do not hold your breath when moving up the bed.
  • Stool softeners as ordered so that stools stay soft.
  • Also avoid heavy lifting and forceful coughing where possible.
🧠 “Don't push.” The exam wording is usually “avoid straining when having a bowel movement.”

👀 Recognising a bleed before it is obvious

SIGNWHAT IT MEANS
Haematemesis — bright red vomitActive, brisk upper GI bleeding
Coffee-ground emesisBlood that has been sitting in the stomach
Melaena — black tarry stoolDigested blood from an upper GI source
Rising pulse, falling BPLosing volume — often before any visible blood
New confusionPoor perfusion, and a protein load feeding ammonia production
Falling haematocritLosing red cells as well as plasma
GO DEEPER NG-304 · GI bleed
🧠 Black tarry stool is old blood; bright red vomit is new blood. Both are emergencies here.

✅ Preventing the first bleed

  • Non-selective beta blockers as prescribed, to lower portal pressure. Check heart rate and blood pressure against the hold parameters before every dose.
  • Endoscopic banding of known varices, done electively.
  • No alcohol, no NSAIDs, no straining, no blind NG tubes.
  • Teach the patient and family what haematemesis and melaena look like, and to call emergency services.
🧠 Prevention beats resuscitation. The beta blocker is doing quiet work every day.

HEPATIC ENCEPHALOPATHY

STEP 4 · THE BRAIN

Cloudy brain from ammonia. The nurse usually spots it before the lab does.

🧪 Where the ammonia comes from and where it ends up

🧠 Protein → ammonia → liver → urea → urine. Break the liver step and the ammonia goes to the brain instead.

🖐️ Asterixis — how to test for it

🧠 “The liver flap.” Arms out, hands back, watch. It looks like a bird flapping its wings.

👀 The three key assessments

1
Ask the orientation questions. Name, date of birth, date, place. Ask them the same way every shift.
2
Extend the arms and watch the hands. Asterixis is the classic motor sign.
3
Compare with the previous shift's documentation and with recent ammonia levels — the direction of travel matters more than one number.

That trio is the answer to the classic select-all-that-apply question about detecting hepatic encephalopathy.

🧠 “Ask · Extend · Compare.” Three assessments, one diagnosis.

📉 How it progresses

1
Subtle. Disturbed sleep pattern (awake at night, sleepy by day), mild forgetfulness, slight personality change. Families notice this first.
▼
2
Obvious. Lethargy, disorientation, asterixis, inappropriate or bizarre behaviour.
▼
3
Marked. Very drowsy but rousable, confused, incoherent speech, cannot follow commands.
▼
4
Coma. Unresponsive. Airway and aspiration risk.
🧠 Sleep pattern first, coma last. Day–night reversal is a real early sign, not just “bad sleep”.

🔥 What sets it off

  • GI bleeding — a huge protein load dumped into the gut. This is the big one.
  • Constipation — ammonia sits in the bowel longer and is reabsorbed.
  • Infection, including SBP.
  • Dehydration and electrolyte disturbance, often from over-diuresis.
  • Sedatives and opioids, which the failing liver cannot clear.
  • A large protein meal in a patient who is already borderline.

Never give a sedative to “settle” a confused cirrhotic patient without a provider's assessment.

🧠 Bleed · block · bug · dry · drug. Five triggers to check whenever the mental status drops.

🛡️ Safety while they are confused

  • Fall precautions — bed low, call bell in reach, frequent rounding.
  • Aspiration precautions — assess swallowing before anything by mouth; keep them sitting up.
  • Reorient calmly and keep the environment consistent; involve family who know their baseline.
  • Skin and pressure care — a lethargic patient does not reposition themselves.
  • Document the mental status in the same words each time so the next nurse can compare.
TREATMENT Lactulose and non-absorbed antibiotics are covered on NG-148 and NG-122 · Lactulose.
🧠 Confused liver patient = fall risk + aspiration risk. Both, every time.

THE LIVER-FAILURE LAB PANEL

STEP 5 · LABS

Which ones go up, which go down, and — more usefully — why.

📊 Every value, and the reason behind it

🧠 “The liver's ABC goes DOWN: Albumin, Bile handling, Coagulation. Everything the liver was supposed to REMOVE goes UP.”

🩸 Coagulopathy — two problems stacked

The liver makes most clotting factors and needs vitamin K to activate several of them. It also sits upstream of a spleen that is now destroying platelets.

PT · PTT · INRAll PROLONGED — it takes longer to clot.
PlateletsLOW — trapped and destroyed in a big spleen.

Result: bruising from a blood-pressure cuff, bleeding gums, prolonged oozing from any puncture, and a GI bleed that will not stop on its own.

LINK NG-153 · Coagulation panel
🧠 “High PT, low platelets, easy bleeder.”

🅰️ Low albumin drives half the picture

Typical adult albumin is 3.5–5.0 g/dL. In cirrhosis it falls, and that single value explains several findings at once:

  • Ascites and peripheral oedema — nothing left to hold water in the vessels.
  • Low total calcium — much of the calcium in blood is carried bound to albumin, so the total falls with it (typical calcium 9.0–10.5 mg/dL).
  • Drugs behave unpredictably — many are albumin-bound, so more free drug circulates.
🧠 Low albumin = low calcium reading + water in the wrong place.

🦴 Low calcium — the two classic signs

TROUSSEAU'S SIGNInflate a blood-pressure cuff above systolic for a few minutes → the hand and wrist go into carpal spasm.
CHVOSTEK'S SIGNTap over the facial nerve just in front of the ear → the facial muscles twitch.

Also watch for numbness and tingling around the mouth and in the fingers, muscle cramps, and hyperactive reflexes.

🧠 “TrousseauS = Spasm of the hand. ChvosteK = Cheek.”

🧪 Enzymes vs function — a distinction that gets tested

TELLS YOUTESTS
Liver cells are being DAMAGEDALT, AST (leaked out of injured cells)
Liver FUNCTION is failingAlbumin, PT/INR, bilirubin, ammonia

Enzymes can even be near normal in end-stage cirrhosis — there are too few working liver cells left to leak anything. A normal ALT does not mean a healthy liver.

🧠 Enzymes = damage. Albumin, INR and bilirubin = function. Function is what matters.

🎓 Select all that apply — the classic version

Which values are expected to be ELEVATED in worsening liver failure?

  • ✅ Ammonia — the liver cannot convert it to urea.
  • ✅ Bilirubin — it cannot be cleared into the bile.
  • ✅ Prothrombin time (PT) / INR — fewer clotting factors, so it takes longer.
  • ❌ Albumin — goes DOWN; the liver cannot make it.
  • ❌ Calcium — goes DOWN, largely because albumin does.
🧠 The trick in this question is that albumin and calcium travel together, and they travel in the OPPOSITE direction to everything else.

JAUNDICE, ITCHING AND THE REST OF THE BODY

STEP 6 · HEAD TO TOE

The visible signs that make an exam question recognisable in one sentence.

💛 The jaundice cascade

🧠 “The Bile Bus has stopped running.” The pigment cannot get to the toilet, so it goes into the skin instead.

🧍 Head-to-toe in one card

SYSTEMWHAT YOU FIND
👁️ Eyes / skinYellow sclera and skin, spider angiomas, palmar erythema, easy bruising, scratch marks
🧠 NeuroConfusion, day–night reversal, asterixis, lethargy → coma
🫁 RespiratoryShortness of breath from a raised diaphragm; rapid shallow breathing
🍽️ GIAnorexia, nausea, distended abdomen, haemorrhoids, melaena, haematemesis
🚽 RenalDark tea-coloured urine, low output if the circulation is depleted
🦵 PeripheralPeripheral oedema, muscle wasting despite a big belly
🩸 HaematologicalLow platelets, prolonged PT/INR, anaemia
🧠 Big belly, thin arms. Muscle wasting with abdominal distension is the classic picture.

🎓 The select-all you will actually see

Worsening liver failure — which findings should the nurse expect?

  • ✅ Enlarged abdomen from ascites
  • ✅ Bruise marks on the skin
  • ✅ Fatigue and possible confusion
  • ✅ Sclera that appears yellow
  • ✅ Reports of itchy skin

All five are correct — and each maps to one failed liver job: fluid handling, clotting, ammonia clearance, bilirubin clearance, bile salt clearance.

🧠 Every symptom = one job the liver stopped doing. Learn the jobs, predict the symptoms.

🩺 What the nurse reports immediately

  • Any haematemesis or melaena — treat as a bleed until proven otherwise.
  • New or deepening confusion, new asterixis, day–night reversal.
  • Fever or abdominal tenderness in a patient with ascites — think SBP.
  • Sudden increase in girth or weight, or breathlessness lying down.
  • Falling blood pressure with a rising pulse.
  • Reduced urine output — hepatorenal involvement is a serious development.

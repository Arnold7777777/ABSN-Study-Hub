Read the text

Category I · normal
  • All must fit: baseline 110 to 160 bpm, moderate variability (6 to 25 bpm), no late or variable decelerations.
  • Early decelerations and accelerations may be present or absent.
  • Continue routine intrapartum care.
Category II · indeterminate
  • Anything that is not I or III: tachycardia over 160, minimal or marked variability, recurrent lates or variables with variability remaining, a prolonged deceleration.
  • Evaluate the cause, correct what can be corrected, reassess. Category II alone does not mean acidemia or require a cesarean.
Category III · abnormal
  • Absent variability plus one of: recurrent late decelerations, recurrent variable decelerations, or bradycardia under 110.
  • Or a true sinusoidal pattern: smooth regular waves, 3 to 5 cycles a minute for 20 minutes or more.
  • Act now: team evaluation and corrective measures; if it does not resolve, prepare for expedited birth.
  • Absent variability alone is not III; minimal is not the same as absent.
Variability and the three dips
  • Absent: undetectable. Minimal: 5 bpm or less. Moderate: 6 to 25 bpm. Marked: more than 25 bpm.
  • Early: gradual, mirrors the contraction — head compression.
  • Late: gradual, lowest point after the contraction peak — uteroplacental insufficiency.
  • Variable: abrupt, onset to lowest point under 30 seconds — cord compression.
Nursing response
  • Check and call: confirm the fetal signal against the maternal pulse, check vitals, alert the team.
  • Reposition: turn her to her side; avoid lying flat. Reassess.
  • Pause stimulation: reduce or stop oxytocin for concerning changes or tachysystole, per protocol.
  • Treat the cause: ordered IV fluid for hypotension, treat fever, amnioinfusion may be ordered for recurrent variables.
  • Oxygen: your ATI book includes oxygen by mask for late decelerations; the 2025 ACOG guideline on this page limits it to maternal hypoxia.
  • Escalate: if III persists despite corrective care, prepare for expedited birth.
  • Recurrent = with at least half of contractions over 20 minutes. Prolonged = a drop of 15 bpm or more for 2 to under 10 minutes. Tachysystole = more than 5 contractions in 10 minutes, averaged over 30 minutes.

Fetal heart patterns, explained

Compare the heart-rate change with the contraction below it. The timing gives you the clue.

Fetal heart rateUterine activity

Start with the timing, then connect the pattern to its cause.

01

Early decelerations

The dip mirrors the contraction

The lowest heart rate lines up with the contraction peak. The dip begins and ends with the contraction.

WHY IT HAPPENS
Head compression stimulates a vagal response.
NURSING RESPONSE
Usually benign when the rest of the tracing is reassuring. Continue assessment. Check cervical dilation and fetal descent when indicated.

Early = head compression

02

Late decelerations

The dip follows the contraction peak

The lowest heart rate comes after the contraction peak. Recovery occurs after the contraction ends.

WHY IT HAPPENS
Reduced oxygen transfer through the placenta.
NURSING RESPONSE
Turn her onto her left side. Stop the oxytocin if it is running. Increase IV fluids. Give oxygen if indicated by maternal hypoxia, or per agency policy or provider prescription. Notify the provider. (ATI’s list; correct hypotension, check blood pressure and contractions.)

Late = placental insufficiency

03

Variable decelerations

An abrupt drop with changing timing

The drop is abrupt: onset to lowest point takes less than 30 seconds. The relationship to contractions may vary.

WHY IT HAPPENS
Usually umbilical cord compression.
NURSING RESPONSE
Reposition the mother. Assess new decelerations after membrane rupture for possible cord prolapse. Escalate persistent or recurrent concerning patterns.

Variable = cord compression

04

Accelerations

A rise above the baseline

At 32 weeks or later: rise of at least 15 bpm for at least 15 seconds, but less than 2 minutes. Before 32 weeks: at least 10 bpm for at least 10 seconds.

WHY IT HAPPENS
Often a normal response to fetal movement.
NURSING RESPONSE
A reassuring feature. Continue routine assessment of the whole tracing and the clinical situation.

Acceleration = reassuring feature

Read the whole tracing. These simplified examples teach timing. Also assess baseline, variability, recurrence, contractions, and the clinical situation.

Oxygen, your textbook’s answer (ATI): give oxygen if indicated by maternal hypoxia, or per agency policy or provider prescription. Side note: newer guidance (ACOG 2025) advises against routine oxygen when the mother’s oxygenation is normal. The exam answer stays ATI’s.

Schematics are not to scale. A single pattern does not establish the complete fetal heart-rate category. Educational review, not a bedside treatment algorithm.

🧠 How to tell early from late in one look

Early decelerations line up with the contraction. The lowest point of the dip sits directly under the peak.

Late decelerations lag. The dip begins after the contraction starts, its lowest point comes after the contraction’s peak, and it has not recovered by the time the contraction is over.

Early decelerations usually mirror contractions and reflect head compression. Late decelerations recover after contractions and can indicate reduced placental oxygen transfer. Interpret the whole tracing and the clinical situation.

🚨 Late decelerations — assess, then resuscitate in utero

🔍 First, read the whole tracing

A late deceleration is never read on its own. Check, every time:

  • Baseline rate — normal, tachycardic or bradycardic?
  • Variability — moderate is reassuring; minimal or absent is not
  • Accelerations present or absent; decelerations — which type, and are they recurrent (with half or more of contractions)?
  • Contractions — more than 5 in 10 minutes (averaged over 30) is tachysystole
  • The trend — better or worse than the last review — and the category below
CategoryWhat it looks likeResponse
I — normalBaseline 110–160, moderate variability, no late or variable decels (early decels and accels may be present)Routine monitoring
II — indeterminateAnything that is not I or III — e.g. recurrent late decels with moderate variability, minimal variability, tachycardiaEvaluate the cause, intrauterine resuscitation, notify, re-evaluate
III — abnormalAbsent variability plus recurrent late decels, recurrent variable decels or bradycardia; or a sinusoidal patternNotify at once; resuscitate; prepare for expedited delivery if it does not resolve

🩺 Intrauterine resuscitation — matched to the cause

Turn her to a side-lying position first (your ATI decelerations section lists it as the first action), then add the measures that fit what is causing the pattern; often several happen at once.

  • Reposition — lateral, left or right, whichever improves the tracing
  • IV fluid bolus per order or protocol
  • Stop or reduce oxytocin, especially with tachysystole
  • Treat maternal hypotension — common after an epidural
  • Cervical exam — rule out cord prolapse or rapid descent
  • Notify the provider early, alongside the rest — not as the last step

Oxygen is not routine. Give it for maternal hypoxia, or per your unit’s protocol — ACOG’s 2025 guidance advises against routine maternal oxygen for category II or III tracings when she is not hypoxic.

⭐ Why lateral positioning helps

The uterus rests on the inferior vena cava when the mother is supine. That reduces venous return, which drops cardiac output, which drops placental blood flow.

Turning her onto her side lifts the uterus off the vessel. Venous return recovers, so placental perfusion recovers.

Left side is the classic answer, but either side relieves the vena cava — and for cord compression, the right position is whichever one frees the cord.

💧 Amnioinfusion

For repetitive variable decelerations, sterile fluid is infused into the uterus to cushion the cord. It treats compression, not placental insufficiency.

👀 Reading the strip properly

Baseline

Normal fetal heart rate is 110–160 bpm, assessed over 10 minutes between contractions.

  • Tachycardia >160 — maternal fever, infection, dehydration, drugs
  • Bradycardia <110 — cord compression, maternal hypotension, rapid descent

✅ Variability — the most important single feature

Moderate variability means the fetal nervous system is well oxygenated. The line should look slightly bumpy, fluctuating 6–25 bpm.

VariabilityMeans
Moderate (6–25)Reassuring — the single best sign of fetal wellbeing
AbsentConcerning — hypoxia, acidosis, or sedation
MinimalMay be sleep cycle, drugs, or early hypoxia
MarkedInvestigate

A flat line with no variability is more worrying than an occasional deceleration. Variability is the thing to protect.

Only internal monitoring measures how strong a contraction is — external toco shows frequency and duration only.

🎯 NCLEX traps

  • Late = placental = the bad one. Variable = cord = reposition
  • Early decelerations need no action
  • No fixed ladder — read the whole tracing, then reposition, fluids, stop or reduce oxytocin, treat hypotension, check for prolapse, and notify
  • Oxygen only for maternal hypoxia or per protocol — not routine
  • Category III = absent variability with recurrent decels or bradycardia, or sinusoidal — prepare for expedited delivery
  • Moderate variability is the best reassurance there is
  • Internal monitoring requires ruptured membranes

Peripherally Acting Antiadrenergics 🧵

Reserpine & guanethidine-class agents — the older "empty the tank" antihypertensives

NG-260 PHARM ADHD-friendly visual edition

These drugs act at the peripheral nerve ending itself — inhibiting the release of norepinephrine before it can ever reach a receptor. That's a different target than the alpha-1 receptor blockers (NG-141) and a different location than clonidine's central brainstem action (NG-110/NG-243). Older, less commonly prescribed today, but still a testable class.

WHAT IT DOES

STEP 1 · THE MECHANISM

This is a different mechanism than an alpha-1 receptor blocker — it stops norepinephrine from ever being released, instead of blocking the receptor it would land on.

🧵 Presynaptic NE release blockade vs postsynaptic receptor blockade

EXAM TIP Peripherally acting antiadrenergics inhibit the release of norepinephrine from adrenergic nerve endings in the peripheral nervous system — the signal never gets sent. That's different from an alpha-1 blocker, which lets NE release normally but occupies the receptor so it can't bind.

🧠 "Peripherally acting antiadrenergics" is an umbrella term for TWO different tricks — cut off the supply (presynaptic) or block the door (postsynaptic). Both happen outside the brain (peripheral), both end in vasodilation, but they attack the signal at different points. Don't assume every drug labeled "peripherally acting" works the identical way.

💊 The prototype drugs

  • Reserpine (Serpasil) — depletes norepinephrine from storage vesicles in the nerve ending; classic older antihypertensive
  • Guanethidine — blocks the release of stored norepinephrine from the nerve terminal; rarely used today
  • Doxazosin (Cardura) & Prazosin (Minipress) — appear in this drug class in some textbooks, but mechanistically they're postsynaptic alpha-1 receptor blockers (see NG-141) — a useful NCLEX distinction to make out loud

Doxazosin: HTN 1–8 mg/day, BPH 1–16 mg/day PO. Prazosin: HTN 1–20 mg/day PO in divided doses. (No confident standard dose is given here for reserpine or guanethidine — verify against current drug references if a dose is needed; these are rarely prescribed today.)

🧠 "Same address, different apartment." Reserpine/guanethidine live in the nerve ending (presynaptic); doxazosin/prazosin live on the target cell (postsynaptic) — both are technically "peripheral," which is why textbooks group them together even though the mechanism differs.

⭐ Indications

  • Hypertension
  • BPH (for the alpha-1-blocker members of this group — see NG-160)
🧠 "Empty the tank, drop the pressure." Whether the tank is emptied at the source (reserpine) or the door is blocked at the destination (prazosin), norepinephrine's vasoconstricting effect never reaches the vessel — pressure falls either way.

WATCH FOR

STEP 2 · THE DANGER LIST

Generalized CNS effects plus the classic peripheral hypotension picture — and one very specific reserpine contraindication.

🚨 Adverse effects

Generalized/CNS: dry mouth, drowsiness, sedation, anorexia, rash, malaise, weakness.

Peripheral (specific to this class): hypotension, weakness, lightheadedness, bradycardia.

🧠 "Slow, dry, and dizzy." The generalized CNS depression plus the hemodynamic drop together explain almost every symptom on this list.

🚨 Reserpine-specific contraindications

  • Active peptic ulcer disease
  • Ulcerative colitis
  • Mental depression

Reserpine depletes catecholamines and can also deplete CNS serotonin/dopamine stores — this is why it's linked to worsening depression, and it increases vagal/GI acid activity, which is why it's avoided in active ulcer disease and ulcerative colitis.

🧠 "Reserpine empties more than the norepinephrine tank." Depleting catecholamines centrally too is exactly why it can unmask or worsen depression — a classic exam trap for an "old" drug.

TEACH

STEP 3 · WHAT THE CLIENT NEEDS TO HEAR

Orthostatic hypotension precautions are the heart of the teaching plan — plus the never-stop-abruptly rule shared with beta-blockers and clonidine.

✅ Orthostatic hypotension — the full step-by-step

Provide assistance getting out of bed or a chair if symptoms are severe. Instruct the client to avoid standing in one place for prolonged periods, and to avoid hot showers or baths, which add extra vasodilation on top of the drug's own effect.

🧠 "1 minute sitting, 1 minute standing, THEN walk." Two full pauses give the cardiovascular system time to catch up before the client moves.

✅ Everyday safety teaching

  • Never stop the drug abruptly — taper only under provider guidance
  • Report signs of heart failure — weight gain, difficulty breathing, extremity edema
  • Don't take OTC cold/flu remedies or nasal decongestants without checking first — many contain adrenergic stimulants that fight the drug's purpose
  • Inform dentists and other providers you're on this therapy
  • Keep all follow-up appointments — close monitoring matters
  • Caution with driving/hazardous tasks — drowsiness, dizziness, lightheadedness are possible
🧠 "Stopping suddenly precipitates or worsens adverse effects" — most of these drugs need a gradual dosage decrease, not a hard stop.

🆚 Central vs peripheral — don't mix them up

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: How do peripherally acting antiadrenergics like reserpine lower blood pressure?
They inhibit the release of norepinephrine from peripheral adrenergic nerve endings — a presynaptic mechanism, different from a receptor blocker.
Q2: Why is reserpine contraindicated in a client with a history of depression?
It depletes catecholamine (and CNS monoamine) stores, which can unmask or worsen depression.
Q3: What is the difference between this class and clonidine?
This class acts peripherally at the nerve ending to block NE release; clonidine is a central alpha-2 AGONIST acting in the brainstem to turn down sympathetic outflow.
Q4: What's the key orthostatic hypotension teaching sequence?
Sit on the edge of the bed and pause about 1 minute, then stand and pause about 1 minute, then ambulate — with assistance and a call light nearby if symptoms are severe.

Heart Defects 🫀

Pathophysiology & Causes — the foundation page for the whole set

NG-001 CARDIO · PEDS ADHD-friendly visual edition

A congenital heart defect is an abnormality in the heart that develops before birth, where one or more problems with the heart's structure change the way blood flows through the heart and out to the body. There are more than 150 described types, but they all end in the same sentence: less oxygen-rich blood OUT to the body → decreased cardiac output → murmurs, dysrhythmias, heart failure & hypoxia.

THE NORMAL HEART

STEP 0 · LEARN THIS ONCE

You cannot see what is broken until you can see what is normal. Learn this one picture and all 150+ defects become variations on it.

🫀 Anterior cutaway — chambers, septa, valves & great vessels

EXAM TIP Blue side and red side never mix in a normal heart — the septum is the wall that keeps them apart, and the valves are the one-way doors that keep blood moving forward.

🧠 “Blue on the right, red on the left — and never the two shall meet.” A congenital defect is simply a place where they do meet, or a door that is too tight, or a pipe plugged into the wrong socket.

🅥 V–V: Veins Vacuum blood back to the heart

In a normal heart, deoxygenated blood is vacuumed back to the heart through the VEINS — through the vena cava into the right side. It is then pushed into the lungs to get oxygenated. That oxygen-rich blood returns to the LEFT side and is pumped OUT to the body — this is cardiac OUTput.

🧠 V–V: Veins Vacuum. And OUTput = OUT to the body. If you can say those two lines you already know what every defect ruins.
🧨

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

STEP 1 · WHAT ACTUALLY BREAKS

Three things can go wrong with the plumbing — a hole, a narrowing, or a wrong connection. Everything else is detail.

🔀 Shunt direction is the whole exam question

A shunt is blood taking a shortcut through an abnormal opening. Blood always flows from HIGH pressure to LOW pressure. Normally the left side runs at much higher pressure than the right — so if you punch a hole, blood goes LEFT → RIGHT first.

deoxygenated oxygenated mixed abnormal opening
🧠 “Left to right = light (pink). Right to left = rotten (blue).” If the shortcut carries oxygenated blood the wrong way you get a pink baby with flooded lungs. If it carries deoxygenated blood the wrong way you get a blue baby.

🗂️ Four hemodynamic families — 150+ defects, four buckets

Do not memorize 150 names. Memorize what the blood is doing, then drop each defect into a bucket.

📚 Named defects from the source list: aortic valve stenosis, coarctation of the aorta, Ebstein's anomaly, patent ductus arteriosus, pulmonary valve stenosis, septal defects, single ventricle defects, total anomalous pulmonary venous connection, tetralogy of Fallot, transposition of the great arteries, truncus arteriosus.

🧠 “Too much lung, too little lung, blocked door, or blender.” Four buckets — put every defect name you meet into one of them and the signs write themselves.

📉 The final common pathway — decreased cardiac OUTput

Whatever the defect, less blood is pumped OUT of the heart. Decreased cardiac output = less oxygen-rich blood OUT to the body.

🧠 Every complication on this page is downstream of that one arrow. Fix the flow → fix the output.

🚨 The four big complications

  • Abnormal heart rhythms — stretched, overloaded chambers fire dysrhythmias
  • Murmurs — turbulent blood crossing a hole or a tight valve makes noise
  • Heart failure — the chamber that is volume- or pressure-overloaded eventually fails
  • Hypoxia — low oxygen in the blood, the reason a cyanotic baby is blue
🧠 “RM-HH” — Rhythms · Murmurs · Heart failure · Hypoxia. Read My Heart's History.

🔊 Why a murmur happens at all

Answer first: a murmur is the sound of turbulence. Smooth flow is silent; blood squeezing through a hole or a tight valve is not.

🧠 Narrow the river, hear the rapids. A new murmur in a newborn is never “nothing” — report it.

CAUSES & RISK

STEP 2 · WHY IT HAPPENED

Two columns only: what the baby was born with, and what happened during the pregnancy.

🗓️ When it happens — the heart is built early

The heart forms in the first trimester, roughly weeks 3–8 of gestation. That is often before a person knows they are pregnant — which is exactly why prevention is a preconception teaching point.

🧠 “Built by 8, then it's too late.” The heart is finished before most people finish the first trimester — teaching happens before conception, not after.

🧬 Genetics

  • Family history of congenital heart disease
  • Down syndrome (trisomy 21) — the classic exam association; strongly linked with AVSD and VSD

👉 Any newborn with a known chromosomal syndrome gets a cardiac evaluation — do not assume the heart is fine.

🧠 Down = Down the middle. Trisomy 21 → the middle of the heart (the AV canal / septum) is the usual problem.

🤰 During pregnancy

  • Infection — rubella 🦠 (maternal rubella in the first trimester)
  • Alcohol / drug use 🍷💊
  • Diabetes 🩸 — poorly controlled maternal diabetes

❌ There is no “safe amount” of alcohol in pregnancy — this is a teaching point, not a judgment call.

🧠 “RAD” babies — Rubella · Alcohol & drugs · Diabetes. Three pregnancy exposures, one heart.

✅ Prevention & preconception teaching — what the nurse actually says

💉Rubella immunitycheck & vaccinate BEFORE pregnancy
🩸Glucose controltight control before & during
🚭No alcoholno tobacco, no illicit drugs
💊Review medswith the provider before conception
🥬Folic acidpreconception supplementation
🧬Genetic counselingif family hx or a prior affected child

EXAM TIP The rubella (MMR) vaccine is a live vaccine — it is not given during pregnancy. Screen and immunize before conception, or postpartum.

🧠 Live vaccine + pregnancy = ✋. If the question offers “give MMR now” to a pregnant client, it's the wrong answer.

CLUES

STEP 3 · WHAT YOU SEE

A baby cannot tell you they are short of breath — they tell you with feeding, weight, color and work of breathing.

👶 Head-to-toe: where a heart defect shows up on the body

🧠 “Sweaty, skinny, breathless, blue.” Four words = the whole peds cardiac assessment. Feeding is an infant's marathon — if they sweat and stop early, the heart is the suspect.

⭐ Acyanotic vs cyanotic — tell them apart

Shunt LEFT → RIGHTShunt RIGHT → LEFT or mixing
↑ pulmonary blood flow↓ pulmonary blood flow / mixed
Baby looks pinkBaby looks blue — central cyanosis

ASD · VSD · PDA · AVSD · stenosis · coarctationTetralogy of Fallot · TGA · truncus · TAPVR · tricuspid atresia
🧠 The 5 T's of cyanotic disease: Tetralogy of Fallot · Transposition · Truncus arteriosus · Total anomalous pulmonary venous connection · Tricuspid atresia.

🧪 Diagnostics — how it gets confirmed

  • Echocardiogram ⭐ — the definitive non-invasive test; shows structure and flow direction
  • Pulse oximetry screening — routine newborn screen for critical defects
  • Chest X-ray — heart size & pulmonary vascular markings
  • ECG — chamber hypertrophy, dysrhythmia
  • Cardiac catheterization — invasive; measures pressures & O₂ saturations chamber by chamber, and can be therapeutic
🧠 Echo = the eyes. Cath = the pressure gauge. If the question asks “which test confirms the structural defect,” echo is your first instinct.

🚨 Cyanosis that does NOT improve with oxygen

HIGH YIELD In a cyanotic (right-to-left) defect, blood is bypassing the lungs altogether — so giving more oxygen does not fix the saturation. That failure to respond is a red flag for a structural problem rather than a lung problem.

❌ Never assume a blue newborn who doesn't “pink up” on oxygen just needs a higher FiO₂ — escalate.

🧠 “O₂ can't fix a detour.” If the blood never meets the alveoli, the FiO₂ doesn't matter.

CARE

STEP 4 · WHAT YOU DO

Foundation nursing care that applies to every defect in the set — the specifics live on the four linked pages.

✅ Priority ladder — the shared nursing plan

1
Oxygenation & work of breathing — assess color, respiratory rate/effort, SpO₂; position for comfort; cluster care to limit oxygen demand.
▼
2
Cardiac output — apical pulse for a full minute, blood pressure, capillary refill, peripheral pulses, level of consciousness.
▼
3
Feeding & growth — small, frequent feeds; rest periods; strict intake & output; daily weights on the same scale at the same time.
▼
4
Infection prevention — keep immunizations current; teach families about fever and about dental/procedure prophylaxis if the cardiologist has prescribed it.
▼
5
Family support & education — signs of worsening heart failure, when to call, medication schedules, growth expectations.
🧠 Airway & oxygen before anything else. In peds cardiac questions the correct first action is almost always assess breathing and perfusion, not call the provider — unless the finding is already an emergency.

🍼 Feeding is the infant's stress test

  • Small, frequent feeds — a long feed burns more calories than it delivers
  • Limit each feed and allow rest periods
  • Higher-calorie feeds may be prescribed to support growth
  • Watch for sweating, tiring, tachypnea, color change during the feed
🧠 “Sweating at the bottle = running a race.” Diaphoresis with feeding is one of the earliest and most quotable signs of a peds cardiac problem.

🚨 Teach families: when to call

  • Color change — new or worsening blueness around lips
  • Breathing harder — faster, retractions, grunting, flaring
  • Feeding worse or fewer wet diapers
  • Sudden weight gain or new puffiness around the eyes
  • Fever, or extreme sleepiness / difficulty waking
🧠 “Blue, breathing, bottle, balance (weight).” Four B's the parents can remember at 3 a.m.

🎯 Cover & check — 6 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What is a congenital heart defect, in one sentence?
An abnormality in the heart that develops before birth, where a problem with the heart's structure changes how blood flows through the heart and out to the body.
Q2: Which direction does a shunt flow first, and why?
Left → right. Blood always moves from high pressure to low pressure, and the left side of the heart is the high-pressure side. That is why these defects are acyanotic (pink) at first.
Q3: Name the four big complications of congenital heart defects.
Abnormal heart rhythms, murmurs, heart failure, and hypoxia — all downstream of decreased cardiac output.
Q4: Give the two genetic risk factors and the three pregnancy risk factors.
Genetics: family history and Down syndrome. Pregnancy: infection (rubella), alcohol/drug use, and diabetes.
Q5: An infant sweats and tires after only a few minutes of feeding, and is not gaining weight. What are you thinking?
Classic congenital heart disease with heart failure. Feeding is an infant's exercise. Assess respiratory rate/effort, color, apical pulse and perfusion; plan small frequent feeds with rest periods, strict I&O and daily weights; report to the provider.
Q6: A newborn is cyanotic and the saturation barely changes on supplemental oxygen. What does that suggest?
A right-to-left (cyanotic) structural defect — blood is bypassing the lungs, so more FiO₂ can't oxygenate it. Escalate; this is not a simple lung problem.

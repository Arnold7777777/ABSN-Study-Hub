Gout 🦶

Crystal Arthropathy — Uric Acid, Not Autoimmune

NG-200 Musculoskeletal ADHD-friendly visual edition

Gout is a crystal arthropathy — uric acid accumulates and crystallizes inside a joint, triggering intense pain and inflammation. This is NOT an autoimmune disease — nothing is attacking "self" tissue like in rheumatoid arthritis (see NG-163); it's a metabolic/chemistry problem where a waste product builds up until it precipitates out as sharp crystals. Classic first attack: the big toe.

CAUSE

STEP 1 · THE CRYSTAL, NOT THE IMMUNE SYSTEM

Uric acid is a normal waste product — gout happens when there's too much of it to stay dissolved.

🧬 Pathophysiology — uric acid crystallizes inside the joint

Uric acid accumulation causes pain & inflammation in the joints, which leads to destruction and — over time — arthritis (bone-on-bone pain from loss of cartilage). Uric acid is the breakdown product of purines (found in many foods and normal cell turnover). When more uric acid is made than the kidneys can clear — or the kidneys simply can't clear enough — it builds up in the blood (hyperuricemia) and precipitates into sharp, needle-shaped monosodium urate crystals inside a joint space.

🧠 "Sharp crystals, not soldiers gone rogue." RA (NG-163) is the immune system attacking the synovium; gout is a chemical waste product turning into literal glass-like shards inside the joint. Different mechanism, similar-looking joint pain — don't mix them up on an exam.

⚠️ Causes & risk factors — dietary triggers

High-purine foods and alcohol raise uric acid production or block its excretion:

🍗Meatschicken, steak, liver
🦐Seafoodcrab, lobster, shrimp
🍷Alcoholwine, beer, liquor
⚖️Obesityhigher uric acid load
🩸Diuretics↓ uric acid excretion
🧬Family historygenetic tendency
🧠 "MSA" — Meats · Seafood · Alcohol — the three trigger categories the source graphic leads with. Say it fast: "gout hates MSA."

⚔️ Gout vs Rheumatoid Arthritis — mechanism matters

CLUES

STEP 2 · SPOT THE ATTACK

One joint, sudden and severe, with a lab test that confirms what's already obvious at the bedside.

🦶 Podagra — the classic first attack

KAPLAN A client experiences an acute bout of gouty arthritis. The nurse expects the client's affected foot to have which appearance?
Answer: red — the joint is red, hot, swollen, and exquisitely tender.

🧪 Diagnostics — confirm with labs

HESI Pain and swelling of the right great toe — which test will the nurse expect the provider to order?
Answer: serum uric acid levels.

⏳ Acute vs chronic (tophaceous) gout

🧠 Tophi are visible, palpable proof that uric acid has been too high for too long — untreated chronic gout, not just "another flare."

CARE

STEP 3 · TREAT THE FLARE, PREVENT THE NEXT ONE

Two completely different jobs: calm today's attack, and lower uric acid so there isn't a next one.

⚔️ Big key difference — treat vs prevent

Colchicine / NSAIDsAllopurinol (see NG-188)
Treats the acute attack happening right nowPrevents future attacks — long-term urate-lowering
Anti-inflammatory action, fast reliefXanthine oxidase inhibitor — lowers uric acid production
Not for daily long-term preventionNever start during an active flare — can prolong it
🧠 "Colchicine puts out today's fire; allopurinol stops tomorrow's." For the full allopurinol dosing, the Stevens-Johnson rash warning, and drug interactions, see the Uric Acid Inhibitors page (NG-188) — this page keeps it to the one distinction gout questions actually test.

💊 Acute attack management

  • 💊 NSAIDs or colchicine for pain/inflammation
  • 🧊 Rest and immobilize the joint; ice may help
  • ⬆️ Elevate the affected limb
  • 🚱 Avoid alcohol and high-purine foods during the flare
🧠 A red-hot, exquisitely tender toe is not the time to "walk it off" — rest and immobilize first.

🥗 Prevent Gout — long-term teaching

  • ⚖️ Lose weight — achieve/maintain a healthy weight
  • 🚫 Avoid high-purine foods — meats, alcohol, seafood
  • 💧 Increase fluid intake
  • 📋 Monitor fluid I&O
HESI Which interventions should the nurse implement for a patient with gout? (Select all that apply)
Answer: increase fluid intake; document intake & output; observe for medication side effects.

🚨 Fluids matter for a specific reason

Increasing fluid intake and monitoring I&O isn't just general good advice — as uric acid levels drop (whether from diet change or starting urate-lowering therapy), diluting the urine helps prevent uric acid from crystallizing into kidney stones while levels are shifting.

🧠 "Flush while you lower." Any time uric acid is actively being reduced, hydration protects the kidneys from a second crystal problem — this time in the urinary tract instead of the joint.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: Is gout an autoimmune disease?
No — gout is a crystal arthropathy caused by uric acid crystal deposition. RA (NG-163) is the autoimmune joint disease, driven by the immune system attacking the synovium.
Q2: What is podagra and what does it look like?
Gout affecting the first metatarsophalangeal (big toe) joint — the classic first attack. The foot appears red, hot, swollen, and exquisitely tender.
Q3: What lab confirms gout, and what foods should be avoided?
Serum uric acid level. Avoid high-purine foods: meats (chicken, steak, liver), seafood (crab, lobster, shrimp), and alcohol (wine, beer, liquor).
Q4: What's the difference between colchicine and allopurinol?
Colchicine (with NSAIDs) treats an acute gout attack. Allopurinol is a xanthine oxidase inhibitor used long-term to prevent future attacks — it should not be started during an active flare.

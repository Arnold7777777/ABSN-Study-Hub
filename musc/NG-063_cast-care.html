Cast Care 🦿

Living With a Cast — Watching For Compartment Syndrome

NG-063 Musculoskeletal ADHD-friendly visual edition

A cast protects a healing bone — but a cast can also become the injury if swelling inside it cuts off blood flow. This page teaches the patient (and you) how to live with a cast day to day, and how to run the same neurovascular check as the 6 P's from the Fracture page (NG-056) every single time — because the earliest sign of compartment syndrome is pain that elevation and pain medicine don't touch.

COMPLICATIONS

STEP 1 · WHAT CAN GO WRONG

A cast can hide swelling from view — which is exactly why the assessment matters more than what you can see.

🚨 Compartment syndrome — the emergency this whole page is built around

Compartment syndrome is an extremely painful condition that happens when pressure within the muscle compartments builds to dangerous levels — cutting off blood flow and oxygen and, left untreated, resulting in permanent tissue death.

🧠 "A cast doesn't stretch — but swelling doesn't stop." Once compartment pressure exceeds vessel pressure, the limb can lose blood flow and oxygen even though pulses may still be felt early on — don't wait for pulselessness.

🚨 Key sign #1: Pain

  • 😖 Unrelieved with opioids — even morphine doesn't touch it
  • 📈 Not resolving with medication or elevation
  • 🖐️ Extreme pain with passive movement — pain when someone else stretches the fingers/toes
SAUNDERS Client reports severe, deep, unrelenting pain and pain as the nurse assesses finger movement. What condition?
Answer: compartment syndrome — pain out of proportion to injury, worse with passive stretch.

⚠️ Key sign #2: Paresthesia

  • ⚡ Tingling, burning, numbness
  • 🖐️ Trouble moving or extending fingers/toes — described as "great difficulty"
KAPLAN A nurse is assessing a client with a short leg cast for compartment syndrome. Which finding matches?
Answer: pain that increases with passive movement.

🌡️ Infection — the other cast complication

Watch for a "hot spot" on the cast — a localized area that feels hot, plus a foul odor coming from the cast. Both point to infection underneath, not just normal healing warmth.

KAPLAN Three hours after arriving on the orthopedic unit, a client reports a hot feeling under the cast. First action?
Answer: assess circulation in the casted extremity and change the client's position.
🧠 "Hot + foul smell = infection; cold + pale = compartment syndrome." Two different temperature clues point to two different emergencies — don't mix them up.

ASSESS

STEP 2 · PMSC NEURO CHECK

This is the 6 P's from NG-056, re-packaged as the bedside "PMSC" check you run on every casted limb.

🖐️ PMSC — Pulses · Movement · Sensation · Color/temp

🧠 PMSC is the cast-specific shorthand for the 6 P's (see NG-056 for the full definitions of pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia). Any abnormal finding — even just one — gets reported.

✅ What normal looks like

  • 💓 Pulses present, not pulseless
  • 🖐️ Can move/grip fingers or wiggle toes
  • ⚡ No tingling or numbness
  • ⏱️ Capillary refill under 3 seconds
  • 🎨 Skin color normal, not pale
  • 🌡️ Extremity warm, not cold or cool
🧠 "NOT" is the operative word for every line above — the nurse is ruling OUT compromise, every single check.

🚨 When any of these appear — notify HCP immediately

  • ❌ Pulselessness
  • ❌ Numbness, tingling
  • ❌ Cold, pale foot/hand
  • ❌ Swelling that keeps increasing
  • ❌ Pain unrelieved by elevation or analgesia
SAUNDERS A plaster cast is placed on the lower extremity. Which instructions should be given to the client?
Answer: notify the HCP immediately for numbness, swelling, or if the foot becomes cold and pale; assess capillary refill, temperature, color, and amount of pain.

TEACH

STEP 3 · LIVING WITH THE CAST

Keep it dry, keep it up, keep it empty of objects — and report pain that won't back down.

🩹 Cast care — the daily rules

1
Clean & dry — NEVER wet. Cover the cast with a plastic bag for bathing/showers.
2
Elevate above the heart for the first 48 hours to limit swelling; continue elevating and icing as needed after.
3
Itch under the cast? Use a hair dryer on a cool setting — never insert an object.
4
Take it easy — no bearing weight on a plaster cast, no resting it on hard surfaces or letting fingers indent it while it's still setting.
🧠 "Bag it, prop it, blow it (cool), never poke it." Four rules, one cast — bathing, elevation, itching, and weight-bearing all covered.

❌ Never do this to a cast

🪥NO objects insertedcoat hangers, pencils
💧NO getting it wetplaster especially
🏋️NO weight-bearingon a plaster cast
🪵NO hard surfaceswhile setting
👉NO finger indentationswhile damp
🧠 An itch feels urgent — an infected scratch wound under a cast is worse. Cool hair dryer only.

⏱️ Drying time — plaster needs patience

🧠 Handle a fresh plaster cast with open palms, not fingertips — fingertip pressure while it's still setting leaves indentations that can press on skin once hard.

⚔️ Normal cast pain vs compartment syndrome — tell them apart

Expected post-fracture/cast pain🚨 Compartment syndrome
Improves with elevation, ice, and scheduled analgesiaNot relieved by elevation or analgesia — even opioids
Worse with active movement of the limbWorse with passive stretch — someone else moving the fingers/toes
Gradually improves over daysPain that keeps climbing
Sensation, pulses, color all normalTingling/numbness, cold or pale skin may accompany it
🧠 The single red-flag phrase to remember: pain unrelieved by elevation and analgesia is not "a bad day" — it's compartment syndrome until proven otherwise. Notify the HCP immediately.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What is the earliest, most reliable red flag for compartment syndrome?
Pain that is unrelieved by elevation and analgesia, especially pain with passive stretch of the fingers/toes.
Q2: What does PMSC stand for, and why is it done on a casted limb?
Pulses, Movement, Sensation, Color/temperature — the bedside version of the 6 P's used to catch neurovascular compromise early.
Q3: How should a patient shower with a plaster cast?
Cover the cast completely with a plastic bag — plaster casts must stay clean and dry, never wet.
Q4: The patient's cast itches. What's the safe teaching?
Use a hair dryer on a cool setting blown into the cast — never insert an object like a coat hanger or pencil.

WHAT IT DOES

STEP 1 · MECHANISM

Two very different mechanisms share one drug class name — knowing which one tells you the whole safety profile.

🧠🦵 Centrally-acting vs peripherally-acting — where the drug actually works

EXAM TIP Most skeletal muscle relaxants do not directly relax the muscle fiber itself — their benefit comes from a sedative effect on the CNS that blunts the perception of spasm and pain. Only one major drug works differently: dantrolene acts directly on the muscle.

🧠 "Central = calm the brain, Peripheral = plug the calcium." If a question says a drug works directly on the muscle, it's describing dantrolene — everything else in this class is working upstream, in the CNS.

🎯 Indication #1 — Acute muscle spasm

  • 🏋️ Muscle strain
  • 🔙 Acute back pain
  • 🩹 Other acute painful musculoskeletal conditions

Drugs used here are almost always centrally-acting and meant for short-term use.

🧠 Spasm = short trip. Acute spasm drugs are a short course, not a lifestyle.

🎯 Indication #2 — Chronic spasticity

  • 🧩 Multiple sclerosis (MS)
  • 🧠 Cerebral palsy
  • 🦴 Spinal cord injury

These clients often need longer-term therapy — baclofen and dantrolene are the classic choices here.

🧠 Spasticity = a standing order. Neurologic spasticity is chronic — that's why abrupt stop of these drugs (baclofen especially) is so dangerous.

🌀 Why relaxants help either way — the spasm-pain-guarding cycle

💥 Injury or CNS lesion → muscle spasm
▼
😣 Spasm → pain
▼
🛡️ Pain → guarding / more tension
▼
🔁 More tension → more spasm (cycle repeats)

A muscle relaxant (central sedation or dantrolene's direct action) breaks the cycle at the spasm step, so pain and guarding can settle too.

🧠 Break one link, break the loop. You don't have to fix all three (spasm/pain/guarding) — interrupting spasm collapses the whole cycle.

WATCH FOR

STEP 2 · SAFETY

One adverse effect towers over the rest — sedation — plus the interactions and contraindications that show up on exams.

🚨 #1 universal risk: CNS depression → fall risk

Every drug in this class can cause drowsiness and sedation. This is the single highest-yield safety point for the whole class — before you memorize one individual drug, know this.

🧠 "Sleepy stacks." Any CNS depressant on board — alcohol, opioids, antihistamines, sedatives/hypnotics — stacks with a muscle relaxant. Fall precautions and caution with driving/hazardous tasks apply to every drug in this class.

🧪 Other adverse effects to know

  • 😴 Drowsiness, sedation, lethargy
  • 🚽 Constipation or diarrhea
  • 💓 Bradycardia or tachycardia
  • 🌡️ Rash
🧠 GI & cardiac effects can go either direction (constipation or diarrhea; brady or tachycardia) — don't assume only one.

💥 Drug interactions

  • CNS depressants (alcohol, antihistamines, opioids, sedatives) → increased CNS depression
  • MAOIs → risk of high fever & convulsions
  • Haloperidol → increased psychosis
  • Antihypertensives → increased risk of hypotension
🧠 "MAOI = fire & seizure." A muscle relaxant plus an MAOI (or within 14 days of one) is a red-flag combination — think hyperpyrexia + convulsions.

❌ Class contraindications — drug-specific, high-yield

DrugContraindicated in…
BaclofenMuscle spasm caused by rheumatic disorders (not a spasticity/neurologic cause)
CarisoprodolKnown hypersensitivity to meprobamate (its active metabolite)
CyclobenzaprineRecent MI, cardiac conduction disorders, hyperthyroidism; within 14 days of an MAOI
Oral dantroleneLactation; active hepatic disease; muscle spasm from rheumatic disorders
🧠 "Rheumatic disorders" keeps showing up — both baclofen and oral dantrolene are contraindicated when the spasm is from a rheumatic cause, because these drugs are built for neurologic/musculoskeletal spasm, not joint disease. TEACH

STEP 3 · PATIENT EDUCATION

Four teaching points cover almost every question this class generates.

✅ Safety while sedated

1
😴 May cause drowsiness — expect it
2
🚗 Do not drive or do hazardous tasks if drowsy
3
🍷 Avoid alcohol / other CNS depressants
🧠 "Limit" is always a trap. If an answer choice says to limit alcohol to a few drinks while on a muscle relaxant, it's wrong — the teaching is avoid, not limit.

⏱️ Duration of use

🧠 "2–3 and done." For acute spasm, if a client is still on the same relaxant past 2–3 weeks with no reassessment, flag it.

🛑 Never abruptly stop — the class-wide taper rule

This applies to any muscle relaxant taken regularly, but it is the single highest-yield point for baclofen: stopping suddenly can trigger rebound spasticity, hallucinations, or seizures. Always taper under provider guidance.

✅ Taper the dose gradually, per provider order
❌ Never stop cold — even if the client "feels fine"
🧠 Orthostatic dizziness on position change is expected, not a reason to hold the dose or stop
🧠 "Never abruptly stop (any muscle relaxant)." The full withdrawal picture for baclofen — and the memory hook "back off slowly" — lives on NG-156.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: How do most centrally-acting muscle relaxants relieve spasm?
Mostly through a sedative effect on the CNS that blunts perception of spasm/pain — they usually don't act directly on the muscle fiber. Dantrolene is the exception; it acts directly on skeletal muscle.
Q2: What is the #1 safety concern shared by every drug in this class?
CNS depression → drowsiness/sedation → fall risk. Caution with driving/hazardous tasks and avoid other CNS depressants (alcohol, opioids, antihistamines, sedatives).
Q3: A client on a muscle relaxant for 4 weeks says they just want to stop taking it today. Best response?
Do not stop abruptly — taper under provider guidance. Sudden discontinuation risks rebound spasticity, seizures, or hallucinations, especially with baclofen.
Q4: Baclofen is contraindicated for muscle spasm caused by which type of condition?
Rheumatic disorders — baclofen (and oral dantrolene) are contraindicated for spasm from rheumatic causes; they're built for neurologic/musculoskeletal spasticity.

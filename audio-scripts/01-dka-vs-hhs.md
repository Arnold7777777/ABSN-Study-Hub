# DKA versus HHS — the one sign that separates them

**For NotebookLM:** make a short audio overview, about two to three minutes,
two speakers, conversational. Do not add material beyond this source. Do not
read out headings.

---

Two clients arrive with a blood sugar in the hundreds and both look
dehydrated. One has diabetic ketoacidosis. One has hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic
state. Telling them apart is worth a mark on every endocrine exam, and it
changes what you do first.

Start with the insulin, because everything follows from it.

In DKA there is essentially **no insulin at all**. This is usually type one.
With no insulin, the cells cannot take glucose in, so the body decides it is
starving and starts burning fat. Burning fat makes **ketones**, and ketones are
acids. So the client becomes acidotic.

In HHS there is **a little insulin** — enough to stop fat being broken down,
so no ketones and no acidosis, but nowhere near enough to control the sugar.
This is usually type two. Because nothing dramatic happens early, it builds
slowly over days, and the sugar climbs much higher. Often above six hundred.

Now, the single finding that separates them.

**Kussmaul respirations.** Deep, rapid, sighing breaths, sometimes with a
fruity smell on the breath. Those breaths are the lungs trying to blow off
acid. They belong to DKA, because DKA is the one with the acid. If there is no
acidosis, there is nothing to blow off, so a client in HHS does not breathe
that way.

Say that back to yourself. Kussmaul means acid. Acid means ketones. Ketones
mean DKA.

What dominates the picture is different too. In DKA it is the acidosis — the
breathing, the abdominal pain, the vomiting. In HHS it is the **dehydration**,
and because the client is so profoundly dry, what you see is neurological.
Confusion. Drowsiness. Sometimes seizures or coma. HHS clients are often
sicker on arrival, precisely because it crept up on them for days.

Now the part people get wrong under pressure. What do you give first?

Not insulin. **Fluid.** Normal saline, and a lot of it. Both of these clients
have been passing enormous volumes of urine for hours or days, dragging water
out with the glucose. They are empty. Fluid alone starts bringing the sugar
down, and it protects perfusion to the kidneys and the brain.

Insulin comes second, and there is a condition attached: **check the potassium
first.** Here is why that matters. In acidosis, potassium moves out of the
cells into the blood, so the serum potassium often looks normal or even high —
while the body as a whole is actually depleted. The moment you give insulin,
potassium is driven back into the cells, and the serum level can fall off a
cliff. Low enough to stop the heart.

So the rule is: if the potassium is low, you replace it before you start the
insulin. Not alongside. Before.

One more. When the glucose comes down to around two hundred and fifty, you do
not stop the insulin — you **add dextrose** to the fluids and keep the insulin
running. The insulin is not there only to lower sugar. It is there to switch
off ketone production, and that job is not finished just because the number
looks better.

---

**The thirty-second version.**

DKA — no insulin, type one, ketones, acidosis, Kussmaul breathing, comes on
fast.

HHS — some insulin, type two, no ketones, sugar higher, dehydration and
confusion, comes on slowly.

Fluid first for both. Insulin second, and only once you know the potassium.
Add dextrose at two fifty and keep the insulin going.

Kussmaul means acid. Acid means DKA.

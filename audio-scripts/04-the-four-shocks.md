# The four shocks — warm or cold tells you which

**For NotebookLM:** make a short audio overview, about two to three minutes,
two speakers, conversational. Do not add material beyond this source. Do not
read out headings.

---

Shock is not one thing. It is four problems that all end the same way — the
tissues stop getting oxygen — and the treatment for one can kill in another.
So the first job at the bedside is working out which one you are looking at.

Here is the frame that makes it simple. Think of the circulation as a pump, a
tank of fluid, and the pipes. Something is wrong with one of those three.

**Hypovolaemic shock.** The tank is empty. Haemorrhage, burns, vomiting,
diarrhoea. The pump and pipes are fine, there is just not enough in the system.

**Cardiogenic shock.** The pump has failed. A big myocardial infarction is the
classic cause. Plenty of fluid, but nothing is moving it.

**Distributive shock.** The pipes have dilated. The volume has not changed, but
the container suddenly got much bigger, so the pressure falls. Sepsis is the
common one. Anaphylaxis and neurogenic shock are also distributive.

**Obstructive shock.** Something is physically blocking flow. A tension
pneumothorax, a cardiac tamponade, a massive pulmonary embolism.

Now, the finding that sorts them at a glance.

**Warm or cold.**

In hypovolaemic, cardiogenic and obstructive shock the body clamps down — it
squeezes the peripheral vessels to protect the brain and the heart. So the skin
is **cold, clammy and pale**, and capillary refill is slow. That is the picture
of shock most people carry in their head.

Early distributive shock is the opposite. The vessels are dilated, so blood is
sitting out at the surface. The skin is **warm and flushed**, and the pulse
pressure is wide. Add a fever and that is early sepsis.

This trips people up because a warm, pink, feverish client does not look like
they are in shock. They are. And distributive shock turns cold later, once
compensation fails — so warm skin tells you it is distributive **and** that you
are early enough to do something about it.

Now the first hour of septic shock, because it is heavily tested. The bundle
is: blood **cultures**, a **lactate**, broad-spectrum **antibiotics**, and a
thirty millilitres per kilogram crystalloid **fluid** bolus.

Cultures go before antibiotics, but only because drawing them takes moments.
They are never a reason to delay the drug — the mortality clock is running.

And fluid comes **before** vasopressors. Every time. Squeezing an empty tank
does not perfuse anything. Vasopressors are for the client who is still
hypotensive after they are filled.

One number to keep. A **mean arterial pressure below sixty-five** will not
perfuse the kidneys. Watch it alongside the urine output and the lactate —
those three all say the same thing in different languages, and a **rising
lactate** is the earliest honest sign that what you are doing is not working.

---

**The thirty-second version.**

Four shocks. Empty tank, failed pump, dilated pipes, physical blockage.

Cold and clammy — hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, obstructive.

Warm and flushed with a wide pulse pressure — distributive, and probably early
sepsis.

Sepsis first hour: cultures, lactate, antibiotics, fluid. Fluid before
pressors, always.

MAP under sixty-five is not perfusing the kidneys. A rising lactate means it
is not working.

# Fetal heart rate decelerations — VEAL CHOP, and what to do first

**For NotebookLM:** make a short audio overview, about two to three minutes,
two speakers, conversational. Do not add material beyond this source. Do not
read out headings.

---

A fetal monitor strip is one of the few things in nursing school with a
memory hook so good that it has survived decades. Let us use it, and then go
past it, because the hook tells you the cause and the exam wants the action.

**VEAL CHOP.** Two columns. V, E, A, L on the left. C, H, O, P on the right.

**V is for Variable decelerations, and C is for Cord compression.** Variables
are abrupt — they drop sharply and come back sharply, and they can happen any
time, with a contraction or between them. The cord is being squeezed.

**E is for Early decelerations, and H is for Head compression.** Earlies mirror
the contraction. They start when it starts, dip at the peak, and recover as it
ends. The baby's head is being pressed against the cervix. **These are benign.**
Nothing needs doing. That is the one people over-react to.

**A is for Accelerations, and O is for Okay.** Accelerations are a well
oxygenated baby. They are the finding you want.

**L is for Late decelerations, and P is for Placental insufficiency.** This is
the dangerous one. Lates begin **after** the contraction starts, reach their
lowest point after the peak, and do not come back to baseline until after the
contraction is over. They are shallow and they look unimpressive, which is
exactly why they get missed. What they mean is that the placenta cannot deliver
enough oxygen while the uterus is squeezing.

So — the shape tells you when it happens relative to the contraction. Early is
in step. Late is lagging behind.

Now the action, because this is what gets asked.

Late decelerations, or repetitive variables, and you go through **intrauterine
resuscitation** in this order:

**Reposition first.** Turn her onto her side. It is instant, it costs nothing,
and it takes the weight of the uterus off the vena cava so more blood reaches
the placenta. It is the first thing precisely because it is the fastest thing.

**Stop the oxytocin** if it is running. Contractions are the problem, so stop
making them.

**Give an IV fluid bolus.** More volume, more placental perfusion.

**Give oxygen** by non-rebreather mask.

**And notify the provider.**

Reposition, stop the pitocin, fluids, oxygen, notify. That sequence is worth
memorising in order, because the exam will offer you all five and ask which
comes **first**.

One last thing that matters more than any deceleration. **Moderate variability**
— the normal jitter in the baseline, six to twenty-five beats — is the single
best sign that the fetus is well oxygenated. A strip with moderate variability
is reassuring even with some decelerations on it. A strip with **absent**
variability is worrying even if it looks flat and peaceful. Flat is not calm.
Flat is bad.

---

**The thirty-second version.**

Variable is cord. Early is head, and it is fine. Accelerations are okay. Late
is placental, and it is the dangerous one.

Early mirrors the contraction. Late lags behind it.

For lates: reposition, stop the oxytocin, fluids, oxygen, notify. Reposition
first.

And moderate variability beats everything. Flat is not calm — flat is bad.

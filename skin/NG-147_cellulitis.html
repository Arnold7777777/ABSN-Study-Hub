Cellulitis 🦠

Deep bacterial skin infection — spot it, mark it, escalate it

NG-147 INTEGUMENTARY · INFECTION ADHD-friendly visual edition

Cellulitis is an acute bacterial infection of the deep dermis and subcutaneous tissue, usually Staph aureus or Group A Strep, that gets in through a break in the skin — most often on a lower leg. Because it is deep, the redness has no crisp border; it spreads. The exam answer is almost always the same three moves: mark and date the margin, elevate the limb, give the antibiotic on time — and know the escalation signs that turn a skin problem into a sepsis problem.

🦠 Deep bacterial infectionStaph aureus or Strep pyogenes in the deep dermis + subcutaneous fat. Deep = the border is fuzzy.
🔥 Red · Hot · Swollen · TenderUsually ONE lower leg. Poorly-defined spreading erythema — not a sharp-edged patch.
🖊️ Mark the marginOutline the redness, write the date & time on the skin. That single line tells the next nurse if it is winning or losing.
💊 Bacteria → antibioticsPlus elevate the limb, treat pain, and find the portal of entry (often athlete's foot).

PATHO & CAUSES

STEP 1 · WHY IT HAPPENS

A break in the barrier + bacteria on the skin + a host who cannot fight back = cellulitis.

🧠 The one-sentence definition

Cellulitis is an acute bacterial infection of the deep dermis and subcutaneous tissue, most often caused by Staphylococcus aureus or Group A Streptococcus, classically in a lower limb (over the shin/tibia).

🧠 "CELLulitis = the CELLar." It lives in the basement of the skin — the deep layers — not the attic.

⚙️ Mechanism, step by step

Break in the skin barrier
▼
Staph / Strep get into the deep dermis
▼
Bacterial enzymes + toxins spread through tissue planes
▼
Inflammation: vessels dilate & leak
▼
Redness · warmth · swelling · pain — spreading outward
▼
Untreated → abscess · lymphangitis · bacteremia
🧠 Strep makes enzymes that dissolve tissue planes, which is exactly why the redness spreads instead of staying in a neat circle.

🚪 Portals of entry — always look for the front door

🧠 "No door, no cellulitis." If you cannot find a break, look harder — between the toes, under a dressing, in a skin fold, at an old IV site.

⭐ Risk factors — who gets it

  • Trauma / any wound — the doorway
  • Diabetes — poor perfusion, neuropathy, impaired white cells
  • Tinea pedis (athlete's foot) — cracks between the toes
  • Lymphedema — stagnant lymph cannot clear bacteria
  • Venous insufficiency — sluggish flow, fragile skin
  • Obesity — moist skin folds, poorer perfusion
  • Previous cellulitis — damaged lymphatics recur
  • Immunosuppression — steroids, chemo, HIV
🧠 "Damp, damaged, diabetic." Wet skin, broken skin, and bad sugar control — the three setups.

🔁 Why it keeps coming back

Each episode scars the lymphatics. Damaged lymphatics → more swelling → less clearance of bacteria → the next episode is easier to get.

Breaking that cycle is nursing work: treat the athlete's foot, moisturise dry cracked skin, manage edema (compression once infection is controlled and arterial flow is adequate), and control blood glucose.

🧠 Vicious circle: swelling → infection → scarring → more swelling.

🧪 Bug → drug: the rule that runs this whole set

🧠 Name the organism and the drug class names itself. Bacteria → antibiotic. Fungus → "-azole"/terbinafine. Virus → "-cyclovir". Autoimmune → calm the immune system down.

CLUES

STEP 2 · WHAT YOU SEE

Think infection: the four cardinal signs plus a body that is starting to react systemically.

🔥 The classic picture

  • Erythema — red, with a poorly-defined spreading border
  • Warmth — compare with the other limb using the back of your hand
  • Edema — tight, shiny, sometimes peau d'orange dimpling
  • Pain / tenderness — and it hurts more than it looks like it should
  • Fever, malaise, sometimes chills
🧠 "RHOP" — Red, Hot, Oedematous, Painful. If all four are on ONE limb, think cellulitis.

🚨 Escalation signs — report these NOW

  • Red streaks running up the limb (lymphangitis) + tender nodes
  • Fever > 38 °C / 100.4 °F, rigors, tachycardia, hypotension, confusion
  • Fluctuance — a soft, boggy pocket = abscess, needs drainage
  • Pain out of proportion, dusky/purple skin, bullae, crepitus, rapid progression
  • Redness clearly past the marked line despite 48 h of antibiotics

Never assume "it just needs more time" when the redness is racing past the line and the patient is febrile.

🧠 "Streaks + spikes = speak up." Red streaks or a fever spike means escalate.

📈 How bad is this? The escalation ladder

🧠 Pain out of proportion is the necrotising fasciitis flag. The skin can look almost normal while the patient is in agony — that mismatch is the whole point.

⭐ Deep vs superficial — cellulitis vs erysipelas

🧠 "Erysipelas = Edge you can feel." Both start with E. Cellulitis fades; erysipelas has a step you can run your finger over.

🧪 Labs & work-up — what to expect

  • WBC ↑ with a left shift (normal adult 4.5–11.0 × 10⁹/L)
  • CRP / ESR ↑ — inflammation markers, useful for trending
  • Blood cultures if febrile/systemically unwell — before the first antibiotic dose
  • Wound or abscess culture if there is drainable pus
  • Blood glucose / HbA1c — undiagnosed diabetes shows up this way
  • Imaging (ultrasound) if abscess or DVT is suspected
🧠 Cultures before antibiotics — once the drug is in, the culture may grow nothing and you lose the answer.

📋 Assessment you document every shift

  • Measure the erythema (cm) and compare with the marked outline
  • Limb circumference at a marked point — same spot, same tape, every time
  • Temperature, pulse, BP, respiratory rate, mental status
  • Pain score — a rising score with a shrinking rash is a red flag
  • Distal pulses, capillary refill, sensation — is perfusion intact?
  • Photograph per facility policy (with consent) alongside the marked line
🧠 Same spot, same tape, same time. Trends only mean something if you measure the same way twice.
🩺

CARE

STEP 3 · WHAT YOU DO

Kill the bacteria, drain the pus if there is any, drop the swelling, and prove it is getting better.

🖊️ THE nursing intervention: mark and date the margin

🧠 "Draw it, date it, watch it." If the exam offers "outline the border of the erythema with a skin marker and note the date/time," that is almost always the answer.

💊 Antibiotics — bacteria means antibiotics

Choice is prescriber-driven and depends on severity and local resistance patterns, but the families you should recognize:

  • Penicillins — e.g. dicloxacillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate
  • Cephalosporins — e.g. cephalexin (oral), cefazolin (IV)
  • Clindamycin — an option in penicillin allergy
  • If MRSA is suspected or there is purulence: doxycycline, TMP-SMX, or IV vancomycin

Nursing: ask about allergies first, give doses on time to keep blood levels steady, and teach finish the whole course.

🧠 Purulent = think MRSA. Non-purulent = think Strep. Pus points to staph.

⬆️ Elevate the limb — free and effective

Raise the affected limb above the level of the heart whenever the patient is resting. Gravity drains the edema, which lowers pressure, improves perfusion and speeds antibiotic delivery to the tissue.

Pair it with a bed cradle or pillows so the linen is not pressing on tender skin.

🧠 "Toes above the nose" is the memorable version — heart level is the real target.

✅ The rest of the bundle

  • Analgesia before dressing changes and repositioning
  • Cool compresses for comfort — never ice directly on the skin
  • Hydration and antipyretics for fever
  • Skin care: moisturise the surrounding skin, treat any tinea pedis
  • Glucose control in diabetes — hyperglycemia stalls healing
  • Mobility when able, plus VTE prevention for the swollen immobile limb
  • Hand hygiene and standard precautions; contact precautions if there is draining pus per facility policy
🧠 ELEVATE: Elevate · Labs · Erythema marked · Vitals · Antibiotics on time · Treat pain · Educate.

❌ Never do these

  • Never massage or vigorously rub a cellulitic limb — you push bacteria along tissue planes.
  • Never apply a hot pack to numb, neuropathic or poorly-perfused skin — burns are silent.
  • Never squeeze or "pop" an abscess at the bedside — drainage is a provider procedure.
  • Never stop antibiotics because the redness improved — relapse and resistance follow.
  • Never apply tight compression before infection and arterial flow have been assessed.
🧠 Don't rub, don't pop, don't stop.

🗣️ Patient teaching — what they take home

  • Finish every dose, even after it looks better
  • Elevate the limb at home; keep moving to prevent clots
  • Come back for: fever, red streaks, spreading past the line, new pus, worsening pain, vomiting the antibiotic
  • Wash and cover every cut; keep skin moisturised so it does not crack
  • Dry between the toes and treat athlete's foot — that is the recurrence prevention step
  • Diabetes: check your feet daily, including between the toes and with a mirror
🧠 "Finish · Elevate · Return." Three sentences the patient can repeat back to you.

TELL THEM APART

STEP 4 · THE REAL EXAM SKILL

Five skin conditions, five patterns. Distribution and appearance narrow it before you read a single lab value.

🗺️ Where each one shows up

🧠 One leg = cellulitis. One band = shingles. Around the mouth = impetigo. Rings = tinea. Elbows & knees with silver = psoriasis.

🧠 "Both legs itch = it is not an infection." Bilateral, itchy, chronic → stasis dermatitis, and it gets misdiagnosed as cellulitis constantly.

🧠 One-line contrasts to memorize

  • Cellulitis vs erysipelas — fuzzy border vs sharp raised border
  • Cellulitis vs abscess — diffuse firmness vs a fluctuant pocket (needs drainage)
  • Cellulitis vs necrotising fasciitis — pain that fits vs pain out of all proportion
  • Cellulitis vs contact dermatitis — painful + febrile vs itchy with a shape that matches an exposure
  • Cellulitis vs gout — spreading limb redness vs an exquisitely tender single joint (classically the great toe)
🧠 Pain says infection. Itch says inflammation or allergy. That single question sorts most red-skin stems.

⭐ NCLEX traps

  • The rash spread past the line → notify the provider, do not simply re-mark it and wait.
  • Blood cultures come before the first antibiotic dose, not after.
  • "Cellulitis of both lower legs" in an afebrile, itchy patient → question the diagnosis.
  • Elevation is the answer more often than students expect.
  • Recurrent cellulitis → the missed intervention is usually treating tinea pedis.
  • Cellulitis itself is not spread person-to-person like impetigo — but draining pus is infectious material.
🧠 If an option says "mark and date the border," read it twice — it is usually right.

🎯 Cover & check — 8 rapid-fire questions
Q1: Which skin layers does cellulitis involve, and why does that matter for what you see?
The deep dermis and subcutaneous tissue. Because it is deep, the erythema has a poorly-defined, fading border rather than a sharp edge.
Q2: Name the two usual organisms.
Staphylococcus aureus and Group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes).
Q3: What is the classic bedside nursing intervention for tracking progress?
Outline the border of the erythema with a skin marker and write the date and time on the skin, then compare each shift. Redness beyond the line = spreading.
Q4: A patient on oral antibiotics for 24 hours now has red streaks traveling up the thigh and a temperature of 38.9 °C. What do you do?
Notify the provider immediately. Red streaking is lymphangitis with systemic infection — expect blood cultures, IV antibiotics, and a sepsis screen. Do not wait for the next scheduled round.
Q5: Why must you look between the toes in recurrent cellulitis?
Tinea pedis creates macerated cracks that act as the portal of entry. If the athlete's foot is not treated with an antifungal, the cellulitis keeps coming back.
Q6: How do you position the affected limb, and why?
Elevated above the level of the heart. Gravity drains edema, which lowers tissue pressure, improves perfusion and helps antibiotics reach the tissue.
Q7: Which finding suggests an abscess rather than simple cellulitis, and what does it change?
A fluctuant, boggy pocket. Antibiotics alone are not enough — it needs incision and drainage by the provider.
Q8: A patient has severe pain, dusky purple skin, bullae and crepitus, and is deteriorating quickly. What are you thinking?
Necrotising fasciitis — a surgical emergency. Pain out of proportion to the appearance is the hallmark. Rapid response, urgent surgical review, IV antibiotics and fluid resuscitation.

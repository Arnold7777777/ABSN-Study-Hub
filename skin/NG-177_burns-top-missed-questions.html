HOW TO ATTACK A BURN QUESTION

STEP 0 · THE SYSTEM

Four questions, asked in the same order every time. They eliminate most distractors before you even read them.

🧭 The four-question filter

🧠 “Phase · Airway · Number · Urine.” Four words. Run them in order on every burn stem before you look at the options.

🧾 The numbers you must have memorised cold

  • Head/neck 9% · each arm 9% · anterior trunk 18% · posterior trunk 18% · each leg 18% · perineum 1%
  • Patient's palm plus fingers ≈ 1% (the palm alone is often taught as ≈0.5%)
  • Parkland: 4 mL × kg × %TBSA, half in 8 h, half in 16 h, clock from injury
  • Targets: urine ≥30 mL/hr · systolic ≥90 · HR <120
  • Peak interstitial shift 18–24 h · diuresis 48–72 h
  • Emergent labs: K⁺ >5.0 · Na⁺ <135 · Hgb and Hct both elevated (typical adult reference bands ≈ Hgb 12–18 g/dL, Hct 36–54% — ranges vary by lab and by sex)
🧠 If a number in an option does not match one of these, it is probably the distractor.

⭐ Read the stem for these five words

  • ANTERIOR / POSTERIOR → halve the limb values
  • ENTIRE / CIRCUMFERENTIAL → use the whole-region value (and think escharotomy)
  • PERINEUM → they want the extra 1%
  • HOURS AFTER THE INJURY → the resuscitation clock, and which phase you are in
  • FULL-THICKNESS / PARTIAL-THICKNESS → both count in TBSA; a superficial (1°) area does not
🧠 Physically underline those words. On a real exam, mouth them. The stem is telling you the answer.

TBSA CALCULATION TRAPS

RULE OF NINES

Two classic items. Both are missed for the same reason: whole-limb values used for a single surface.

🎓 Question 1 — full-thickness burns to ALL posterior body surfaces

A client has full-thickness burns to all posterior body surfaces. Using the Rule of Nines, calculate the percentage of total body surface area affected.
❌ 36% — This counts only the back of the trunk (18%) plus the back of both legs (18%) and forgets the head and the arms entirely.
❌ 45% — A tempting "round number." It drops the posterior head and neck (4.5%).
✅ 49.5% — Correct. Back of head & neck 4.5 + entire back 18 + posterior arms 4.5 × 2 = 9 + posterior legs 9 × 2 = 18.
❌ 76.5% — The classic error: whole arms (9% each) and whole legs (18% each) were used instead of halving for the posterior surface only.
🧠 49.5% = "just under half." If your answer to an all-posterior burn is over 50%, you forgot to halve the limbs.

→ Rule of Nines in full on NG-146

🎓 Question 2 — anterior legs & perineum

A client has partial-thickness burns to the anterior legs and the perineum. Using the Rule of Nines, calculate the percentage of total body surface area affected.
❌ 9% — Only one leg was counted. The stem says legs, plural.
❌ 18% — The two anterior legs are right, but the perineum's 1% was dropped. If the stem names the perineum, the 1% is the point of the question.
✅ 19% — Correct. Anterior right leg 9 + anterior left leg 9 + perineum 1 = 19% TBSA.
❌ 37% — Whole legs (18% each) were used instead of the anterior surfaces only, plus the 1%.
🧠 “Eighteen and the little one.” Both anterior legs plus the perineum = 19%.

PARKLAND CALCULATION TRAPS

4 × kg × %TBSA

The multiplication is easy. The marks are lost on the split, the units and the clock.

🎓 Question 3 — 100 kg client with 19% TBSA

The client above weighs 100 kg with 19% TBSA. Calculate the Lactated Ringer's fluid resuscitation needed in the first 24 hours.
❌ 1,900 mL — Someone used 1 mL instead of 4 mL. The constant is 4.
❌ 3,800 mL — That is only half the total, i.e. the volume for the first 8 hours. The question asked for the whole 24 hours.
✅ 7,600 mL — Correct. 4 mL × 100 kg × 19 = 7,600 mL of LR in the first 24 hours.
❌ 15,200 mL — The total was doubled instead of halved. Halving applies to the timing, not to the total.
4 mL × 100 kg × 19 %TBSA = 7,600 mL Lactated Ringer's in the first 24 hours ½ = 3,800 mL in the first 8 h → 475 mL/hr ½ = 3,800 mL over the next 16 h → 237.5 mL/hr
🧠 “Four, weight, burn — then halve the CLOCK, not the answer.”

🎓 Question 4 — the delayed-arrival variant

A 70 kg client sustained burns to 36% TBSA at 06:00 and arrives in the emergency department at 09:00. The Parkland formula is prescribed. How much LR should be infused by 14:00?
❌ 10,080 mL — That is the entire 24-hour total, not the first-8-hour portion.
✅ 5,040 mL — Correct. Total = 4 × 70 × 36 = 10,080 mL. Half = 5,040 mL must be in by 8 hours after the injury, i.e. by 14:00.
❌ 5,040 mL by 17:00 — This restarted the clock at arrival. The 8 hours run from 06:00, so the deadline is 14:00, not 17:00.
❌ 2,520 mL — The total was halved twice.
4 × 70 kg × 36 % = 10,080 mL total half = 5,040 mL due by 8 h post-injury (14:00) 3 h already gone → 5,040 ÷ 5 remaining hours ≈ 1,008 mL/hr until 14:00, then 5,040 mL over 16 h = 315 mL/hr
🧠 “What time did it happen?” Ask it before you touch the calculator.

TITRATION, TIMING & LAB TRAPS

JUDGEMENT ITEMS

These three are the most-missed non-calculation burn questions in the bank.

🎓 Question 5 — the client is not responding to the fluids

A client is undergoing fluid replacement after being burned on 20% of her body 12 hours ago. Assessment: BP 90/50, pulse 110, urine output 20 mL over the past hour. The nurse anticipates which prescription?
✅ Increasing the IV Lactated Ringer's — Correct. Urine under 30 mL/hr with a borderline BP and a rising pulse means the patient is under-resuscitated. The fix for hypovolemia is more isotonic fluid.
❌ Administering a diuretic (e.g. furosemide) — The most attractive wrong answer. It "treats" the low urine number while emptying a tank that is already empty, and will worsen shock and kidney injury. Low urine output in burn shock is never treated with a diuretic.
❌ Decreasing the IV rate — Backwards. Nothing in the data suggests overload; there are no crackles, no JVD, no dyspnoea.
❌ Transfusing packed red blood cells — Blood is not first-line burn resuscitation. There is no bleeding and no haemoglobin value here; in the first 24 hours the H&H is usually falsely high from hemoconcentration.
🧠 “Low urine in a burn = more fluid, never a diuretic.” The kidney is not lazy; it is dry.

→ Titration in full on NG-123

🎓 Question 6 — when is the third-spacing worst?

A client has an extensive burn injury involving 45% of total body surface area. When planning for fluid resuscitation, the nurse should consider that fluid shifting to the interstitial spaces is greatest during which time period?
❌ During the first 2 hours after the injury — Leaking has started, but it has not peaked. Most of the shift is still ahead of you.
❌ Between 6 and 12 hours after the injury — Still climbing. Tempting because this is when the first (faster) half of the fluid is running.
✅ Between 18 and 24 hours after the injury — Correct. Capillary permeability peaks here; this is when interstitial shifting — and the oedema — is greatest.
❌ Between 48 and 72 hours after the injury — This is the opposite: capillaries are sealing and fluid is coming back into the vessels. This is the diuresis window, when you start watching for fluid overload instead.
🧠 “Out by 24, back by 72.” Peak leak 18–24 h; reabsorption/diuresis 48–72 h.

🎓 Question 7 — which lab would you expect?

A client has a severe burn injury covering 35% of the total body surface area. The nurse is most likely to note which finding on the laboratory report?
✅ Haematocrit 60% (0.60) — Correct. Plasma has leaked out of the vessels while red cells stayed behind, so the Hct is falsely elevated — hemoconcentration. A typical adult reference band is about 36–54% (varies by lab and sex).
❌ Haematocrit 28% — A low Hct fits the acute phase (hemodilution once fluid returns, plus red-cell destruction) — not the first 24 hours.
❌ Potassium 3.0 mEq/L — Backwards for the emergent phase. Burned cells rupture and dump potassium, so early K⁺ is high (>5.0). Low potassium belongs to the acute/diuretic phase.
❌ Sodium 148 mEq/L — Also backwards. Sodium follows water into the interstitium and is lost through the wound, so serum Na⁺ tends to be low (<135).
🧠 “Fluids flow — electrolytes go.” Water leaving the vessels concentrates the blood and floods it with potassium.

INFECTION & REHAB TRAPS

TEACHING & SAFETY

These feel like "common sense" questions, which is exactly why they are missed.

🎓 Question 8 — the immunocompromised burn client

A client with burns is immunocompromised. What precaution should be taken to prevent infection?
✅ Avoid placing fresh flowers or plants in or near the client's room — Correct. Standing vase water harbours Pseudomonas and potting soil harbours Aspergillus and other fungi. Both are dangerous to a patient with no skin barrier and a suppressed immune system.
❌ Place the client in a semi-private room — The opposite. A private room with protective isolation is indicated; a roommate is an infection source.
❌ Restrict all fluids to reduce wound drainage — Dangerous and unrelated. Burn patients need more fluid, not less, and drainage is not controlled by dehydration.
❌ Administer prophylactic systemic antibiotics to all burn clients — Not routine. Broad prophylaxis selects for resistant organisms; antibiotics treat documented infection. Topical antimicrobials are used on the wound as prescribed.
✅ Hand hygiene before & after ✅ Private room ✅ Gown, gloves, mask, cap ✅ Sterile dressing technique 🚫 Fresh flowers 🚫 Potted plants 🚫 Sick visitors 🚫 Routine prophylactic antibiotics
🧠 “No flowers for the burn patient.” It feels unkind — which is exactly why it is the right answer.

→ Infection prevention in full on NG-123

🎓 Question 9 — rehabilitation-phase teaching

A client is entering the rehabilitation phase after a major burn. Which statement indicates that teaching has been effective?
✅ "I'll use a water-based lotion several times a day, wear my pressure garment as prescribed, and do my exercises every day." — Correct. That is the whole W-W-E teaching plan: Water-based lotion · Wear pressure garments · Exercise daily (range of motion).
❌ "I'll take the pressure garment off whenever it feels tight or hot." — Wrong. It is supposed to be snug; it is commonly worn up to 23 hours a day for many months. Report numbness, tingling or colour change — but do not simply stop wearing it.
❌ "Now that the wounds are closed, infection is my biggest risk." — Wrong phase. Infection was the danger in the acute phase. Once wounds are healed, the risks are contractures, hypertrophic scarring, loss of function and psychosocial problems.
❌ "I can go back to sunbathing now that the skin has healed." — Wrong. Healed skin has less melanin, burns quickly and pigments permanently. Avoid direct sun to healed areas for at least a year; cover up and use high-SPF sunscreen.
🧠 “W-W-E — like the wrestling. You have to wrestle the scar every day.”

🎓 Question 10 — the priority question

A client is brought to the emergency department after being rescued from a house fire. There are burns to the face and neck, the voice is hoarse, and there is soot around the nose and mouth. Which action does the nurse take FIRST?
✅ Assess the airway and apply 100% oxygen by non-rebreather, anticipating early intubation — Correct. Enclosed-space fire + facial/neck burns + hoarseness + soot = inhalation injury. Airway swelling worsens over hours; intubate before it closes. Airway always outranks the burn.
❌ Calculate the TBSA using the Rule of Nines — Necessary, but not first. Nobody dies in the first five minutes from an un-calculated percentage.
❌ Start two large-bore IVs and begin Lactated Ringer's — This is second, not first. Circulation follows airway and breathing.
❌ Administer IV morphine for pain — Pain control matters, and it must be IV — but sedating a patient with a threatened airway before it is secured is unsafe.

AIRWAY — the four signs: facial/neck burn · singed nasal hair · sooty (carbonaceous) sputum · hoarseness or stridor

🧠 “Airway before area.” If one option protects the airway, stop reading the others.

🎯 Final speed round — 10 answers, no options
1. All posterior surfaces = ?
49.5% TBSA (4.5 + 18 + 9 + 18).
2. Anterior legs + perineum = ?
19% TBSA (9 + 9 + 1).
3. 100 kg, 19% TBSA — total LR in 24 h?
7,600 mL. Half (3,800 mL) in the first 8 hours, half over the next 16 hours.
4. When does the resuscitation clock start?
At the time of injury — not at arrival, not when the IV is started.
5. Urine 20 mL/hr at 12 hours — what do you anticipate?
Increasing the IV Lactated Ringer's. Never a diuretic, never slowing the rate.
6. When is interstitial shifting greatest?
Between 18 and 24 hours after the injury. Diuresis begins around 48–72 hours.
7. Expected haematocrit in a 35% burn on day one?
Elevated — classically 60% (0.60), from hemoconcentration. A typical adult reference band is about 36–54% (varies by lab and sex).
8. Emergent-phase potassium and sodium?
Potassium HIGH (over 5.0) from cell lysis; sodium LOW (under 135) as it follows water into the interstitium. Watch for tall peaked T waves.
9. Infection precaution for the immunocompromised burn client?
No fresh flowers or plants in or near the room — plus hand hygiene, a private room, PPE and sterile dressing technique.
10. The three rehab-phase teaching points?
W-W-E: Water-based lotion, Wear pressure garments, Exercise daily (range of motion). And remember — infection is not the main risk in this phase.

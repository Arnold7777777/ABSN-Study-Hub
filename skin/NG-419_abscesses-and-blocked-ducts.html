NG-419

🦠 Abscesses & Blocked Ducts

Three collections of pus in three places you would not otherwise put together. They turn up on the same exam because each has one signature symptom that gives the location away — and the question is testing whether you know which one.

⭐ The one idea

Location plus signature symptom. You are not being asked to diagnose. You are being asked to hear one odd detail — a voice, a mealtime, a place on the body — and name where the problem is.

And one of the three has an airway attached to it. That is the one that turns from a sore throat into an emergency.

🖼️ All three, side by side

📍 Three locations, three clues

👅 Peritonsillar abscess (quinsy) Beside a tonsil, usually on one side only Muffled “hot potato” voice

Pus collects between the tonsil and the wall of the throat. Look for severe one-sided sore throat, trismus (she cannot open her mouth widely), drooling, and the uvula pushed away from the swollen side.

It usually follows tonsillitis, and it is treated by drainage plus antibiotics — antibiotics alone do not empty a collection.

Drooling, stridor or difficulty breathing is an airway emergency. Assess the airway before anything else, and do not leave her alone to go and find someone.

🦠 Salivary stone (sialolithiasis) Usually the submandibular gland, in Wharton's duct under the tongue Pain and swelling that come on with meals

A stone blocks the duct. Saliva backs up behind it, so the gland swells while she is eating or even just smelling food, then settles again afterwards. That timing is the whole giveaway.

Conservative care first: fluids, warm compresses, gland massage, and sour sweets or lemon to make the gland produce saliva and push the stone along.

If it becomes hot, red, exquisitely tender and she is febrile, the blocked gland has become infected — that needs a provider.

🧹 Pilonidal disease The midline at the top of the buttock cleft, over the sacrum Pain or drainage at the top of the cleft

Hair and debris work into a small pit in the skin and set up a sinus or an abscess. Most often in young adults, and more so with prolonged sitting, hirsutism and obesity.

An acute abscess is drained. A chronic sinus may need excision, and those wounds are frequently left to heal by secondary intention — packed, open, slow.

Teaching that actually changes the recurrence rate: keep the area clean and dry, hair removal as advised, and avoid long unbroken periods of sitting.

✅ What is the same in all three

  • A collection of pus has to be drained. Antibiotics help the surrounding tissue; they do not empty a cavity.
  • Warm compresses for comfort and to encourage drainage — except where an airway is involved, where assessment comes first.
  • Pain control before care, not after. Dressing changes on an open pilonidal wound hurt.
  • Watch for spread: rising fever, expanding redness, tachycardia, feeling generally unwell.
  • Finish the antibiotic course even once it feels better, and return if it worsens.

The one that is different: only the peritonsillar abscess can close an airway. If a question gives you drooling, a muffled voice and trouble breathing, the answer is airway assessment — not the warm compress, not the antibiotic.

⭐ High yield

  • Peritonsillar abscess: one-sided, muffled voice, trismus, uvula deviated away from the swelling
  • Drooling or stridor with a peritonsillar abscess = airway emergency
  • Salivary stone: pain and swelling with meals, submandibular gland most often
  • Sialolithiasis first-line: fluids, warm compress, massage, sour sweets
  • Pilonidal disease: midline, top of the buttock cleft, young adults, sitting and hair are the risks
  • Pilonidal wounds often heal by secondary intention — packed and open
  • Pus gets drained. Antibiotics alone do not empty a collection
Sources. Written from the Merck Manual Professional Edition on peritonsillar abscess and cellulitis and salivary stones, and the Merck Manual Consumer Version on pilonidal disease.

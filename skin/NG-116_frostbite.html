Frostbite 🧊

The local cold injury — tissue that literally freezes

NG-116 INTEGUMENTARY · EMERGENCY ADHD-friendly visual edition

Frostbite is tissue that actually freezes: ice crystals form in and around the cells while the vessels clamp down, so the part is starved of blood at the same time as it is being torn apart from the inside. Frostbite is the LOCAL injury; hypothermia (NG-041) is the SYSTEMIC one — and the two arrive together far more often than not.

WHAT THE COLD ACTUALLY DOES

STEP 1 · PATHO

Two injuries happening at once: ice inside the tissue, and no blood getting to it.

🧊 Normal tissue vs frozen tissue — the cutaway

🧠 "Every cell becomes a little snowball." Two things are killing the tissue: the ice (direct cell damage) and the clamped, sludged vessels (ischemia). Rewarming fixes the ice — it does not undo the clot.

🧨 The cascade — why the damage keeps going after you warm it

Cold exposure → skin vessels vasoconstrict to protect the core
▼
Blood flow to fingers, toes, ears, nose falls → vascular stasis
▼
Tissue temperature drops below freezing → ice crystals form outside then inside cells
▼
Water pulled out of cells → cellular dehydration, electrolyte shifts, membrane rupture
▼
Rewarming → blood floods back → reperfusion injury: inflammation, edema, more microthrombi
▼
Progressive dermal ischemia over hours to days → blistering, then necrosis / gangrene

The final tissue loss is decided as much by the clotting and inflammation after thawing as by the freeze itself — which is why the appearance on day 1 does not predict the outcome.

🧠 Freeze, then flood. The freeze breaks the cells; the flood (reperfusion) clots the vessels. That is why frostbite keeps declaring itself for weeks.

📍 Where frostbite happens — the far corners

🧠 "The body sacrifices the edges to save the middle." Vasoconstriction protects the core and abandons the periphery — so the periphery is what freezes.

HOW DEEP? HOW BAD?

STEP 2 · CLUES

Same depth language as every other wound page — this time the injury came from cold.

🔎 The depth cutaway — frostnip → 4th degree

You may also see it split simply into two: superficial frostbite (skin blue, mottled or waxy yellow; clear blisters; skin still moves over the tissue below) and deep frostbite (skin white, hard as wood, blood-filled blisters, progressing to gangrene). The true depth cannot be judged until after rewarming — and often not for days.

🧠 Blister color = depth. Clear blister = superficial and likely to recover. Blood-filled blister = deep and likely to lose tissue. Exactly the same rule as pressure injuries on NG-044.

🔎 What you assess — before and after rewarming

  • Color — white / waxy yellow / mottled blue / gray; after thawing, does it turn red-purple (good) or stay pale (bad)?
  • Texture — soft and pliable (superficial) vs hard, wooden, non-indentable (deep).
  • Sensation — numbness is universal at first; return of sensation and pain after rewarming is a good sign.
  • Blisters — present? clear or hemorrhagic? when did they appear?
  • Perfusion — capillary refill, pulses (may need Doppler), warmth.
  • Edema — expect it to increase for 24–48 h after thawing; check for tight compartments.
  • Core temperature — always. Look for coexisting hypothermia.
SEE ALSO NG-051 Skin — capillary refill, blanching and lesion vocabulary; NG-080 Wound Care — for the wound that follows.
🧠 Pain returning is progress. A frostbitten part that hurts a lot after rewarming still has living nerve — the frightening one is the part that stays numb.

⭐ Frostbite vs hypothermia — local vs systemic

If the patient has both, the core comes first. Stabilize airway, breathing, circulation and core temperature before you commit to rewarming a limb — and handle the patient gently, because a cold heart is an irritable heart.

🧠 "Life before limb." Frostbite loses a finger; hypothermia loses the patient.

REWARMING — THE PRIORITY

STEP 3 · CARE

One correct method, and a list of tempting wrong ones.

🔥 Do this / never do that

🧠 "Warm water, no rubbing, no fire, no pressure." The frozen part is full of microscopic glass — rubbing drags it through the tissue, and anesthetic skin cannot tell you it is being burned.

✅ The rewarming sequence, in order

1
ABCs and core temperature first
Get the patient out of the cold. Treat systemic hypothermia before or alongside the limb, and handle gently — the cold heart is irritable.
▼
2
Remove wet and constrictive things
Wet clothing, boots, gloves, rings, watches and jewelry — before the swelling starts. Dry the patient, insulate the rest of the body.
▼
3
Decide: is refreezing possible?
If the part could refreeze before definitive care, do NOT thaw it. Protect and transport frozen. Freeze–thaw–refreeze causes far more tissue loss than staying frozen.
▼
4
Analgesia BEFORE you thaw
Rewarming is severely painful; opioid analgesia is usually required. Give it first, not after the screaming starts.
▼
5
Rapid rewarming
Immerse in circulating warm water at 37–39 °C (about 98.6–102 °F) — a whirlpool or a basin with gentle agitation — typically 20–30 minutes, until the part is soft, pliable and red-purple. Keep the part suspended, not resting on the bottom or sides of the basin. Recheck the water temperature constantly and add warm water away from the limb.
▼
6
After thawing
Elevate the extremity to control edema. Separate the digits with dry sterile gauze/cotton. Apply loose, bulky, non-adherent dressings. No occlusive or circumferential dressings. Bed rest — no weight-bearing on thawed feet.
▼
7
Ongoing
Tetanus status, infection surveillance, hydration, stop smoking, keep the part warm, ongoing analgesia. Ibuprofen is commonly used for its anti-prostaglandin effect; thrombolytic therapy exists for selected severe cases at specialist centers within a narrow time window. Follow provider orders and protocol.
🧠 1 core · 2 strip · 3 refreeze? · 4 pain meds · 5 warm water · 6 elevate & separate · 7 watch. Say the seven and the exam question answers itself.

🚨 The refreezing rule — the highest-yield trap

Do not begin rewarming unless the part can be kept thawed.

🧠 "Better frozen than half-thawed." A second freeze makes bigger ice crystals in tissue that is already injured.

💧 Blisters, dressings and the little details

  • Dressings: loose, bulky, dry, non-adherent. Place gauze between every finger and toe so they don't macerate against each other.
  • No occlusive dressings and nothing circumferential/tight — swelling after thawing can turn a snug wrap into a tourniquet.
  • Elevate the part after rewarming; keep the patient on bed rest if the feet are involved.
  • Blisters: practice varies. Many protocols debride or aspirate clear blisters and leave hemorrhagic blisters intact; some leave all blisters intact. Follow the provider's order and your facility protocol rather than guessing.
  • Handle the part like glass — no pressure, no rubbing, no adhesive tape directly on injured skin.
  • Keep the whole patient warm and hydrated; warm oral fluids if fully alert. No alcohol, no smoking.
🧠 Fluffy, loose, and separated. Picture packing a hand of raw eggs, not bandaging a sprain.

AFTER THE THAW

STEP 4 · WATCH & TEACH

The wound that follows behaves like any other wound — see NG-080 — but the timeline is unusually slow.

📅 What it looks like over time

Surgery is not rushed. Except for infection or compartment syndrome, amputation decisions wait for a clear line of demarcation — classically summarized as "frostbite in January, amputate in July." Early appearance badly over-predicts tissue loss.

🧠 Frostbite lies on day one. A black finger in week two may still have a viable base — which is why nobody cuts early.

🚨 Complications to watch for

  • Infection / cellulitis — the dead tissue is a culture medium. Fever, spreading redness, purulence, worsening pain.
  • Compartment syndrome — post-thaw edema in a closed space. Pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, tense compartment, paraesthesia. Escalate immediately; pulselessness is a late sign.
  • Gangrene and auto-amputation — dry gangrene mummifies and may separate on its own.
  • Rhabdomyolysis and AKI in extensive deep injury.
  • Long-term sequelae: permanent cold sensitivity, chronic neuropathic pain, numbness, hyperhidrosis, nail deformity, arthritis, and — in children — growth-plate damage.
🧠 Pain out of proportion after rewarming ≠ "just frostbite." Think compartment syndrome and escalate.

✅ Teach before discharge

  • You are now permanently cold-sensitive in that area — dress in layers, cover the ears, nose and hands, and change wet gloves and socks immediately.
  • Stop smoking — nicotine constricts the exact vessels the healing tissue depends on. Avoid alcohol in the cold.
  • Loose, layered, dry clothing; mittens beat gloves; boots that are not tight.
  • Protect the injured part — no direct heat, no soaking without instruction, no picking at blisters or eschar.
  • Report: fever, increasing pain, spreading redness, foul drainage, or any new blackening.
  • Keep every follow-up. The final extent takes weeks to declare, so appointments are not optional.

🎯 Cover & check — 9 rapid-fire questions
Q1: In one sentence, what is happening to the tissue in frostbite?
Ice crystals form in and around the cells, pulling water out and rupturing membranes, while cold-induced vasoconstriction causes vascular stasis and microthrombi, so the tissue is simultaneously frozen and starved of blood.
Q2: What is the priority treatment, and at what temperature?
Rapid rewarming by immersing the part in circulating warm water at about 37 to 39 degrees Celsius (about 98.6 to 102 degrees Fahrenheit), typically for 20 to 30 minutes, until the tissue is pliable and red-purple. Give analgesia first — thawing is intensely painful.
Q3: A patient with frostbitten feet still has a two-hour walk to shelter in freezing conditions. Do you rewarm now?
No. Do not thaw a part that could refreeze before definitive care. Freeze–thaw–refreeze produces far more tissue destruction than remaining frozen. Protect the part, keep the patient as warm as possible, and transport.
Q4: Name four things you must never do to a frostbitten part.
Never rub or massage it; never apply dry heat (fire, heater, stove, hot water bottle); never apply pressure, heavy blankets or tight/circumferential or occlusive dressings; and never let the patient walk on thawed frostbitten feet. Also never thaw if refreezing is possible.
Q5: Clear blister vs blood-filled blister — what do they tell you?
Clear or milky blisters indicate superficial (partial-thickness) injury with a generally good prognosis. Blood-filled (hemorrhagic) blisters indicate deep injury involving the subdermal vascular plexus, with likely tissue loss.
Q6: Your patient has frostbitten hands and a core temperature of 33 °C. What comes first?
The core. Treat the systemic hypothermia — ABCs, cardiac monitoring, gentle handling, core rewarming — before or alongside rewarming the hands. Life before limb.
Q7: What do you do immediately after rewarming?
Elevate the extremity to control edema, separate the digits with dry sterile gauze, apply loose bulky non-adherent dressings with nothing circumferential or occlusive, keep the patient on bed rest if the feet are involved, continue analgesia, and check tetanus status.
Q8: Why isn't amputation decided in the first week?
Because the early appearance badly over-predicts tissue loss. Surgeons wait for a clear line of demarcation between viable and non-viable tissue, which can take weeks to months — "frostbite in January, amputate in July." Exceptions are infection and compartment syndrome, which need urgent surgery.
Q9: How is frostbite related to hypothermia?
Frostbite is the LOCAL cold injury — actual freezing of peripheral tissue that threatens the limb. Hypothermia is the SYSTEMIC injury — a core temperature below 35 °C (95 °F) that threatens life through arrhythmia. They frequently occur together, and the core is always the priority.

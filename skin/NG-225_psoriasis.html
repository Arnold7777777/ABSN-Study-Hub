Psoriasis 🩹

Autoimmune skin — silvery plaques, and the one word patients need to hear

NG-225 INTEGUMENTARY · AUTOIMMUNE ADHD-friendly visual edition

Psoriasis is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the body attacks its own skin, pushing keratinocyte turnover from about 30 days down to 3–5 days. Immature cells pile up as well-demarcated red plaques topped with thick silvery scale on extensor surfaces — elbows, knees, scalp, sacrum and nails. There is no organism, so it is not contagious — that sentence is the single most important thing you will teach. Care is moisturise relentlessly, climb the treatment ladder only as far as needed, remember that sunlight helps, and always ask about the joints.

THE SKIN MODEL

STEP 0 · LEARN THIS ONCE

Same skin drawing as every page in this set — but look closely: there is no organism anywhere in it.

🔬 Skin cutaway — normal on the left, psoriatic plaque on the right

NO ORGANISM Cellulitis, impetigo, tinea and shingles all have something living in the picture. Psoriasis does not. The immune system is the pathogen — which is why it is treated by calming immunity down, and why it can never be caught from another person.

  • ① THICK SILVERY SCALE — keratinocytes shed before they mature (parakeratosis: they still carry their nuclei)
  • ② NO granular layer — the maturation step is skipped entirely
  • ③ DILATED, TORTUOUS CAPILLARIES — climbing high into long dermal papillae → the plaque is red, and lifting the scale bleeds
  • ④ ELONGATED RETE RIDGES — acanthosis, a thickened epidermis → the plaque is raised
  • ⑤ T LYMPHOCYTES — the autoimmune engine, releasing TNF-α, IL-17 and IL-23
silvery scale thickened epidermis dilated capillaries T lymphocytes neutrophil microabscesses
🧠 Long ridges + long capillaries + thin epidermis over them = the Auspitz sign. The picture explains the sign.

📖 Lesion vocabulary — psoriasis is the PLAQUE + SCALE disease

A plaque is a raised, flat-topped lesion larger than 1 cm. Add silvery scale and you have psoriasis.

🧠 Plaque = a papule that grew up. Bigger than 1 cm and flat on top.

PATHO & TRIGGERS

STEP 1 · WHY IT HAPPENS

An immune system that attacks its own skin, plus a trigger that sets it off.

🧠 The one-sentence definition

Psoriasis is a chronic autoimmune inflammatory disease in which immune cells attack healthy skin, causing massively accelerated division of epidermal cells. The immature cells pile up on the surface as thick, silvery, scaly plaques.

It is lifelong, comes in flares and remissions, and is not curable — but it is very controllable.

🧠 "Psoriasis = skin in a hurry." Nothing is invading; the factory is just running far too fast.

⚙️ Mechanism, step by step

Genetic predisposition + a trigger
▼
T lymphocytes are wrongly activated in the skin
▼
They release cytokines (TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23)
▼
Keratinocytes divide ~10× too fast — 3–5 days instead of ~30
▼
Immature cells stack up + capillaries dilate and grow
▼
Raised red plaque with silvery scale
🧠 The cytokines in that box are exactly what the biologic drugs block — TNF, IL-17, IL-23.

⏩ The speed problem — 30 days vs 4 days

🧠 The scale IS the cells. Silvery flakes are keratinocytes that were rushed to the surface before they finished.

🎯 Triggers — and the one that is NOT a trigger

🧠 "S · S · S — Stress, Sickness, Sepsis." And the trap: SUN IS GOOD, not a trigger. Sunburn is bad because it is trauma.

🧪 Cause → treatment class: the rule that runs this whole set

🧠 No organism → no antibiotic, no antifungal, no antiviral. You calm the immune system instead.

CLUES

STEP 2 · WHAT YOU SEE

Sharp-edged red plaques with silvery scale, on the outside of joints, in a patient who feels well.

👀 Signs & symptoms

  • Well-demarcated plaques — you can trace the edge with a finger
  • Raised and flat-topped, deep red or salmon-pink underneath
  • Thick, loose, SILVERY-WHITE scale on the surface
  • Symmetrical and on EXTENSOR surfaces — elbows, knees, scalp, sacrum
  • Itching, burning or soreness; plaques may crack and bleed
  • Nail changes — pitting, oil-drop discolouration, lifting, thickening
  • The patient is systemically well — no fever, no pus, no spreading redness
🧠 "Sharp · Silver · Symmetrical · Stretched-out (extensor)." Four S's.

🧬 The main types

  • Plaque (vulgaris) — by far the commonest; the classic silvery plaques
  • Guttate — sudden crop of small "raindrop" spots on the trunk, often after strep throat, common in children and young adults
  • Inverse (flexural) — smooth, shiny, red patches in the skin folds with little scale
  • Pustular — sterile pustules; generalised pustular psoriasis is a medical emergency
  • Erythrodermic — widespread redness and shedding; emergency (fluid loss, temperature instability, infection risk)
  • Nail and scalp psoriasis often accompany any of the above
🧠 "Guttate = gutter = raindrops" — small drop-like spots after a sore throat.

⭐ Two signs with names — Auspitz & Koebner

🧠 Auspitz = A for After you lift the scale (bleeding). Koebner = K for Knock/injury makes new plaques.

🗺️ Extensor vs flexural — plus the nails

🧠 Psoriasis is on the OUTSIDE of the joint. Eczema is on the INSIDE. That one line sorts most stems.

🦴 Psoriatic arthritis — psoriasis is not only skin-deep

🧠 "Skin first, joints later." Ask about morning stiffness at every visit — early referral prevents permanent damage.

CARE

STEP 3 · WHAT YOU DO

Moisturise relentlessly, calm the immune system as far as you need to, and treat the person, not just the plaque.

🪜 The treatment ladder

🧠 Every rung sits on emollient. If "apply moisturiser regularly" is an option, it is rarely wrong.

🧴 THE two NCLEX answers: moisturise & sunlight

1. Moisturise frequently. Apply a thick emollient immediately after bathing, onto damp skin, to trap water in. Use lukewarm (not hot) water, a mild non-soap cleanser, and pat dry — never rub.

2. Sunlight in moderation is GOOD. Controlled UV light slows the overactive skin cells. Do not tell a psoriasis patient to avoid the sun — but do teach them to avoid sunburn, which is trauma and can trigger Koebner.

🧠 Students answer "avoid sunlight" and lose the mark. Sun helps. Sunburn hurts.

💊 Topical therapy — the workhorse

  • Emollients — the foundation, used every day forever
  • Topical corticosteroids — the mainstay for flares; use the lowest effective potency for the shortest time, and only mild preparations on the face and folds (skin thinning, striae)
  • Vitamin D analogues (calcipotriene/calcipotriol) — often paired with a steroid
  • Coal tar — effective; warn about staining and smell
  • Salicylic acid — lifts thick scale so other treatments can get in
  • Calcineurin inhibitors — steroid-sparing option for the face and folds
  • Apply thin, rub in gently along the direction of hair growth; wash hands after
🧠 Descale first, then treat. Salicylic acid or a soak lifts scale so the active drug reaches skin.

💉 Systemic & biologic therapy — safety points

  • Methotrexate is dosed WEEKLY, never daily. Daily dosing is a fatal medication error. Folic acid is usually prescribed alongside it
  • Methotrexate is teratogenic — it must be stopped and effective contraception used; check pregnancy status. Monitor FBC, LFTs, renal function; avoid alcohol
  • Biologics (infliximab, adalimumab, etanercept; IL-17/IL-23 inhibitors) — screen for latent TB and hepatitis first; hold for active infection; teach infection precautions and injection technique
  • Live vaccines are generally avoided on biologics/immunosuppressants — check before administering
  • Ciclosporin — monitor BP and renal function. Acitretin — strongly teratogenic
  • Systemic corticosteroids are generally avoided — withdrawal can cause a severe rebound flare
🧠 "Methotrexate: Monday, not morning-and-night." Weekly. Always check the frequency.

🗣️ Teaching — start with the sentence that matters most

  • "You cannot give this to anyone. It is not an infection." Say it first, and say it to the family too
  • Moisturise daily, especially after bathing; lukewarm water, gentle cleanser, pat dry
  • Protect the skin — avoid scratching, picking scale, tight clothing and sunburn (Koebner)
  • Use treatments as prescribed, even when the skin looks better; flares will come and go
  • Stop smoking, moderate alcohol, manage weight, manage stress
  • Report: joint pain or morning stiffness, widespread redness, pustules, fever, or signs of infection on immunosuppressants
  • Signpost support — psoriasis charities, patient groups, mental-health support
🧠 The first teaching point is not a cream — it is "this is not catching."

❌ Never do these

  • Never imply psoriasis is contagious, or use isolation precautions for it.
  • Never give methotrexate daily — it is a weekly dose.
  • Never scrub, pick or forcibly remove the scale — that is trauma and Koebnerises.
  • Never use potent topical steroids on the face or skin folds without a specific order.
  • Never tell the patient to stay out of the sun.
  • Never start a biologic without checking TB/hepatitis screening and for active infection.
🧠 Don't isolate, don't scrub, don't dose it daily, don't ban the sun.

🚨 When psoriasis becomes an emergency

  • Erythrodermic psoriasis — widespread redness and shedding over most of the body. The skin barrier fails: fluid and protein loss, hypothermia, high-output heart failure, sepsis risk
  • Generalised pustular psoriasis — sheets of sterile pustules with fever and feeling very unwell
  • Often triggered by abrupt withdrawal of systemic steroids, severe infection or drug reactions
  • Nursing: monitor temperature, fluid balance and vital signs; warm environment; meticulous skin care; IV fluids as prescribed; urgent dermatology review
🧠 Skin is an organ. Lose enough of it and the patient behaves like a burns patient.

⭐ NCLEX traps

  • Sunlight is GOOD. The most-missed question on this topic.
  • Psoriasis is NOT contagious — no isolation, no gloves-for-fear, and say so to the family.
  • Methotrexate is WEEKLY. Check the frequency on every order.
  • Moisturise after bathing on damp skin — not "avoid moisturiser because it is greasy."
  • Do not pick or scrub the scale — Koebner makes new plaques.
  • New small "raindrop" spots after a sore throat = guttate psoriasis.
  • Widespread redness + shedding = erythrodermic = emergency, treat like a barrier failure.
  • Joint pain in a psoriasis patient = refer, do not dismiss.
🧠 If the option says "reassure the patient it is not contagious", it is almost always correct.

🧠 One-line contrasts

  • Psoriasis vs tinea — solid silvery plaque vs a ring with a clear centre
  • Psoriasis vs eczema — extensor & sharply edged vs flexural & ill-defined
  • Psoriasis vs cellulitis — chronic, scaly, well patient vs acute, hot, febrile patient
  • Psoriasis vs seborrhoeic dermatitis — thick silvery scale vs greasy yellowish scale on the scalp and nose folds
  • Nail psoriasis vs fungal nail — pitting and oil-drop vs thick crumbly yellow-brown; test if unsure
  • Psoriatic arthritis vs rheumatoid — DIP joints and sausage digits vs MCP/PIP and symmetrical small joints
🧠 Silver scale, sharp edge, extensor, well patient. Four boxes ticked = psoriasis.

🎯 Cover & check — 10 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What is happening in the skin in psoriasis?
Autoimmune T-cell activation drives keratinocytes to divide far too fast — turnover falls from about 28–30 days to 3–5 days — so immature cells pile up as thick silvery scale on a red, thickened plaque.
Q2: Describe the classic lesion and where it appears.
Well-demarcated, raised, flat-topped red plaques with thick silvery-white scale, symmetrically on extensor surfaces — elbows, knees, scalp and hairline, lower back/sacrum — plus nail changes.
Q3: What is the Auspitz sign, and why does it happen?
Pinpoint bleeding when scale is lifted off a plaque. The dermal papillae are elongated and packed with dilated capillaries lying just under a very thin epidermis, so removing the scale tears them open.
Q4: What is the Koebner phenomenon?
New psoriatic lesions appearing at sites of skin injury — a scratch, surgical scar, sunburn, tattoo or friction. It is why patients are taught to protect their skin and not to pick scale.
Q5: A patient asks whether they can pass psoriasis to their children. What do you say?
Psoriasis is not contagious — it cannot be caught or passed on by contact, because there is no infecting organism. There is a genetic tendency, so relatives have a higher chance of developing it, but that is inheritance, not infection.
Q6: Is sun exposure good or bad in psoriasis?
Moderate sun exposure usually improves psoriasis — that is the principle behind UVB phototherapy. Sunburn, however, is skin trauma and can trigger new lesions through the Koebner phenomenon, so teach sensible exposure.
Q7: Name the S-S-S triggers, and two others.
Stress, Sickness (infection — strep throat can trigger guttate psoriasis) and Sepsis. Others: skin trauma, certain drugs (beta blockers, lithium, antimalarials, and withdrawal of systemic steroids), smoking, alcohol, cold dry weather and obesity.
Q8: What are the two most important pieces of everyday self-care?
Moisturise frequently — a thick emollient onto damp skin straight after a lukewarm bath or shower — and protect the skin from trauma and sunburn. Emollients underpin every other treatment.
Q9: Give the key safety points for methotrexate.
It is dosed WEEKLY, never daily — daily dosing is a fatal error. Folic acid is usually prescribed with it. It is teratogenic, so pregnancy must be excluded and effective contraception used. Monitor full blood count, liver and renal function, and advise avoiding alcohol.
Q10: A patient with psoriasis reports 45 minutes of morning stiffness and a swollen index finger. What is this and what do you do?
Likely psoriatic arthritis with dactylitis — a "sausage digit". It typically affects the DIP joints, unlike rheumatoid arthritis. Report it and arrange early rheumatology referral, because untreated joint inflammation causes permanent damage.

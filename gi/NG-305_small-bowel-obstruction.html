Small Bowel Obstruction 🌀

SBO — Mechanical vs. Non-Mechanical (Paralytic Ileus)

NG-305 GI ADHD-friendly visual edition

A blockage in the small intestine — food, fluid & gas back up behind the block. Two flavors: mechanical (a physical block — adhesions, hernia, tumor, volvulus) and non-mechanical (paralytic ileus — the bowel just stops moving). Untreated → bowel perforation & peritonitis.

🌀 The classic triadColicky pain + distension + vomiting.
🚨 Fever + tachycardia + rigid belly= bowel perforation, notify HCP.
🎧 Bowel sounds tell locationHyperactive above the block, hypoactive/absent below.
❌ No opioidsSlow the gut further — avoid morphine, hydrocodone, hydromorphone.
🧨

CAUSE

STEP 1 · WHAT'S BLOCKING THE TUBE

Split obstruction into two categories: something physically in the way, or the bowel simply stopped moving.

🌀 Mechanical vs. non-mechanical (paralytic ileus)

DEFINITION A small bowel obstruction is a blockage in the small intestine that stops the normal flow of contents.

🧠 "Mechanical = something's IN the way. Paralytic = nothing works, PERIOD." One is a roadblock; the other is a stalled engine.

⚠️ Mechanical obstruction — causes

  • 🩹 Adhesions (scar tissue) — from prior surgery, most common cause
  • 🕳️ Hernias
  • 🌀 Volvulus — twisting of the bowel
  • 📥 Intussusception — bowel telescopes into itself
  • 🎗️ Tumor (cancer)
🧠 "AHVIT" — Adhesions · Hernias · Volvulus · Intussusception · Tumor. Adhesions from a past surgery are the #1 answer.

⚠️ Non-mechanical (paralytic ileus) — think "why isn't it moving?"

  • 🔪 Recent abdominal surgery (bowel handling)
  • 💊 Opioids & anticholinergics slow motility
  • 🧂 Electrolyte imbalance — hypokalemia is classic
  • 🦠 Peritonitis / sepsis
🧠 A quiet, non-tender, evenly distended belly after surgery = suspect ileus, not a mechanical block.

🚨 RED FLAG — call the provider now (bowel perforation)

An unrelieved obstruction stretches the bowel wall until it perforates → contents spill into the peritoneum → peritonitis is a medical emergency.

🌡️Feversudden
💓Tachycardia 
💨Tachypnea 
🪵Rigid, board-like abdomen 
🎈Worsening distension 
🧠 COMMON EXAM QUESTION Client with an SBO suddenly develops tachycardia, tachypnea, and a high fever. Most likely problem? Bowel perforation — notify the HCP immediately. (Not PE, pneumonia, or atelectasis — those don't fit a sudden fever + rigid abdomen picture in this context.)
🔎

CLUES

STEP 2 · THE CLASSIC TRIAD

Colicky pain, a swelling belly, and vomiting that won't quit — plus bowel sounds that tell you exactly where the block is.

🌀 The classic triad — RAPID onset

⚡Colicky painintermittent, cramping
🎈Abdominal distension 
🤮Frequent nausea & vomiting 
🧠 "Colic, Cramp, Can't-keep-it-down." Three C-sounding clues = triad of SBO: Colicky pain, belly bloats like a bCalloon (distension), and Can't stop vomiting.

🎧 Bowel sounds — location clue

🧠 Loud before, quiet after. The gut is straining to push past the block (hyperactive above); nothing gets through to the segment below (hypoactive/absent).

⚠️ Complication ladder

🌀 Obstruction — pain, distension, vomiting
▼
🎈 Worsening distension, dehydration, electrolyte loss
▼
🚨 Peritonitis — medical emergency
🧠 If it's not relieved, the bowel wall stretches until it tears. Every unmanaged SBO is a countdown to perforation.

🔬 Common exam question, broken down

"Client with a small bowel obstruction suddenly develops tachycardia and tachypnea with a high fever. What is most likely the problem?"

  • 1. Pulmonary embolism — ❌ no chest/leg clue given
  • 2. Bowel perforation, notify HCP — ✅ correct answer
  • 3. Pneumonia — ❌ no respiratory clue given
  • 4. Atelectasis — ❌ doesn't cause fever/tachycardia like this
🧠 New fever + tachycardia + tachypnea in the context of a known obstruction = perforation until proven otherwise.
🩺

CARE

STEP 3 · REST THE GUT, RELIEVE THE PRESSURE

Stop feeding it, decompress it, and don't slow it down further with opioids.

✅ Treatment — priority actions

1
NPO — nothing by mouth, rest the bowel
2
NGT insertion — nasogastric tube for decompression + IV fluids
3
Semi-Fowler's position
4
Pain control — non-opioid preferred
🧠 "NNSP" — NPO · NGT · Semi-Fowler's · Pain control (non-opioid). Rest it, drain it, sit it up, treat pain smartly.

❌ AVOID opioid analgesics

Opioids further slow GI motility — the last thing a stalled bowel needs.

💊Morphine 
💊Hydrocodone 
💊Hydromorphone 
🧠 Opioids = "constipation in a syringe." They slow the very motility the bowel is already failing at.

🔪 Surgery — when conservative care fails

Clients who fail to improve need a bowel resection to remove the diseased/dead segment, and may need a temporary ostomy until the bowel heals.

🧠 NGT + NPO buys time — surgery is the fallback if the obstruction won't resolve or perforation occurs.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What's the classic triad of small bowel obstruction?
Colicky abdominal pain, abdominal distension, and frequent nausea/vomiting.
Q2: Client's bowel sounds are hyperactive in the upper abdomen and absent lower down. What does that tell you?
The obstruction is between the two — hyperactive sounds occur above the block, hypoactive/absent sounds occur below it.
Q3: What's the most common cause of mechanical SBO?
Adhesions (scar tissue) from prior abdominal surgery.
Q4: Why avoid morphine for SBO pain?
Opioids further slow GI motility, worsening the obstruction — use non-opioid pain control instead.

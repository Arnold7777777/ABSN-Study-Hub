MAP

STEP 1 · THE 4 QUADRANTS

Draw a cross through the umbilicus — everything the exam asks about lives in one of these four boxes.

🧭 The abdomen, divided into 4 quadrants (RUQ / LUQ / RLQ / LLQ)

🟦 RUQ = liver/gallbladder🟩 LUQ = stomach/spleen/pancreas 🩷 RLQ = appendix🟨 LLQ = sigmoid colon
🧠 “RUQ, LUQ on top — RLQ, LLQ down below.” Right is always the patient's right, not yours — stand at the bedside and mirror it in your head.

🔤 Reading the letters

  • R/L = patient's Right / Left
  • U = Upper (above umbilicus)
  • L = Lower (below umbilicus)
  • Q = Quadrant
🧠 Always the patient's right/left, never the observer's — a classic first-semester trap.

📋 Inspection — what "normal" looks like

  • 🫃 Contour: flat, rounded, scaphoid (sunken), or distended
  • ⚖️ Symmetry: should be symmetric side to side
  • 🩹 Skin: no lesions, striae are common/benign, no bulging at umbilicus (hernia)
  • 💓 No visible pulsations (except faint epigastric aortic pulsation is normal in thin clients)
🧠 Look before you touch — a visibly distended, rigid, or asymmetric abdomen changes your whole plan before you even auscultate.
👂

TECHNIQUE

STEP 2 · THE ORDER IS BACKWARDS

Every other body system: inspect → palpate → percuss → auscultate. The abdomen breaks that rule on purpose.

🔄 IAPP — Auscultate BEFORE you Palpate

1
👀 Inspect — contour, symmetry, skin, pulsations
2
👂 Auscultate — bowel sounds in all 4 quadrants
3
🥁 Percuss — tympany (gas) vs dullness (fluid/organ)
4
🤲 Palpate — light first, then deep, tender areas LAST

Why? Percussion and palpation can stimulate or falsely change bowel motility/sounds — so you must listen first to get an accurate baseline.

🧠 “Look, Listen, Tap, Touch.” Every other system is Inspect-Palpate-Percuss-Auscultate — the abdomen swaps the last two to the front: I-A-P-P.

👂 Bowel sounds — how to actually listen

  • 📈 Hyperactive ("borborygmi") — early obstruction, gastroenteritis, diarrhea
  • 📉 Hypoactive — post-op ileus, peritonitis, opioids
  • 🚫 Absent — must listen 5 full minutes per quadrant before documenting "absent"; late obstruction, paralytic ileus, peritonitis = emergency
🧠 “Silence is not nothing — silence is an emergency.” Never chart "absent bowel sounds" after a quick listen.

🤲 Palpation — light, then deep, painful area LAST

  • ✅ Start away from the painful area
  • ✅ Light palpation first (~1 cm), then deep (~5–8 cm)
  • 🚨 Guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness = peritoneal signs → stop, this is an emergency finding
🧠 If a stem describes a rigid, board-like abdomen with rebound tenderness, fever, and rising pain — think peritonitis, report to the HCP immediately, don't keep palpating.

🚨 Red-flag findings — stop and report

  • 🪵 Rigid, board-like abdomen
  • 🤲 Rebound tenderness
  • 🌡️ Fever >100.3°F
  • 📈 Increasing, worsening pain
  • 💓 Tachycardia / tachypnea + restlessness
🧠 This exact red-flag cluster = peritonitis — you'll see it repeat across GERD, pancreatitis, appendicitis, and diverticulitis pages. Learn it once here.

📝 Documentation shape

Chart: contour → symmetry → bowel sounds (present/hyper/hypo/absent, which quadrants) → tenderness/guarding location → masses/pulsations.

🧠 “Look, Listen, Tap, Touch — then write it down in that order.” Matching your documentation order to your exam order keeps you from skipping steps.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What is the correct order of abdominal assessment, and why does it differ from other systems?
Inspect → Auscultate → Percuss → Palpate. Auscultation comes before palpation because percussion/palpation can alter bowel motility and change the sounds you hear.
Q2: How long should you listen before charting "absent" bowel sounds?
A full 5 minutes per quadrant — don't document "absent" after a quick listen.
Q3: A client has pain and an inspiratory pause when you press deep under the right costal margin — what sign, and what condition?
Murphy's sign — cholecystitis (RUQ, gallbladder).
Q4: What abdominal finding cluster means peritonitis and requires immediate provider notification?
Rigid/board-like abdomen, rebound tenderness, fever >100.3°F, increasing pain, restlessness, tachycardia/tachypnea.

Cholecystitis 🫘

Gallbladder Inflammation — Biliary Colic vs. Acute Attack

NG-179 GI ADHD-friendly visual edition

Inflammation of the gallbladder — typically caused by gallstones (cholelithiasis) that block the cystic duct, so bile backs up and inflames the gallbladder wall. Classic picture: RUQ pain that radiates to the right shoulder, worse after a fatty meal, plus a positive Murphy's sign.

CAUSE

STEP 1 · THE BLOCKED DUCT

One blocked duct explains the whole disease — bile with nowhere to go inflames the gallbladder wall.

🫘 Gallstones block the cystic duct → bile backs up → inflammation

DEFINITION Inflammation of the gallbladder, typically caused by gallstones (cholelithiasis) that block the ducts leading out of the gallbladder, causing a backup of bile.

🧠 "Stone in the road = bile with nowhere to go." The gallstone plugs the cystic duct — bile can't drain, pressure builds, the wall inflames.

⚠️ Risk factors — the classic "4 F's" (expanded)

  • 🥓 High-fat diet
  • ⚖️ Obesity
  • 🎂 Age over 40
  • ♀️ Female sex & history of pregnancy (estrogen raises cholesterol saturation in bile)
🧠 "Fat, Forty, Female, Fertile" — the classic 4 F's for gallstone risk. Not every client fits the mnemonic, but it's a fast recall trigger.

CLUES

STEP 2 · COLIC VS. ACUTE ATTACK

Biliary colic is the stone moving; acute cholecystitis is the gallbladder actually inflamed and infected.

⚖️ Biliary colic vs. acute cholecystitis

Feature🌗 Biliary colic🔥 Acute cholecystitis
CauseStone temporarily blocks the duct, then passes/repositionsStone stays lodged — persistent obstruction & inflammation, often with infection
Pain patternComes and goes, often after a fatty meal, resolves within a few hoursConstant, severe RUQ pain that doesn't resolve on its own
FeverUsually absentPresent — fever with chills
Murphy's signUsually negativePositive — pain + sudden inspiratory arrest on RUQ palpation
🧠 "Colic comes and goes; cholecystitis stays and grows." If the pain is constant, feverish, and the belly guards on exam — that's the acute attack, not simple colic.

⭐ Highly tested signs & symptoms

  • 📍 RUQ pain "radiates to the right shoulder"
  • 🌡️ Fever with chills
  • 💓 Tachycardia
  • 🖐️ Positive Murphy's sign
  • 🥓 Pain worse after a high-fat meal — fat triggers cholecystokinin release, gallbladder contracts against the blockage

🚨 RED FLAG — call the provider now

These suggest complication (perforation, empyema, ascending cholangitis):

🌡️High fever, rigors 
💛Jaundicebile duct involvement
💓Marked tachycardia 
🪵Rigid abdomenperitonitis
🧠 Fever + jaundice + RUQ pain together = think Charcot's triad (ascending cholangitis) — an emergency, not routine colic.

🖐️ Murphy's sign — how it's tested

Examiner presses on the RUQ and asks the client to take a deep breath. Pain + sudden stop of inspiration = positive sign — the inflamed gallbladder drops onto the examiner's fingers on inhalation.

🧠 "Murphy's law — you get caught mid-breath." A positive sign literally stops the breath.
🩺

CARE

STEP 3 · REST THE GALLBLADDER, THEN REMOVE IT

NPO during the attack, low fat always, and surgery is the definitive fix.

✅ Priority intervention — treatment

1
NPO status — nothing by mouth; eating triggers more pain & complications
2
IV fluids, pain control, antiemetics as ordered
3
Antibiotics if infection suspected
4
Monitor for surgical vs. non-surgical management
🧠 NPO first, always. Any fat or food makes the gallbladder contract against a blocked duct — that's exactly what you don't want.

🔨 Lithotripsy vs. surgery

  • 💥 Lithotripsy — shock waves break up stones; used when stones are small enough
  • 🔪 Cholecystectomy — surgical removal of the gallbladder, done when stones are too large or disease is recurrent/severe
🧠 Lithotripsy shatters; cholecystectomy removes. If the stone's too big to shatter, the whole organ comes out.

🏥 Post-op cholecystectomy care

  • 🚶 Assist with early ambulation — prevents pneumonia
  • 🫁 Deep breathe & cough — priority teaching for an open cholecystectomy (bigger incision, higher pneumonia risk)
  • 🚿 No baths — shower only, protects the incision
  • 🔴 Report redness/swelling at incision sites to the surgeon
🧠 KAPLAN QUESTION Priority action for a client scheduled for an open cholecystectomy? Demonstrate ways to deep breathe and cough. A bigger incision near the diaphragm = higher risk of shallow breathing & pneumonia post-op.

🎓 Discharge teaching — HESI-style questions

HESI QUESTION Following a laparoscopic cholecystectomy, which instructions would the nurse include? Select all that apply.

  • ✅ May take a shower
  • ✅ Wait about 1 week after surgery before returning to work (varies with procedure & provider order)
  • ✅ Lose weight if overweight
  • ✅ Avoid fatty, fried foods

HESI QUESTION A nurse caring for a patient who recently had the gallbladder removed knows the patient will have difficulty digesting large amounts of which nutrient? Fats — bile helps emulsify fat, and without a gallbladder to concentrate/store bile, fat digestion is less efficient.

🧠 No gallbladder = go low-fat, forever-ish. Bile still drips from the liver, but there's no reservoir to release a big bolus when a fatty meal hits.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: Where does gallbladder pain classically radiate?
RUQ pain radiating to the right shoulder/scapula.
Q2: What's the difference between biliary colic and acute cholecystitis?
Biliary colic is intermittent pain as a stone temporarily blocks/moves, usually without fever. Acute cholecystitis is constant pain with fever, chills, and a positive Murphy's sign — the duct stays blocked and the gallbladder becomes inflamed/infected.
Q3: What's the priority teaching for a client scheduled for an open cholecystectomy?
Demonstrate deep breathing and coughing — reduces pneumonia risk from the upper abdominal incision.
Q4: What nutrient will a post-cholecystectomy client have trouble digesting in large amounts?
Fats — teach a low-fat diet since there's no gallbladder reservoir to release a bile bolus for a fatty meal.

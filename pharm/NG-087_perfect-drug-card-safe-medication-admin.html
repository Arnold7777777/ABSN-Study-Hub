THE TEMPLATE

STEP 1 · THE FAB 5

Every drug card, no matter the class, answers the same 5 questions in the same order.

🧾 The Fab 5 — filled in for a real drug (Lisinopril)

🧠 "Name it, Class it, Explain it, Watch it, Skip it." Every drug card on Earth is this same 5-box chain — only the words inside the boxes change.

①② Name & Class — the ID tags

  • Every drug has 1 generic name but can have multiple brand/trade names
  • EXAM TIP NCLEX tests generic names only, never brand names
  • Class = the drug family — often told by its suffix (see the Drug Suffixes reference page)
GenericBrand
WarfarinCoumadin
IbuprofenAdvil, Motrin
AcetaminophenTylenol
🧠 1 drug, 1 generic, many nicknames. Learn the generic first — that's the one that shows up on the exam.

③ Mechanism → Indication — the "why it works"

Mechanism of action = how the drug works at the cellular/chemical level. Indication = what it's used for — the two are always connected by a chain of logic.

  • Penicillin breaks down the bacterial cell wall → kills bacteria → treats infection
  • Heparin/Warfarin block clotting factors → prevents clots → treats DVT/PE/AFib
  • Labetalol blocks alpha & beta receptors → vessels relax, heart slows → treats hypertension
🧠 "Mechanism is the verb, indication is the reason." If you can explain the mechanism in one sentence, the indication almost writes itself.

④⑤ Side Effects & Contraindications — the "watch for"

Side effects = expected, usually tolerable. Contraindications = reasons to never give this drug to this client.

  • Antibiotics for ear/GI/skin infections often cause nausea & vomiting (side effect)
  • Acetaminophen + liver disease = contraindication (hepatotoxicity risk)
🧠 A side effect you monitor; a contraindication you don't even start.

KNOW THE WORDS

STEP 2 · EFFECT VOCABULARY

These 4 words get mixed up constantly on exams — the difference is severity and predictability.

📊 Therapeutic → Side → Adverse → Toxic: one severity ladder

🧠 "Therapeutic is the goal, side is a shrug, adverse is an alarm, toxic is a buildup." Toxic effect specifically means the drug is accumulating — think renal failure or elderly clients who can't clear it.

🔗 Synergistic vs. ❌ Antagonistic vs. ➕ Additive interactions

InteractionWhat happens
AdditiveCombined effect = same as the sum of each drug alone
SynergisticCombined effect is greater than expected — e.g. opioids + alcohol → dangerous CNS depression
AntagonisticOne drug blocks another — e.g. naloxone reverses opioids
🧠 Synergy stacks, antagonism blocks. Naloxone is the antagonist superhero of every opioid overdose question.

🤰 Pregnancy categories — safest to most dangerous

A = well-controlled studies show no risk. X = studies show proven fetal harm — never given in pregnancy.

🧠 "A is Awesome, X marks eXit." Alphabetical order = risk order.

🚫 Contraindication vs. ⚠️ Caution — know which one stops you cold

Contraindication = a reason never to give the drug to this client at all — e.g. acetaminophen in severe liver disease, pain medication + active alcohol use, caffeine-containing stimulants + Adderall.

These aren't side effects to monitor — they're reasons to hold the drug and call the prescriber before it's ever given.

🧠 A side effect is something you watch. A contraindication is something you stop.

SAFE ADMIN

STEP 3 · APPLY THE TEMPLATE SAFELY

Knowing the card means nothing if the med doesn't reach the right client safely — this is where the 5 Rights page picks up.

✅ From card to bedside — the full loop

🗂️ Study the Fab 5 for this drug (name · class · mechanism → indication · side fx · contraindications)
▼
🧾 Verify the order against the MAR — 3 checks: med room, order, bedside
▼
✅ Confirm the 5 Rights — patient · drug · dose · route · time
▼
📝 Document immediately & evaluate the response
🧠 Knowledge (the card) + Process (the rights) = safety. One without the other still causes errors.

🧪 Toxic effect — a Fab-5 red flag to watch

Accumulation happens fastest when the client can't eliminate the drug — think renal failure, hepatic impairment, or the elderly with reduced clearance.

🧠 If a drug card doesn't mention how it's cleared, ask — renal vs. hepatic clearance changes your monitoring plan.

🎓 Client education is part of the card

A complete drug card always ends with: what should the client watch for and report? That single question turns memorized facts into real teaching.

🧠 "If you can't teach it back, you don't know the card yet."

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What are the 5 fields of a complete drug card?
Name (generic + brand), class, mechanism of action leading to indication, side effects, and contraindications.
Q2: What's the difference between a side effect and an adverse effect?
Side effect = expected, usually mild. Adverse effect = unexpected and severe, like an allergic reaction with hives or respiratory distress.
Q3: Naloxone reversing an opioid overdose is what kind of drug interaction?
Antagonistic — one drug blocks the action of another.
Q4: Does NCLEX test brand names or generic names?
Generic names only — learn the generic name for every drug you study.

Adrenergic Blockers 🚫⚡

Adrenergic Antagonists — the umbrella term for alpha- & beta-blockers

NG-095 PHARM ADHD-friendly visual edition

"Adrenergic antagonist" just means a drug that blocks a receptor the sympathetic nervous system uses. There are two families — alpha-blockers and beta-blockers — and some drugs (carvedilol, labetalol) block both at once. This page is the overview and the alpha side; beta-blockers get their own deep dive.

🚫 Antagonist = blockerSits on the receptor and stops norepinephrine/epinephrine from working there.
🩸 Alpha blocked = vessels relaxVasodilation → BP drops → orthostatic hypotension is the #1 safety issue.
🫀 Beta blocked = heart slowsFull mechanism, drug list & asthma warning live on NG-176 Beta Blockers — cross-reference, don't re-learn it here.
🚨 Epi is contraindicatedIn an alpha-blocker hypotensive crisis, epinephrine can drop BP further — give norepinephrine instead.

WHAT IT DOES

STEP 1 · THE MECHANISM

Norepinephrine and epinephrine turn receptors ON — an adrenergic blocker's whole job is to sit on that same receptor and turn it back OFF.

🔒 Agonist turns it on · Antagonist blocks it

EXAM TIP Block alpha receptors → vasodilation by relaxing the smooth muscle of blood vessels. In ophthalmic preps, alpha blockade also relaxes the iris dilator muscle, which is why alpha-blocker use is linked to intraoperative floppy iris syndrome during cataract surgery.

🧠 "Antagonist = anti-agonist." The blocker doesn't do anything itself — it just squats on the parking spot so norepinephrine can't park there. No signal in, no effect out.

🗺️ Where the receptors live

  • Alpha-1 — blood vessels, bladder neck/prostate smooth muscle, iris dilator
  • Alpha-2 — mostly presynaptic (brakes on further NE release); clonidine stimulates this one centrally — see NG-243 Clonidine
  • Beta-1 — heart (rate & contractility)
  • Beta-2 — bronchioles & vessels (bronchodilation, vasodilation)
🧠 "1 is the one right next to it." Beta-1 = heart, right in your chest. Beta-2 = the other two letters away — lungs.

🧠 "Alpha drops the pipes, Beta drops the pump." Alpha-blockers relax the pipes (vessels); beta-blockers slow the pump (heart).

💊 The dual blockers — carvedilol & labetalol block BOTH

A few drugs don't pick a side — they block alpha-1 AND beta receptors at the same time, giving both vasodilation and a slower heart rate in one pill.

GenericTradeUseDose / route
CarvedilolCoreg, Coreg CREssential HTN; HF — reduces disease progression6.25 mg PO twice daily (starting dose)
LabetalolTrandateHTN — usually an add-on to a diuretic100 mg PO twice daily; also IV

Wean, don't crash: in uncompensated HF needing IV inotropes, those must be tapered off before starting carvedilol — starting a beta-blocking drug on top of a failing, inotrope-dependent heart can push it into decompensation.

🧠 "Carve out both problems." Carvedilol = carves into both alpha and beta receptors — one drug, two mechanisms, two reasons the BP comes down.

🧠 "Head to toe, blocked receptors mess with all of it." You don't need to recite every line — know the three bold-red never-miss ones: Stevens-Johnson/anaphylaxis, bradycardia/HF/pulmonary edema, and bronchospasm.

🚨 Contraindications — don't give if…

  • History of serious hypersensitivity reaction
  • Pulmonary edema or cardiogenic shock
  • Bradycardia, heart block, or sick sinus syndrome
  • Uncompensated HF needing IV inotropes — wean first
  • Severe hepatic impairment
  • Asthma or other bronchospastic disorders
🧠 "Slow heart, tight lungs, sick liver — say no."

💊 Interactions to flag

  • Antidepressants → tremors
  • Cimetidine → increased adrenergic-blocker effect
  • Clonidine → increases clonidine's effect (two BP-lowering drugs stacking)
  • Digoxin → digoxin toxicity risk
🧠 "Cimetidine slows the liver, so the blocker builds up." Same logic shows up with lots of H2-blocker drug interactions.

🚨 Hypotensive crisis on IV therapy — why epinephrine is the wrong answer

Monitor BP, pulse, and ECG every 2 minutes until stable during IV administration. If a hypotensive crisis occurs, epinephrine is contraindicated — because alpha is blocked, epi's unopposed beta-2 effect can push BP down further ("epinephrine reversal"). Norepinephrine may be used instead — it's a much weaker beta-2 agonist, so its alpha-mediated vasoconstriction still wins. Instruct the client to change positions slowly to minimize orthostatic hypotension, and to notify the HCP if chest pain occurs during the IV infusion.

🧠 "Epi backfires, Norepi works." On an alpha-blocker, giving epinephrine for a pressure drop is like stepping on the gas pedal that's disconnected — only the brake-release (β2 dilation) still works.

TEACH

STEP 3 · WHAT THE CLIENT NEEDS TO HEAR

If it's an adrenergic blocker of any kind, orthostatic precautions and "never stop cold turkey" apply.

✅ Universal teaching for any adrenergic blocker

1
🪑 Rise slowly — sit, pause, then stand
2
🩺 Check BP/pulse before dosing per parameters
3
❌ Never stop abruptly — taper under provider guidance
4
📞 Report chest pain, fainting, or new SOB right away
🧠 "Blocked receptors, gradual changes." Whatever adrenergic receptor is blocked, the body needs time to adapt — both starting the drug (slow position changes) and stopping it (taper).

🔗 Where to go deeper

  • NG-141 Adrenergic Alpha Blockers — tamsulosin, doxazosin, prazosin; first-dose orthostatic hypotension
  • NG-160 Prostate – BPH — alpha blockers as first-line BPH therapy
  • NG-260 Peripherally Acting Antiadrenergics — reserpine/guanethidine-class, an older mechanism
  • NG-176 Beta Blockers (Cardio batch) — full cardioselective vs nonselective deep dive
  • NG-243 Clonidine — a central alpha-2 agonist, not a blocker; easy to mix up on exams
🧠 "Antagonist blocks, agonist activates." Adrenergic blockers ≠ clonidine. Say it every time you see the word "adrenergic" on an exam question.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What does "adrenergic antagonist" mean?
A drug that blocks an adrenergic (alpha or beta) receptor, preventing norepinephrine/epinephrine from acting there — the opposite of an agonist.
Q2: Why is epinephrine contraindicated during an alpha-blocker hypotensive crisis?
With alpha blocked, epinephrine's unopposed beta-2 vasodilation can drop BP even further ("epinephrine reversal"). Norepinephrine is used instead because it has minimal beta-2 effect.
Q3: Name two drugs that block BOTH alpha and beta receptors.
Carvedilol (Coreg) and labetalol (Trandate).
Q4: Is clonidine an adrenergic antagonist?
No — clonidine is a central alpha-2 AGONIST. It's a classic mix-up; see NG-243.

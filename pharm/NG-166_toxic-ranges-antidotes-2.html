🔗 Ties to another card in this set

The Acetaminophen (Tylenol) page covers the overdose mechanism (hepatotoxicity) and the full acetylcysteine protocol in depth — this page is the quick-lookup version.

🧠 If a question mentions elevated LFTs after a Tylenol overdose, the answer is almost always acetylcysteine, started as early as possible.

⚠️ Antidotes aren't risk-free

  • Flumazenil can trigger seizures in patients physically dependent on benzodiazepines
  • Protamine sulfate given too fast can cause severe hypotension
  • Naloxone can precipitate acute opioid withdrawal
🧠 Every antidote is still a drug. Reversing one problem can create another — monitor closely after any antidote is given.

THERAPEUTIC RANGES

STEP 2 · NARROW WINDOWS

Standard adult reference ranges for drugs where "a little too much" becomes toxic fast — confirm against your facility's lab before acting clinically.

📈 What "narrow therapeutic index" means

🧠 Narrow window drugs need blood levels drawn — you cannot judge safety by symptoms alone until toxicity has already started.

🧠 These are standard teaching ranges for study purposes — labs, populations, and indications vary. Never use this card alone to make a real dosing decision.

🩸 Peak vs. trough — draw it right

🧠 "Peak after, trough before." Draw the peak shortly after the dose infuses in; draw the trough right before the very next dose is due.

🚨 What raises toxicity risk for ALL of these

  • Renal impairment — most of these drugs (or active metabolites) are cleared by the kidney
  • Hepatic impairment — slows metabolism of many agents
  • Dehydration and drug interactions that change clearance
  • Missed or mistimed lab draws — a level drawn at the wrong time is misleading
🧠 Before trusting any level, ask: was it drawn at the right time, in a patient with normal kidney/liver function?

NURSING PRIORITIES

STEP 3 · CATCH TOXICITY EARLY

Know the toxicity signs for the two highest-yield agents — digoxin and lithium — cold.

❤️ Digoxin toxicity — assess BEFORE you give it

Always check the apical pulse for one full minute before giving digoxin — hold the dose and notify the HCP per facility parameters if it is too low (commonly taught: <60 bpm in adults).

🧠 "Slow heart, weird vision, sick stomach." Any one of the three should make you check a digoxin level before the next dose.

🧠 Lithium toxicity — mild to severe

  • Early: GI upset, fine hand tremor
  • Worsening: ataxia, slurred speech, confusion
  • Severe: seizures, decreased LOC — medical emergency

Adequate sodium and fluid intake matters — dehydration or a low-sodium diet can raise lithium levels toward toxicity.

🧠 Lithium follows salt. Low sodium/dehydration → the body reabsorbs more lithium → level climbs → toxicity risk rises.

✅ General "narrow window" nursing checklist

1
🧪 Confirm the level was drawn at the correct time (peak/trough)
2
📋 Compare the result to the current facility reference range
3
🩺 Correlate with symptoms, not the number alone
4
📞 Hold the dose & notify the HCP if toxic or symptomatic
🧠 Number + symptoms, not number alone. A level can look "in range" and the patient can still be symptomatic if renal/hepatic function changed quickly.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: A patient is found unresponsive with pinpoint pupils and slow, shallow breathing after taking an unknown medication. What antidote do you anticipate?
Naloxone — the classic opioid overdose presentation (respiratory depression + miosis).
Q2: What is the antidote for warfarin (Coumadin) toxicity/overdose?
Vitamin K. It takes hours to work, so FFP or prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) may be used when faster reversal is needed.
Q3: Before giving digoxin, what must the nurse assess, and what's the action if it's abnormal?
Apical pulse for one full minute. If it's too low (commonly taught as under 60 bpm in an adult), hold the dose and notify the HCP.
Q4: Why does a trough level need to be drawn right before the next dose, not any time during the day?
The trough is the drug's lowest point in the blood, right before the next dose is due — drawing it at the wrong time gives a falsely high or low result that can lead to an unsafe dosing decision.

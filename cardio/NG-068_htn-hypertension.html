Hypertension 🩸

HTN — the silent killer, staged, sourced & matched to the right drug class

NG-068 Cardio ADHD-friendly visual edition

Persistently elevated arterial blood pressure — usually silent until it has already damaged an organ. Two big questions drive this whole topic: where is it coming from (primary vs secondary), and which lever fixes it — lifestyle first, then the right drug class for the right patient. This page is the overview that ties your ACE inhibitor, ARB, beta blocker, CCB, clonidine, diuretic & nitrate pages together — it won't re-teach each drug, just where each one fits.

CAUSE

STEP 1 · PRIMARY vs SECONDARY

Same number on the cuff, two very different reasons it got there.

⭐ The core equation — everything else is commentary on this

BP = CO × SVR  (Cardiac Output × Systemic Vascular Resistance)

Anything that raises how much blood the heart pumps out, or how tightly the vessels squeeze around it, raises the pressure. Every risk factor, every cause, and every drug class on this page works by pushing one of those two numbers up or down.

🧠 “Pump harder or squeeze tighter — either one raises the number.” Diuretics and vasodilators attack the equation from opposite sides but land on the same number.

🧭 Primary vs secondary — where the ~90/10 split comes from

🧠 “ROPE” for secondary causes — Renal disease · OSA · Pheochromocytoma / aldosteronism · Endocrine & Estrogen (OCPs, pregnancy). Pull the rope (fix the cause) and the BP often comes down on its own.

⚠️ Modifiable risk factors — the ones she can change

  • 🧂 High-sodium diet
  • ⚖️ Obesity
  • 🛋️ Physical inactivity
  • 🚬 Smoking
  • 🍺 Excess alcohol
  • 😖 Chronic stress, poor sleep
🧠 These six are exactly the six targets of the lifestyle modification card in the CARE section below.

🧬 Non-modifiable risk factors — the ones she can't

  • 📅 Age — risk rises as vessels stiffen
  • 🧬 Family history / genetics
  • 🌍 Race — Black adults have earlier onset, higher rates & often more severe disease
  • 🚻 Sex — higher risk in men at younger ages; women's risk rises after menopause
🧠 Can't change these — so the modifiable list works harder to compensate.

CLUES

STEP 2 · STAGING & TARGET ORGANS

Numbers on a chart — until they've been high long enough to hit an organ.

📏 BP categories — a labeled bar scale

Only one value has to fall in a category to qualify for it — whichever number (systolic or diastolic) is worse determines the stage.

🧠 “130 starts the staging, 180 is a crisis.” Anchor those two numbers and interpolate the rest — but always double-check the exact table your instructor is using.

🚨 Hypertensive crisis — urgency vs emergency

Urgency: severely elevated BP without acute symptoms of organ damage. Usually managed with oral meds and close follow-up.

Emergency: severely elevated BP with acute target-organ damage — chest pain, dyspnea, vision changes, confusion, worsening renal function. This is an ICU-level, IV-drip situation.

🧠 Symptoms are what turn "urgency" into "emergency." Same terrifying number, totally different level of response.

🤫 Why it's the "silent killer"

Most people feel nothing until damage has already started. When symptoms do show up — severe headache, visual changes, nosebleed, dizziness — the pressure is often already very high.

This is exactly why routine screening matters more than waiting for symptoms.

🧠 A disease with no early warning system needs a nurse with a cuff instead.

🗺️ Target organ damage — the body map

🧠 "HEBK" — Heart · Eyes · Brain · Kidneys. Say it like "he broke" — that's exactly what unchecked HTN quietly does.

CARE

STEP 3 · LIFESTYLE + THE RIGHT DRUG

Lifestyle changes come first and stay first — drugs get layered on top, matched to who the patient is.

✅ Lifestyle modification — the five levers

DASH = Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension: fruits, vegetables, whole grains, low-fat dairy, lean protein; low in saturated fat, sodium & added sugar. Sodium restriction guidance commonly cited by the AHA is roughly <2,300 mg/day, with greater BP benefit closer to 1,500 mg/day — confirm the exact figure your course uses. Weight loss and exercise (regular moderate aerobic activity most days of the week) both independently lower BP; alcohol should be kept to a moderate amount or avoided.

🧠 "DASH away the salt, walk off the weight, pour less wine." Five levers, zero prescriptions — and they're never optional just because a drug got added.

⚠️ One NCLEX trap that lives on this page

Never let clonidine stop abruptly — rebound hypertensive crisis. It's the single most-tested safety point that touches this overview; full teaching is on the Clonidine page.

🧠 Clonidine doesn't get to just stop — it gets tapered.

🔁 The theme across every class

Every one of these drug classes attacks BP = CO × SVR from a different angle: diuretics shrink volume, ACEi/ARB relax vessels & protect kidneys, beta blockers slow & soften the heart, CCBs relax vessels or slow the heart depending on type, clonidine turns down sympathetic outflow centrally, and nitrates dilate veins to drop preload.

🧠 Learn the equation once here, then every drug page is just "which side of CO × SVR does this one push?"

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What's the core equation behind every blood pressure number?
BP = Cardiac Output × Systemic Vascular Resistance. Every risk factor and every drug class pushes one of those two numbers.
Q2: A patient has HTN caused by pheochromocytoma. Primary or secondary — and what's different about the plan?
Secondary — there's one identifiable cause. Treating the pheochromocytoma can improve or resolve the BP, unlike primary HTN which is managed, not cured.
Q3: Which four organs make up the target-organ-damage body map?
Heart, Eyes, Brain, Kidneys — "HEBK."
Q4: A patient with diabetes and proteinuria needs an antihypertensive. Which class, and why?
ACE inhibitor or ARB — kidney-protective, reduces proteinuria. See the ACE Inhibitors / ARBs pages for the mechanism and teaching.

Vasopressors 💉

Alpha & beta receptors — the drugs that squeeze

NG-053 Cardio · Pharmacology ADHD-friendly visual edition

Vaso-PRESS-ors PRESS on the blood vessels, increasing blood pressure in order to squeeze oxygen-rich blood back to the CORE of the body and perfuse the vital organs — sort of like squeezing a toothpaste bottle. They do it by activating alpha & beta receptors inside the heart and blood vessels. Indications: raise the BP · shock · cardiac arrest (ACLS).

🐍 Alpha 1 = ANACONDAConstricts vessels → SVR ↑ → BP ↑. Blood is squeezed back to the core.
1️⃣ Beta 1 = 1 HEARTChronotropic (rate ↑) + inotropic (force ↑) → cardiac output ↑.
2️⃣ Beta 2 = 2 LUNGSDilates bronchi & vessels — big balloon. (Albuterol lives here.)
💧 Fluids BEFORE pressorsThen norepinephrine first-line in septic shock. Extravasation antidote = phentolamine.

WHAT IT DOES

STEP 1 · THE RECEPTORS

Four receptors explain every one of these drugs. Learn the receptors and you never have to memorize a drug list again.

🗺️ The receptor map — where each receptor lives and what it does

🧠 “1 heart, 2 lungs, Alpha the Anaconda, Dopamine does the kidneys.” Beta 1 = you have 1 heart. Beta 2 = you have 2 lungs. Alpha 1 wraps the vessels like a snake. Say it four times and it's yours.

⭐ Where each drug LANDS — the receptor matrix

Relative strengths only — the exact effect of dopamine and epinephrine shifts with the infusion rate. No doses are given here on purpose; always titrate to the ordered MAP goal per protocol.

🧠 Read the matrix as a shape. Norepinephrine = mostly squeeze. Dobutamine = mostly force. Epinephrine = everything. Phenylephrine = squeeze only. Vasopressin = a different door entirely.

SOURCE FIX The source lists clonidine as an example of an “alpha antagonist.” Clonidine does lower blood pressure, but it is a central alpha-2 AGONIST — it lowers BP by reducing sympathetic outflow from the brain, not by blocking alpha-1 on the vessel. True alpha-1 antagonists are the “-osin” drugs and phentolamine.

🧠 “AG = ADD. ANT = ANTI.” Two syllables carry half of cardiac pharmacology. Then just ask which receptor? — heart, vessels, or lungs.

🪥 Why it's called a PRESSor

VasoPRESSors PRESS on the vessels. Squeezing narrows the container, so the same volume generates a higher pressure — and blood is pushed inward toward the CORE, where the vital organs are.

The trade-off: the core wins and the periphery loses. Fingers, toes, gut and skin are the tissue you sacrifice to keep the brain, heart and kidneys perfused. That is why you assess extremities every shift on a pressor.

🧠 Squeeze the toothpaste tube. Same paste, narrower tube, more pressure at the opening. And if the tube is empty, squeezing harder only splits the tube — which is exactly why fluids come first.

WHICH DRUG, WHICH SHOCK

STEP 2 · MATCH IT

Pick the drug that fixes the broken part. Every choice on this page reconciles with the hypovolemic, cardiogenic and septic shock pages.

💧 Rule zero: fluids come before pressors

Why: blood pressure is a number; perfusion is flow. Vasoconstricting an under-filled circulation raises the number while cutting flow to the gut, kidneys and extremities — you can normalize a MAP and still let the patient die of tissue ischemia.

The exception: cardiogenic shock, where the tank is already overfull — fluid boluses cause flash pulmonary edema. There you support the pump instead.

🧠 “Fill before you squeeze.” One sentence that answers a whole category of exam questions — and the one thing every shock page on this site repeats.

💊 The main vasopressors — one line each

  • Norepinephrine — alpha-1 +++, beta-1 ++. First-line in septic shock.
  • Epinephrine — alpha-1 +++, beta-1 +++, beta-2 ++. Anaphylaxis & cardiac arrest.
  • Vasopressin — V1 receptors, not adrenergic. Second agent in septic shock.
  • Dopamine — dose-dependent: dopaminergic → beta-1 → alpha-1 as the rate rises. Named on the source card for cardiogenic shock. More tachycardia and arrhythmias.
  • Dobutamine — beta-1 +++. An INOTROPE, not really a pressor — it can lower blood pressure through mild beta-2 dilation.
  • Phenylephrine — pure alpha-1. Squeeze with no direct heart effect.

SOURCE FIX Desmopressin appears in the source's drug list. Desmopressin (DDAVP) is NOT a vasopressor — it is a synthetic ADH analog selective for V2 receptors, used for diabetes insipidus, nocturnal enuresis and von Willebrand disease. The pressor is vasopressin (V1). The two names look alike and do completely different jobs.

🧠 “VasoPRESSin PRESSes. DesmoPRESSin does not.” Vasopressin = V1 = vessels. Desmopressin = V2 = urine (and clotting factor release). One raises pressure, one saves water.

🧠 Pump vs pipes, again. A failing pump needs an inotrope. Failing pipes need a pressor. Naming the broken part picks the drug every single time.

📈 Stage progression — where the vasopressor belongs

🧠 The pressor belongs at stage III, not stage II. If the stem says the patient is still hypotensive after adequate fluid, that phrase is the green light. Without it, the answer is fluid.

WATCH FOR & NURSING CARE

STEP 4 · KEEP THEM SAFE

These are high-alert drugs. The nursing care is as tested as the pharmacology.

🚨 Extravasation — the emergency with an antidote

Why phentolamine? The damage is caused by alpha-1 vasoconstriction strangling the local tissue. Phentolamine is an alpha-adrenergic antagonist — it blocks the very receptor the vasopressor is over-stimulating, so blood flow returns before the tissue dies.

Prevention beats rescue: give vasopressors through a central line whenever possible, on a dedicated lumen and an infusion pump, and assess the site frequently.

🧠 “Phentolamine un-does the anaconda.” Alpha-1 agonist squeezed the tissue shut; the alpha-1 blocker lets go. Never just pull the IV and walk away — you lose your chance to aspirate the drug.

👀 Monitoring while a pressor runs

  • 🎯 Titrate to the ordered MAP — usually ≥65 mmHg. Continuous BP, ideally an arterial line.
  • 📟 Continuous ECG — dysrhythmias and tachycardia are the main dose-limiting effects
  • 🚽 Hourly urine output — the real proof organs are being perfused
  • 🫱 Assess extremities every shift and with every rate change — color, temperature, pulses, cap refill. Digital ischemia is a real complication.
  • 🩸 Glucose — epinephrine raises blood sugar
  • 💉 Always on an infusion pump, dedicated line, and check compatibility
  • 📉 Wean gradually — never stop a vasopressor infusion abruptly; the pressure can crash
🧠 “Numbers on the monitor, fingers on the patient.” A perfect MAP with dusky fingertips means the squeeze has gone too far. Both have to be true.

🧠 Every pressor buys pressure with oxygen. You are asking a starving heart to work harder. That's why the goal is the lowest dose that holds the MAP — and why you fix the cause, not the number.

🎯 Cover & check — 7 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What does each receptor do — alpha-1, beta-1, beta-2, dopaminergic?
Alpha-1: constricts blood vessels (SVR ↑, BP ↑) — "the anaconda." Beta-1: 1 heart — positive chronotropic (rate) and inotropic (force), so cardiac output rises. Beta-2: 2 lungs — dilates bronchi and also skeletal-muscle vessels. Dopaminergic: dilates renal and mesenteric vessels at low dopamine rates.
Q2: Why fluids before pressors?
Blood pressure is a number; perfusion is flow. Vasoconstricting an under-filled circulation raises the displayed MAP while cutting flow to gut, kidneys and extremities. Fill the tank first — except in cardiogenic shock, where the tank is already overfull.
Q3: Why norepinephrine first-line in septic shock?
The defect in septic shock is lost vessel tone (SVR ↓↓) from cytokine-driven vasodilation. Norepinephrine is a potent alpha-1 vasoconstrictor with modest beta-1 support that directly replaces the missing tone, and it causes fewer tachyarrhythmias than dopamine. Vasopressin (V1 receptors — a separate pathway) is the usual second agent.
Q4: Which drug for anaphylaxis, by what route, and why does it work on everything at once?
Epinephrine, intramuscularly into the anterolateral thigh, immediately. Alpha-1 constricts the dilated leaking vessels (BP up, swelling down), beta-1 supports rate and force, beta-2 opens the bronchioles. Never delay it for antihistamines or steroids.
Q5: Is dobutamine a vasopressor?
Not really — it's an INOTROPE. It's almost purely beta-1, so it raises contractility and cardiac output, and its mild beta-2 activity can actually lower blood pressure. In cardiogenic shock it's often paired with norepinephrine when the MAP needs support.
Q6: Vasopressin vs desmopressin?
Vasopressin acts on V1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle and raises blood pressure — it's used in septic shock alongside norepinephrine. Desmopressin (DDAVP) is a V2-selective ADH analog for diabetes insipidus, nocturnal enuresis and von Willebrand disease — it is NOT a vasopressor. The source graphic lists it with the pressors; that is incorrect.
Q7: A norepinephrine infusion has extravasated. Steps and antidote?
Stop the infusion; leave the catheter in place and aspirate residual drug; notify the provider; elevate the limb, mark the border, apply a warm compress; anticipate PHENTOLAMINE infiltrated subcutaneously around the site. Phentolamine is an alpha-blocker, so it reverses the alpha-1 vasoconstriction that is strangling the tissue.

First-degree vs. Wenckebach: watch the PR

First-degree: long, same PR; every P gets a QRS.

Wenckebach: PR gets longer, then a QRS drops; the next PR resets.

Mobitz II: same conducted PR, then sudden dropped QRS.

Third-degree: atria and ventricles have separate clocks.

The small P wave is the atrial signal. The tall QRS is ventricular activation. A “dropped beat” in second-degree AV block means a P wave is present but its QRS does not follow.

Memory-picture nuance: “Same” is the key in Mobitz II; its conducted PR can be normal or prolonged. A short-looking ribbon in the mnemonic does not mean it must be normal.

Read every strip in the same order

  1. Patient first: responsiveness, breathing, pulse, blood pressure and perfusion.
  2. Rate and regularity: compare R–R intervals; count atrial P waves separately when needed. On a 6-second strip, QRS count × 10 estimates ventricular rate. For a regular rhythm: 300 ÷ large boxes between R waves.
  3. P waves: do they look alike? Does each P have a QRS? Are there extra P waves?
  4. PR: measure from the start of P to the start of QRS—not to the R peak. Usual adult PR is 0.12–0.20 second (3–5 small boxes).
  5. QRS: narrow is <0.12 second; wide is ≥0.12 second. Then inspect QT, ST/T and the full 12-lead ECG when appropriate.

Paper key: at 25 mm/s, one small box = 0.04 second; one large box = 0.20 second. Standard gain is 10 mm/mV. Screen resizing changes physical size, so count boxes rather than measuring your screen with a ruler.

All new strips are original, simulated lead-II-style teaching diagrams. Real tracings may include artifact, overlapping waves, variable conduction and different shapes across leads. A single strip cannot show everything a 12-lead ECG shows.

Why bundle branch block is different from AV block

AV block: impaired atrial-to-ventricular conduction; examine P–QRS relationships. Bundle branch block: delayed conduction within the ventricles; examine QRS width and morphology across the 12 leads. A wide QRS alone cannot identify right versus left bundle branch block.

First-degree AV block

Long, fixed PR. Nothing drops.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Every P has a QRS; PR stays >0.20 s.
  • Picture it: A slow open gate: everyone gets through.

Nursing priority: Assess symptoms and review contributing medicines; a stable patient may need observation rather than urgent intervention.

Easy mix-up: A dropped QRS rules out uncomplicated first-degree block.

Reference: Merck Manual: AV block ↗

Mobitz I / Wenckebach

Longer, longer, DROP — reset.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: PR grows across conducted beats; after the nonconducted P, the next PR shortens.
  • Picture it: A stretching ribbon, one closed gate, then start over.

Nursing priority: Assess perfusion. Escalate symptomatic bradycardia; treatment depends on the patient, not the name alone.

Easy mix-up: The P wave still occurs during the pause. It is the QRS that is missing.

Reference: Merck Manual: AV block ↗

Mobitz II

Same PR, sudden missing QRS.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Some P waves fail to conduct without preceding progressive PR lengthening. QRS may be narrow or wide.
  • Picture it: Same-length tickets; a gate suddenly shuts.

Nursing priority: Promptly notify the clinician; monitor closely and prepare for pacing if unstable or ordered. This can progress to complete block.

Easy mix-up: Do not call every dropped beat Wenckebach. Compare the conducted PR intervals.

Reference: Merck Manual: AV block ↗

Third-degree / complete AV block

P and QRS have separate clocks.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: P–P and R–R can each be regular, but P waves have no fixed relationship to QRS complexes. Some P waves hide inside QRS/T.
  • Picture it: Two conductors playing independently.

Nursing priority: Urgent assessment and expert help; prepare pacing support, especially with poor perfusion.

Easy mix-up: Changing apparent PR intervals alone are not Wenckebach: here no atrial impulses conduct.

Reference: Resuscitation Council UK: heart-block examples ↗

2:1 AV block

Every other P conducts.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Only one conducted PR is available between blocked beats. This pattern alone cannot distinguish Mobitz I from Mobitz II.
  • Picture it: Two knocks; one answer. Type still uncertain.

Nursing priority: Assess stability; obtain longer monitoring / 12-lead ECG and expert evaluation.

Easy mix-up: Do not automatically label 2:1 as Mobitz II.

Reference: Resuscitation Council UK: heart-block examples ↗

High-grade AV block

Two or more P waves in a row fail to conduct.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Some atrial impulses still conduct, with consecutive nonconducted P waves between them.
  • Picture it: Several locked gates before one opens.

Nursing priority: Treat as high risk: get help, monitor perfusion, and prepare pacing support as directed.

Easy mix-up: High-grade block is not identical to complete AV dissociation.

Reference: ACC/AHA/HRS: bradycardia and conduction-delay guideline ↗

Normal sinus rhythm

P → QRS → T, repeating evenly.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Adult resting rate 60–100/min, regular; consistent P before every narrow QRS.
  • Picture it: One conductor, one P for each QRS.

Nursing priority: Use this as the comparison pattern; continue clinical assessment.

Easy mix-up: An organized monitor trace does not prove there is a pulse.

Reference: Existing Study Hub: five-step EKG interpretation ↗

Sinus bradycardia

Normal sequence, slower rate.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Sinus pattern with adult rate <60/min.
  • Picture it: The conductor slows down.

Nursing priority: Check blood pressure, mentation and symptoms. If poor perfusion is attributable to bradycardia, follow the bradycardia pathway.

Easy mix-up: A low rate alone is not the same as unstable bradycardia.

Reference: NHLBI: types of arrhythmias ↗

Sinus tachycardia

Normal sequence, faster rate.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Sinus pattern with adult rate >100/min; P waves may merge with the preceding T at higher rates.
  • Picture it: The usual conductor speeds up.

Nursing priority: Look for and address causes such as pain, fever, hypovolemia or hypoxemia.

Easy mix-up: Do not cardiovert compensatory sinus tachycardia.

Reference: AHA 2025: tachyarrhythmia with a pulse algorithm ↗

Respiratory sinus arrhythmia

Sinus beats gradually speed up and slow down.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: R–R changes cyclically, often with respiration; each P still conducts.
  • Picture it: The conductor breathes with the music.

Nursing priority: Correlate with breathing and symptoms; often a normal variant.

Easy mix-up: Unlike AF, organized P waves are present.

Reference: Existing Study Hub: five-step EKG interpretation ↗

Sinus pause / sinus node dysfunction

The expected P wave is missing during the pause.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Sinus node dysfunction can include inappropriate bradycardia, pauses or tachy–brady episodes; it is not one fixed ECG pattern.
  • Picture it: The conductor stops giving the cue.

Nursing priority: Assess symptoms and reversible causes; report symptomatic or prolonged pauses.

Easy mix-up: In AV block, P waves continue through the missing QRS. In this sinus-pause example, P waves pause too.

Reference: NHLBI: conduction disorders and sick sinus syndrome ↗

Premature atrial contraction (PAC)

An EARLY, different-looking P.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: An atrial impulse arrives sooner than expected; the P may be hidden in the preceding T wave.
  • Picture it: An atrial musician enters early.

Nursing priority: Assess symptoms, triggers and frequency; obtain further evaluation when indicated.

Easy mix-up: An early beat is different from a late escape beat.

Reference: American Heart Association: PACs and PVCs ↗

Junctional escape rhythm

Narrow QRS; P may be inverted, hidden or after QRS.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: The AV junction acts as a backup pacemaker, usually 40–60/min for an escape rhythm.
  • Picture it: The backup conductor steps in.

Nursing priority: Check perfusion and why the sinus node is not leading; escalate symptomatic bradycardia.

Easy mix-up: A missing visible P does not automatically mean atrial fibrillation.

Reference: Existing Study Hub: five-step EKG interpretation ↗

Atrial fibrillation (AF / AFib)

Irregularly irregular R–R + no consistent P waves.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: The baseline may have fine fibrillatory activity; ventricular rate varies.
  • Picture it: Musicians out of step; uneven stepping stones.

Nursing priority: Assess stability and rate; follow prescribed rate/rhythm control and stroke-risk anticoagulation plan.

Easy mix-up: QRS complexes remain identifiable. VF has no organized QRS pattern.

Reference: NHLBI: types of arrhythmias ↗

Atrial flutter

Repeating sawtooth flutter waves.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Atrial activity is organized and rapid. Ventricular response can be regular or variable.
  • Picture it: Flutter = a carpenter’s repeating sawteeth.

Nursing priority: Assess perfusion and rate; follow prescribed rate/rhythm and stroke-prevention management.

Easy mix-up: Flutter is not always a regular ventricular rhythm; conduction ratio can change.

Reference: NHLBI: types of arrhythmias ↗

Regular narrow-complex SVT

Very fast, usually regular, narrow complexes.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: P waves may be buried in or near QRS/T; abrupt onset favors reentrant SVT.
  • Picture it: A fast train on a narrow track.

Nursing priority: If stable, follow ordered vagal/medication pathway. If causing instability, prepare synchronized cardioversion.

Easy mix-up: Rate alone cannot identify the mechanism. Do not give adenosine to irregular wide-complex tachycardia.

Reference: AHA 2025: tachyarrhythmia with a pulse algorithm ↗

Premature ventricular contraction (PVC)

An EARLY, wide, different-looking QRS.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Usually followed by a pause; one PVC is not a sustained VT rhythm.
  • Picture it: A wide ventricular car cuts into the line.

Nursing priority: Assess symptoms, oxygenation and potential triggers; report frequent, new or symptomatic ectopy.

Easy mix-up: PACs start with an early P; PVCs start in the ventricles.

Reference: Merck Manual: ventricular premature beats ↗

Unifocal PVCs

The PVCs have the SAME shape in this lead.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Similar PVC morphology suggests the same ventricular focus. Their timing does not have to be regular.
  • Picture it: One focus, one repeating appearance.

Nursing priority: Assess perfusion, symptoms and contributing conditions; report new or frequent ectopy.

Easy mix-up: Unifocal describes appearance/origin, not frequency. Unifocal PVCs can also occur in bigeminy or trigeminy.

Reference: NCBI: PVC morphology and repeating patterns ↗

Multifocal PVCs

The PVCs have DIFFERENT shapes in the same lead.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Different PVC morphologies suggest different ventricular origins; compare within the same lead and verify electrode placement.
  • Picture it: Multiple sources, multiple appearances.

Nursing priority: Promptly report new or complex ventricular ectopy and assess symptoms, oxygenation and possible causes.

Easy mix-up: Do not compare QRS shape across different leads to label PVCs multifocal.

Reference: NCBI: PVC morphology and repeating patterns ↗

Ventricular bigeminy

Normal → PVC → normal → PVC.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: A repeating pair: one normal beat followed by one PVC. The wide early complexes are marked pink.
  • Picture it: BI = two: normal, PVC; normal, PVC.

Nursing priority: Assess the patient and pulse; frequent PVCs may reduce effective output. Report new or symptomatic patterns.

Easy mix-up: Bigeminy is a timing pattern. The PVCs may also be unifocal, as in this example.

Reference: NCBI: PVC morphology and repeating patterns ↗

Ventricular trigeminy

Normal → normal → PVC; repeat.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: A repeating group of three: two normal beats, then one PVC.
  • Picture it: TRI = three: normal, normal, PVC.

Nursing priority: Assess symptoms and perfusion; report new or frequent ectopy and investigate contributing conditions.

Easy mix-up: Trigeminy does not mean three PVCs in a row. Two consecutive PVCs are a couplet; three consecutive ventricular beats form a triplet/run.

Reference: NCBI: PVC morphology and repeating patterns ↗

Monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT)

Fast, broad complexes with similar shape.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Three or more consecutive ventricular beats; assess whether a pulse is present.
  • Picture it: A fast train of wide cars.

Nursing priority: No pulse: CPR + defibrillation pathway. Pulse with instability: synchronized cardioversion pathway.

Easy mix-up: Never decide “shock or no shock” from the word VT without checking the patient.

Reference: AHA 2025: adult cardiac arrest algorithm ↗

Torsades de pointes / polymorphic VT

Complexes appear to twist around the baseline.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Torsades is polymorphic VT associated with prolonged QT; not all polymorphic VT is torsades.
  • Picture it: A twisting ribbon with a long-QT warning.

Nursing priority: Sustained polymorphic VT requires immediate unsynchronized shock. For long-QT torsades, magnesium and correction of causes are directed by the clinical team.

Easy mix-up: Magnesium does not replace defibrillation when sustained polymorphic VT is present.

Reference: AHA 2025: adult advanced life support ↗

Ventricular fibrillation (VF)

Chaotic waves; no organized QRS complexes.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Disorganized ventricular activity, unlike AF where QRS complexes can still be counted.
  • Picture it: An electrical storm stops coordinated pumping.

Nursing priority: Activate the emergency response; begin high-quality CPR and defibrillate promptly per protocol.

Easy mix-up: VF is shockable. Verify apparent artifact without delaying care in an unresponsive pulseless patient.

Reference: AHA 2025: adult cardiac arrest algorithm ↗

Asystole

No ventricular electrical activity.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: Check the patient, connections, gain and another lead to exclude lead failure or fine VF.
  • Picture it: The ventricular music is silent.

Nursing priority: If pulseless: CPR, emergency response, epinephrine and reversible-cause treatment per protocol.

Easy mix-up: Asystole is NOT shockable. Do not delay CPR while checking the monitor.

Reference: AHA 2025: adult cardiac arrest algorithm ↗

Pulseless electrical activity (PEA)

An organized rhythm on the screen, but NO palpable pulse.

Open whole strip in a new tab ↗
  • Look for: PEA is a clinical state, not a unique ECG shape. It may look slow, fast, narrow or wide.
  • Picture it: The monitor has activity; the pulse gauge is empty.

Nursing priority: CPR, emergency response, epinephrine and rapid treatment of reversible causes per protocol.

Easy mix-up: PEA is NOT shockable; the monitor cannot tell you whether circulation is present.

Reference: AHA 2025: adult cardiac arrest algorithm ↗

Clinical note · pulse and perfusion choose the pathway

These emergency priorities reflect the 2025 AHA guidance. Use your course material for exam wording and your facility’s protocol for care.

  • No pulse: call for help and start CPR. VF / pulseless VT are shockable. PEA / asystole are not shockable.
  • Pulse + unstable tachyarrhythmia: prepare synchronized cardioversion. Sustained polymorphic VT requires an unsynchronized shock because reliable synchronization is not possible.
  • Pulse + symptomatic bradycardia: treat causes; follow the atropine / pacing / infusion pathway as directed. High-grade block may need pacing promptly; do not let repeated medication attempts delay needed pacing.
  • Instability: hypotension, acute altered mental status, shock, ischemic chest discomfort or acute heart failure attributable to the rhythm.

A five-minute plan for the blocks
  1. Minute 1: trace P → QRS on first-degree and Wenckebach. Say “same” versus “longer, drop, reset.”
  2. Minute 2: compare Wenckebach and Mobitz II. Point to the PR change—or lack of change.
  3. Minute 3: march P–P and R–R separately on complete block.
  4. Minute 4: answer the five block questions without looking at the labels.
  5. Minute 5: reread only the clue you missed; explain why the closest wrong answer is wrong. Repeat later with the labels hidden.

Endocarditis 🦠

Infective & noninfective inflammation of the heart's INNER lining & valves

NG-229 CARDIO ADHD-friendly visual edition

Inflammation INside the heart — bacteria (or, less often, sterile clot/inflammation with no bacteria) seed the endocardium and valves, growing a vegetation that keeps valves from closing fully. Less cardiac output = less oxygen OUT to the body, plus pieces of that vegetation can break off and embolize to the brain, lungs, and fingertips.

🦠 Bugs on the valveInfective (bacteria — "mold on the valve") vs. noninfective (inflammation, no bacteria).
🩸 Blood cultures BEFORE antibioticsDraw multiple sets from different sites first — don't let the first antibiotic dose sterilize the culture.
🖐️ Four classic exam signsOsler nodes, Janeway lesions, splinter hemorrhages, Roth spots.
🦷 Protect the at-risk valveAntibiotic prophylaxis before dental/procedures; gentle oral care, no flossing.
🧨

CAUSE

STEP 1 · BUGS FIND A DOOR

A valve gets damaged or exposed, bacteria land on it, and a vegetation grows — the whole disease follows from that one event.

🫀 A heart valve with vegetation growth

EXAM TIP Infective endocarditis = bacteria seed a valve (often one already scarred or abnormal), forming a sticky vegetation of fibrin, platelets, and bacteria. The leaflets can't close fully → regurgitation → the vegetation keeps growing and keeps shedding infected emboli.

🧠 "Mold on the valve." Picture the vegetation exactly like fuzzy mold growing on a screen door — the door (valve) can't shut all the way anymore, and little bits of mold keep breaking off and traveling.

⚠️ Causes — how bacteria get in

  • 💉 Dirty needles — IV drug use
  • 🦷 Dental visits without antibiotic prophylaxis in an at-risk client
  • 🔪 Heart surgery — valve replacement & CABG
  • 🤒 Untreated strep throat
🧠 "Four doors bacteria walk through." Needles, teeth, surgery, and an ignored strep infection — every route is a break in a barrier that lets bacteria reach the bloodstream and then the valve.

🔤 Infective vs noninfective

🦠 Infective🧊 Noninfective
Bacteria present — "mold on the valve"No bacteria — sterile inflammation/clot only
Needs antibioticsTreat the underlying cause (e.g., lupus, cancer)
🧠 Say the word: Endo (inside) + card (heart) + itis (inflammation) = inflammation INSIDE the heart — the lining & valves, not the sac around it (that's pericarditis, NG-066) and not just the muscle (myocarditis).
🔎

CLUES

STEP 2 · SPOT IT

One mnemonic for the systemic picture, four unmistakable fingertip-and-eye findings, and the culture rule that changes the whole plan.

🔤 Signs & symptoms — "CLOT"

  • C — Clots in the heart & brain — risk for stroke/CVA; monitor for agitation, change in level of consciousness
  • L — Lung fluid — crackles
  • O — Overheated — fever
  • T — Too little oxygen — low cardiac output

Plus: clubbing fingers, new or changing heart murmur, fatigue, and the four classic peripheral findings below.

🧠 A CLOT is exactly what's forming — on the valve, and sometimes flying to the brain. The mnemonic is the pathophysiology.

🖐️ The four classic exam findings — each one is visually distinct

🔴 Osler nodes

OUCH — painful/tender raised nodules on finger/toe pads. Immune complex deposition.

🟣 Janeway lesions

Painless flat macules on palms/soles — from septic microemboli.

🩸 Splinter hemorrhages

Thin red-brown lines under the nail bed — tiny clots (microemboli).

👁️ Roth spots

Retinal hemorrhages with a pale white center — seen on fundoscopic exam.

🧠 "Osler = Ouch, Janeway = No way (no pain)." Both are red/purple skin lesions from the same immune/embolic process — the ONE thing that tells them apart on an exam is pain. Splinters live under the nail; Roth spots live in the eye.

🧪 Diagnostics: cultures BEFORE antibiotics

Draw blood cultures from multiple sites before the first dose of antibiotics. Giving antibiotics first can sterilize the blood and make it impossible to identify the organism — which is exactly what guides therapy.

  • 🩸 Multiple sets, different venipuncture sites, before treatment starts
  • 🫀 Echocardiogram — visualizes the vegetation directly
  • 🧫 Elevated WBC, inflammatory markers
🧠 "Culture first, cure second." If a question shows antibiotics being hung before cultures are drawn, that's the error to catch.

🚨 Watch for embolic stroke

Pieces of the vegetation can break off and travel to the brain. New agitation or a change in level of consciousness in an endocarditis client is a stroke until ruled out.

🧠 The C in CLOT is not abstract — it's a real embolus that can lodge in the brain within minutes.
🩺

CARE

STEP 3 · CLEAR THE INFECTION, PROTECT THE VALVE

Antibiotics do the heavy lifting; teaching and prophylaxis keep it from happening again.

💊 Treatment

  • 💉 IV antibiotics — organism-specific, typically a prolonged course; started only after cultures are drawn
  • 🔪 Valve repair or replacement for severe damage, large/embolizing vegetations, or heart failure from valve destruction
🧠 "Bugs out, then rebuild." Kill the infection first — surgery on an actively infected valve is reserved for those who need it urgently (large vegetations, heart failure, uncontrolled infection).

🦷 Oral care — brush, don't floss

  • 🪥 Brush 2x/day with a soft toothbrush
  • NO flossing — can introduce bacteria into the bloodstream
  • 🦷 Routine dental care is still important — just done with antibiotic prophylaxis, not skipped
🧠 "Brush soft, skip the string." Any break in the gum line is a door bacteria can walk through straight to a damaged valve.

🦷 Dental & procedure prophylaxis for at-risk clients

Clients with a history of endocarditis, a prosthetic heart valve, certain congenital heart disease, or a cardiac transplant with valve regurgitation need prophylactic antibiotics before dental work or other procedures that can cause bacteremia.

  • 📣 Tell every provider — dentist, surgeon, even routine procedures — about the history of endocarditis
  • 🪪 Consider a medical alert card/bracelet
🧠 "Announce it before they touch you." The single biggest prevention failure is the client not mentioning their history — make that the habit you teach.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What must happen before the first dose of antibiotics is given?
Blood cultures — multiple sets from different sites — must be drawn first, or the organism may never be identified.
Q2: How do you tell Osler nodes apart from Janeway lesions?
Osler nodes are painful/tender nodules on the finger or toe pads. Janeway lesions are painless flat macules on the palms or soles.
Q3: What does the CLOT mnemonic stand for?
Clots in the heart & brain (stroke risk) · Lung fluid (crackles) · Overheated (fever) · Too little oxygen (low cardiac output).
Q4: Which clients need antibiotic prophylaxis before dental visits or procedures?
Those with a history of endocarditis, a prosthetic heart valve, certain congenital heart disease, or a cardiac transplant with valve regurgitation — and they should tell every provider about that history.

ACE Inhibitors 🫀

Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors — the "-PRIL" drugs

NG-091 CARDIO ADHD-friendly visual edition

Block ACE, the enzyme that converts Angiotensin I → Angiotensin II — upstream in the RAAS pathway. Less angiotensin II means less vasoconstriction and less aldosterone, so blood pressure drops. Because ACE also breaks down bradykinin, blocking it lets bradykinin build up — that's the signature dry cough & angioedema that ARBs do NOT cause.

🔗 Paired with NG-137 ARBs — same pathway, same job, constantly confused on exams. Open the ARB page → to see the side-by-side comparison and the shared RAAS diagram.

🤧 Dry cough / angioedemaBradykinin builds up because ACE can't break it down anymore. ARBs don't do this.

WHAT IT DOES

STEP 1 · THE MECHANISM

One pathway explains the whole drug class — block the enzyme, and everything downstream of it shrinks.

🧬 The RAAS pathway — where ACE inhibitors block it

EXAM TIP ACE inhibitors block Angiotensin I → Angiotensin II, the conversion step. This is the SAME shared diagram used on the ARB page — compare where each class blocks the cascade.

🧠 "PRIL blocks the Pril-conversion, not the reception." ACE inhibitors act upstream, at the enzyme — angiotensin II is never made. ARBs act downstream, at the receptor — angiotensin II is made but can't bind. Same ending pathway, different blockade point.

🔤 Say the suffix — it tells you the class

  • -PRIL = ACE inhibitor 🫀
  • Lisinopril (Prinivil, Zestril)
  • Enalapril (Vasotec) — also comes IV as enalaprilat
  • Captopril (Capoten) — shortest half-life, dosed 2–3×/day
  • Ramipril (Altace)
🧠 "PRIL" sounds like "drill" — it drills into the ACE enzyme upstream, before angiotensin II ever exists.

⭐ Why we give it — indications

  • Hypertension — first-line in many guidelines
  • Heart failure — reduces afterload & remodeling
  • Post-MI — cardioprotective
  • Diabetic nephropathy — protects the kidney by lowering intraglomerular pressure
🧠 "Pril protects the kidney AND the heart." One drug, two organs it's guarding — that's why it's a diabetic/CKD favorite even at normal BP.

WATCH FOR

STEP 2 · THE DANGER LIST

The cough is annoying, the angioedema is an airway emergency, and the potassium creeps up quietly.

🚨 Angioedema — airway emergency

Angioedema can happen with the first dose or years into therapy. It is bradykinin-driven, not allergic in the usual IgE sense — antihistamines and epinephrine may not fully reverse it. Higher risk in Black patients and those with a prior history of angioedema on any ACE inhibitor.

🧠 "No PRIL, ever again." A client with ACE-inhibitor angioedema does not get re-challenged with any -pril — switch classes (often to an ARB, though ARBs carry a small residual risk too).

🧎 First-dose orthostatic hypotension

Biggest drop after the very first dose. Give it at bedtime the first time, and teach the client to rise slowly — sit on the edge of the bed before standing.

🧠 "First pril, first fall risk." Same first-dose orthostatic teaching applies to ARBs — it's a RAAS-wide effect.

🍌 Hyperkalemia risk

Aldosterone normally makes the kidney excrete K⁺. Block aldosterone → K⁺ stays in → hyperkalemia. Watch peaked T waves on ECG. Avoid stacking with K⁺-sparing diuretics, K⁺ supplements, and salt substitutes (often potassium chloride).

🧠 "Pril keeps potassium." Teach clients to check the ingredient label on salt substitutes before assuming "salt-free" is safe.

❌ Contraindications — absolute no

🤰Pregnancy2nd/3rd trimester — fetal renal injury/death
🫁Angioedema hxwith any ACE inhibitor
🩸Bilateral renalartery stenosis
🍌Hyperkalemiapre-existing
💧Volume depletionsevere dehydration
🧠 "No PRIL in pregnancy — ever." Confirm pregnancy status before starting, and stop immediately if pregnancy is discovered.

TEACH

STEP 3 · WHAT THE CLIENT NEEDS TO HEAR

Get up slowly, skip the salt substitute, report the cough — and never double up on labs.

✅ Teaching checklist

1
🧎 Rise slowly — sit, then stand, especially the first few doses
2
🧂 Avoid salt substitutes — many contain potassium chloride
3
🧪 Routine labs — potassium & renal function (BUN/creatinine)
4
🤧 Report a dry, hacking cough — may need to switch to an ARB
5
🚨 Report facial/lip swelling or trouble breathing immediately
🧠 "SALT-K-COUGH" — Slowly rise · Avoid salt subs · Labs (K⁺/renal) · Tell them about the cough.

💊 Drug interactions to flag

  • NSAIDs — blunt the antihypertensive effect, raise renal injury risk
  • Potassium-sparing diuretics / K⁺ supplements — additive hyperkalemia
  • Lithium — decreased clearance → possible lithium toxicity
  • Diuretics (loop/thiazide) — additive hypotension, especially the first dose
🧠 "Pril + NSAID = pressure won't fall, kidneys might." NSAIDs fight the BP-lowering effect and stress the kidney at the same time.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: Where in the RAAS pathway do ACE inhibitors act?
Upstream, at the ACE enzyme — they block the conversion of Angiotensin I to Angiotensin II.
Q2: Why do ACE inhibitors cause a dry cough but ARBs don't?
ACE also breaks down bradykinin. Blocking ACE lets bradykinin build up, causing cough and angioedema. ARBs act at the receptor and never touch bradykinin.
Q3: A client on lisinopril reports lip and tongue swelling. What's the priority?
Angioedema — an airway emergency. Stop the drug, protect the airway, notify the provider immediately, and never re-challenge with another ACE inhibitor.
Q4: What should the client avoid to prevent hyperkalemia?
Salt substitutes (often potassium chloride), potassium supplements, and potassium-sparing diuretics without provider guidance — and get routine K⁺/renal labs.

Ino · Chrono · Dromotropic 🫀

Three suffixes that decode every cardiac drug question you'll ever see

NG-152 Cardio ADHD-friendly visual edition

Every cardiac drug — digoxin, beta blockers, CCBs, epinephrine, atropine, adenosine — can be fully described by what it does to three things: how hard the heart squeezes, how fast it beats, and how quickly the signal crosses the AV node. Learn these three words and the direction arrows, and every other cardiac drug page starts making sense together.

💪 INO = forceInotropic = strength of contraction. Positive ↑ squeeze, negative ↓ squeeze.
⏱️ CHRONO = rateChronotropic = heart rate. Positive ↑ HR, negative ↓ HR. Chronos = time.
🛣️ DROMO = AV node speedDromotropic = conduction speed through the AV node. Positive ↑ speed, negative ↓ speed.
📋 Digoxin breaks the pattern+ Inotropic but – Chronotropic and – Dromotropic — the one drug that doesn't move all three the same way.

DEFINE

STEP 1 · THE THREE WORDS

Same root pattern every time: what it means, then which direction is "positive."

💪 Inotropic — force of contraction

Ino- refers to muscle fiber/strength. Inotropic effects change how hard the ventricle squeezes with each beat — this is contractility, which drives stroke volume.

🧠 "Ino = the squeeze." Positive inotropes are the drugs you reach for when the heart isn't squeezing hard enough (cardiogenic shock, acute HF).

⏱️ Chronotropic — heart rate

Chrono- = time (chronology). Chronotropic effects change how many times per minute the heart beats — driven mainly by the SA node.

🧠 "Chrono = the clock." Positive chronotropes speed the clock (atropine, epinephrine); negative chronotropes slow it (beta blockers, digoxin).

🛣️ Dromotropic — conduction speed through the AV node

Dromo- = running/racetrack (like a hippodrome). Dromotropic effects change how fast the electrical signal travels through the AV node on its way from the atria to the ventricles — the AV node is the "gatekeeper" between them.

🧠 "Dromo = the road." Think hippodrome — a racetrack. Negative dromotropes put up a toll booth at the AV node; positive dromotropes open the highway.

🔤 Word roots — why the syllables mean what they mean

  • Ino- — Greek is / inos, "fiber, muscle" → force of the squeeze
  • Chrono- — Greek chronos, "time" → rate, the clock
  • Dromo- — Greek dromos, "running, racetrack" → speed through the AV node
  • -tropic — "turning toward, affecting"
🧠 "Force, Clock, Track." Three roots, three totally separate jobs — never let one drug's effect on one blur into your answer for another.

🧠 Force, rate, and speed can move independently. That's exactly why digoxin (↑ force, ↓ rate, ↓ speed) is the drug that trips people up — see the TABLE section below.

🚨 The digoxin hold parameter comes from this page

Digoxin is negative chronotropic — it slows the heart on purpose. That's exactly why you check an apical pulse for a full minute before giving it, and hold the dose and notify the provider if the apical pulse is below 60 bpm in an adult.

🧠 Slow drug + slow heart = don't stack them. The chronotropic direction of the drug is the nursing safety check.

⚠️ NCLEX trap: stacking negative drugs

Digoxin + a beta blocker, or digoxin + a non-dihydropyridine CCB (verapamil/diltiazem), both push chronotropic and dromotropic effects further negative — additive risk of severe bradycardia or heart block.

🧠 If two drugs both live on the "negative" side of the same dial, expect the effect to add up — not cancel out.

🧪 Dihydropyridine vs non-dihydropyridine CCBs

Not all CCBs behave the same on this table. Non-dihydropyridines (verapamil, diltiazem) act directly on the heart — negative triad, just like a beta blocker. Dihydropyridines (amlodipine, nifedipine) mainly relax peripheral vessels and have little direct cardiac effect.

🧠 Full mechanism and teaching for both types live on the Calcium Channel Blockers page — this table is only the ino/chrono/dromo summary.

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What does "dromotropic" mean, and where does the effect happen?
The speed of electrical conduction through the AV node — the gatekeeper between the atria and ventricles.
Q2: A patient is on digoxin. What are its three effects?
Positive inotropic (↑ contractility), negative chronotropic (↓ heart rate), negative dromotropic (↓ AV conduction) — the one drug where the arrows split.
Q3: Why is adenosine effective at terminating SVT?
It is profoundly negative dromotropic — it transiently blocks conduction through the AV node almost completely, interrupting the reentry circuit.
Q4: Before giving digoxin, what apical pulse threshold makes a nurse hold the dose and notify the provider in an adult?
Below 60 bpm — because digoxin is already negative chronotropic, stacking it on an already-slow heart risks further bradycardia.

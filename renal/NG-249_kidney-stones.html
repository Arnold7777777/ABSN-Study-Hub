Kidney Stones 🪨

Renal calculi · urolithiasis · lithotripsy & nephrostomy

NG-249 Renal ADHD-friendly visual edition

Renal calculi = a hard, calcified stone in the renal system — usually made of calcium. The pain is famous: “like a knife in the back,” equivalent to childbirth. Three nursing moves carry the whole topic: relieve the pain 💉, strain ALL the urine 🧺, and flood them with fluid 💧.

🪨 “LITH” = stoneUrolithiasis · Nephrolithiasis · Lithotripsy — all the same rock.
🔪 Knife-in-the-back painFlank pain radiating to the groin, in waves. PRIORITY = relieve pain.
🧺 Strain ALL urineEvery void, every time. The stone is the diagnosis.
💧 3 L/day + WALKFluids flush it, ambulation moves it. Never bed rest. Never massage.
🪨

THE STONE

STEP 1 · WHAT & WHERE

Name the stone by where it sits, then learn the four kinds — because the diet changes with the kind.

🔤 Say the word — it tells you the location

WordMeansWhere
Renal calculiHard stone calcified in the renal systemKidney
Renal lithiasisLith = stoneStone in the renal (kidney)
UrolithiasisStone in the urinary systemAnywhere in the tract
UreterolithiasisStone in the ureterThe tubes connecting the kidney & bladder — where the worst pain comes from
NephrolithotomyNephro = kidney · tomy = cutSurgery to remove the stone
🧠 Any word with “LITH” = a stone. Think monolith — a giant rock. Lithotripsy = "stone crushing." Learn one root, decode five words.

📍 Where stones get stuck — the 3 narrow points of the ureter

A stone floating in the kidney can be silent. Pain starts when it enters the ureter and jams at one of three natural narrowings. Where it sticks decides where it hurts.

🧠 “Three toll booths on the ureter.” 🚧 Top (UPJ) = flank. Middle (iliac crossing) = lower belly. Bottom (UVJ) = groin + gotta go. The lower the stone, the lower the pain.

🔬 The FOUR stone types — the type picks the diet

The trap: people assume a calcium stone means "stop eating calcium." Do NOT slash dietary calcium — low calcium in the gut lets more oxalate get absorbed and can make stones worse. Restrict oxalate and animal protein, keep calcium normal, unless the provider orders otherwise.

🧠 “CUSC” — Calcium · Uric acid · Struvite · Cystine. Say it "cusk." And pH: Uric acid loves ACID; Struvite loves ALKALINE. (U-A and S-A… Uric = Under 5.5, Struvite = Super alkaline.)

⚠️ Who gets stones — the risk list

  • 💧 Dehydration — the #1 modifiable cause. Concentrated urine crystallizes.
  • 🥩 High animal protein & high sodium diet
  • 🦠 Recurrent UTIs (struvite)
  • 🦶 Gout / high purine (uric acid)
  • 🦴 Hyperparathyroidism & prolonged immobility — both dump calcium into the blood and urine
  • 👨‍👩‍👧 Family history · previous stone (recurrence is common)
  • 🌡️ Hot climates & heavy sweating
🧠 Immobility makes stones. Bones dissolve into the blood when nobody's weight-bearing — that calcium ends up in the urine. It's also why we walk stone clients.

🧠 The mechanism in 4 beats

💧 Low urine volume / high solute load
▼
🧂 Salts supersaturate and crystallize
▼
🪨 Crystals clump into a stone
▼
🚧 Stone enters the ureter ➜ obstruction + spasm = COLIC
🧠 Think sweet tea. Too much sugar, not enough water = crystals at the bottom of the glass. Kidneys work the same way.
🔎

CLUES

STEP 2 · SPOT IT

Extreme wave-like pain plus blood in the urine — and a fever that changes everything.

🔪 EXTREME PAIN — “like a knife in the back,” equivalent to childbirth

🧠 “Colic = a stone rolling in a spasming tube.” The waves come from the ureter squeezing around the rock. Restless + writhing = renal colic. Still as a statue = peritonitis. That single contrast answers a lot of questions.

🧪 Urinalysis: HEMATURIA NCLEX TIP

Answer first: the sharp stone scrapes the ureter lining ➜ blood in the urine. It may be visible (gross) or only on the dipstick/micro.

  • 🩸 RBCs — the classic finding
  • 💎 Crystals — can name the stone type
  • 📊 pH — <5.5 acidic hints uric acid; >7 alkaline hints struvite or calcium phosphate
  • 🦠 WBCs / bacteria / nitrites = infection on top of the stone
🧠 Sharp rock, scraped pipe, bloody urine.

🔎 The rest of the picture

  • 🤢 Nausea & vomiting, diaphoresis, pallor — the pain is that severe
  • 🚽 Urgency, frequency, dysuria when the stone is low (near the bladder)
  • 📉 Oliguria or anuria = obstruction — report immediately
  • 🫃 Abdominal distention, decreased bowel sounds (reflex ileus)
  • 🌡️ Fever & chills = INFECTION — emergency
🧠 A stone with a fever is a surgical emergency — an obstructed, infected kidney can go to urosepsis fast.

🖥️ How they find it

TestWhat it does
Non-contrast CT of the abdomen/pelvis ⭐The gold standard — sees essentially all stones, including uric acid stones that x-ray misses. No contrast needed.
KUB x-rayKidney-Ureter-Bladder film. Shows radiopaque (calcium, struvite) stones; misses uric acid stones.
Renal ultrasoundNo radiation — preferred in pregnancy and children; shows hydronephrosis.
Urinalysis + urine cultureHematuria, crystals, pH, and any infection.
Stone analysis 🧺The strained stone is sent to the lab — that result decides the lifelong diet.
BUN / creatinineChecks whether the obstruction is hurting kidney function.
🧠 CT sees everything; x-ray misses uric acid. If the question says "the stone didn't show on x-ray," you're looking at a uric acid stone.

BEFORE — CONSERVATIVE CARE

STEP 3 · PAIN · FLUIDS · STRAIN

Most small stones pass on their own. Your job before any procedure is six numbered actions.

✅ Priority intervention for urinary calculi with flank pain = RELIEVING PAIN CAUTION

1
💉 Administer analgesics. Pain is the priority. Opioids and/or NSAIDs (e.g. ketorolac) per order — NSAIDs also reduce the ureteral swelling. Give antiemetics for the vomiting. Give it on a schedule, not "as the pain returns."
2
💧 Fluid intake 3 L/day (oral and/or IV as ordered) — flushes the stone down and dilutes the urine so new ones can't form.
3
🧺 STRAIN ALL URINE for stones. Every void, every time, into the strainer — the stone goes to the lab.
4
🚶 AMBULATION — “walk & move.” Gravity and movement help the stone travel down.
5
🛏️ NOT bed rest. Bed rest is the wrong answer — immobility pulls more calcium out of the bones and makes stones worse.
6
🙅 NEVER massage the flank or abdomen. Never massage.
🧠 “Pain · Pour · Pan · Pace — no Pillow, no Pressing.” Analgesia, 3 L, strainer, walk — and the two NEVERs: no bed rest, no massage.

🧺 How to strain urine — teach it exactly NCLEX TIP

🧠 “No stone, no answer.” If the stone goes down the toilet, nobody ever learns what kind it was — and she can't be taught the right diet.

💊 Meds that help it pass

  • Alpha blockers — e.g. tamsulosin — relax the smooth muscle of the distal ureter so the stone slips through. Teach: orthostatic hypotension, change positions slowly.
  • NSAIDs — e.g. ketorolac — pain + swelling. Caution with impaired kidney function.
  • Opioids — for severe colic per order.
  • Antiemetics — the vomiting is part of the picture.
  • Allopurinol — lowers uric acid for uric-acid stone formers.
🧠 “-osin loosens.” Tamsulosin relaxes the ureter — and it drops the blood pressure on standing.

📋 Pre-procedure prep (any stone procedure)

  • 📝 Informed consent signed & on the chart
  • 🍽️ NPO per order if sedation/anesthesia is planned
  • 🩸 Labs: CBC, coagulation studies, BUN/creatinine; hold anticoagulants per order
  • 🦠 Treat UTI first — procedures on an infected tract can trigger sepsis
  • 🤰 Pregnancy status; allergy check (contrast, shellfish/iodine as applicable)
  • 💬 Teach what to expect afterward — this is where the exam questions live
🧠 Consent · NPO · Labs · No infection. The universal pre-procedure four.

DURING — THE PROCEDURES

STEP 4 · BREAK IT · GRAB IT · SUCK IT OUT

Three ways to get a stone out, ranked from least to most invasive.

⚡ Shockwave lithotripsy (ESWL) — shock waves break LARGE stones into smaller stones that can be easily passed

Expected finding after lithotripsy? Stone fragments in the urine. That is the goal, not a complication.

🧠 “Litho-TRIP-sy = TRIP the stone and break it.” Big rock in ➜ gravel out. If the stone doesn't break the first time, the procedure can be repeated.

🪛 Percutaneous nephrolithotomy / nephrolithotripsy (PCNL) — the needle & scope

What it is: Also called nephrolithotomy — the HCP sticks a needle & scope into the kidney through the back (flank) to suck out the stones. Used for large or staghorn stones that shock waves can't clear.

🧠 “Percutaneous = punch through the skin.” And the one-line teaching point: teach the client with a new nephrostomy tube to report BACK PAIN to the provider — back pain means urine has nowhere to go. 🔒

🎣 Ureteroscopy + stone basket

A thin scope goes up through the urethra and bladder into the ureter. The stone is grabbed with a basket or lasered into fragments — no incision at all.

  • A ureteral stent is often left to hold the ureter open
  • Expect flank discomfort, urgency, frequency and pink urine while the stent is in
  • Teach the client to keep the stent removal appointment
🧠 Scope up, basket out. The stent is a temporary drinking straw holding the ureter open.

🪜 Which procedure, in order of invasiveness

💧 Pass it — fluids, pain control, tamsulosin, strain
▼
⚡ ESWL — shock waves from outside, no incision
▼
🎣 Ureteroscopy — scope up the natural path, basket/laser
▼
🪛 PCNL — flank incision into the kidney, for big/staghorn stones
🧠 Least cutting first. Size and location choose the procedure — big stone, bigger approach.

AFTER — EDUCATE

STEP 5 · NORMAL vs NOT NORMAL

This is the single most tested block: what she should expect, and what earns a phone call.

🚦 After lithotripsy — NORMAL vs NOT NORMAL ATI

🧠 “Bruise & blood = fine. Fever & no flow = phone.” Bruising and a day of pink urine are the receipt for the shock waves. A fever or no urine is never expected.

🔧 Nephrostomy tube care — PRIORITY: maintain tube patency CAUTION

1
💧 Irrigate ONLY with an order, using STERILE normal saline, small volume, gently. Never force against resistance.
2
🔒 NEVER clamp a nephrostomy tube — the kidney has no other way out.
3
🎒 Keep the bag BELOW the level of the kidney; secure the tube so it can't be pulled.
4
👀 Expect SEDIMENT in the bottom of the bag — those are the loose fragments draining out.
5
📞 Teach the client to REPORT BACK PAIN — new back/flank pain means the tube is clogged and urine is backing up. Also report no drainage, fever, or leaking around the site.
6
📏 Record output from the tube SEPARATELY from voided urine.
🧠 “Clog ➜ backup ➜ BACK PAIN.” Sediment in the bag is good news; pain in the back is bad news. Same tube, opposite meanings.

🍽️ Diet — restrict animal protein, limit purines

RuleDetails
Restrict protein 🥩“Animal meats.” Animal protein makes the urine acidic and raises calcium & uric acid excretion. Key urolithiasis teaching = restriction of animal protein.
Limit purines 🍺Red meats, organ meats, beer — plus sardines, anchovies, gravy. Purines break down into uric acid.
Limit sodium 🧂High sodium pushes more calcium into the urine.
Limit oxalate 🥬Spinach, rhubarb, beets, nuts, chocolate, strong tea, sweet potatoes — for calcium oxalate formers.
Keep calcium NORMAL 🥛Do not slash dietary calcium unless ordered — low gut calcium = more oxalate absorbed = more stones.
Fluids 3 L/day 💧The single most important lifelong habit. Urine should look pale straw. Drink extra in heat and with exercise, and some at night.
🧠 “Meat makes rocks.” 🥩➜🪨 Cut the animal protein, cut the purines, cut the salt — and drink until the urine is almost clear.

📣 Discharge teaching checklist

  • 💧 3 L of fluid a day, mostly water, spread across the day
  • 🧺 Keep straining until told to stop — fragments pass for days
  • 🚶 Stay active; no bed rest
  • 💊 Finish antibiotics; take pain meds before the pain peaks
  • 🌡️ Call for fever/chills, no urine output, uncontrolled pain, heavy bleeding
  • 🔁 Stones recur — keep follow-up appointments and get the stone analyzed
🧠 Water is the cheapest prescription in this whole chapter.

🚨 Complications to watch for

  • Obstruction ➜ hydronephrosis — urine backs up and swells the kidney; can permanently damage it
  • Infection ➜ urosepsis — fever, chills, hypotension, confusion. Emergency.
  • Hemorrhage — especially after PCNL; watch VS, Hgb, flank swelling
  • Recurrence — very common without a diet & fluid change
🧠 Blocked + infected = the kidney is on a clock. That combination goes to the OR, not to the med list.

🎯 Cover & check — 7 rapid-fire questions
Q1: A client arrives with severe right flank pain from urinary calculi. What is the priority intervention?
Relieving the pain — administer the ordered analgesics (opioid and/or NSAID) plus an antiemetic. Then fluids 3 L/day, strain all urine, and ambulate.
Q2: What is the expected finding in the urine after lithotripsy?
Stone fragments. Also expected: bruising and pain over the flank, and blood in the urine for up to 24 hours. NOT expected: fever or chills, inability to urinate, uncontrolled pain.
Q3: A client has a new nephrostomy tube. What one symptom must they be taught to report?
BACK PAIN — it means the tube is clogged and urine is backing up in the kidney. Priority for the nurse is maintaining tube patency: irrigate only as ordered with sterile normal saline, never clamp the tube, keep the bag below the kidney. Sediment in the bag is expected.
Q4: Which two things should never be part of the stone care plan?
Bed rest (ambulation helps the stone move and prevents calcium loss from bone) and massaging the flank/abdomen. Never massage.
Q5: Key urolithiasis diet teaching?
Restriction of animal protein. Also limit purines (red meat, organ meat, beer), limit sodium and oxalate, keep dietary calcium normal, and drink 3 L of fluid daily.
Q6: Where do stones typically lodge, and how does pain change with location?
The three narrow points of the ureter: the ureteropelvic junction (flank/CVA pain), where the ureter crosses the iliac vessels at the pelvic brim (pain into the lower abdomen and groin), and the ureterovesical junction (groin/testicle/labia pain plus urgency and frequency). The lower the stone, the lower the pain.
Q7: A stone client spikes a temperature of 102°F with chills. Why does this change everything?
An obstructed kidney plus infection is a urologic emergency — it can progress to urosepsis. Report immediately; the obstruction usually has to be relieved (stent or nephrostomy) along with antibiotics.

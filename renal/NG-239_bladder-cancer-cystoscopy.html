Bladder Cancer 🎗️

& Cystoscopy — the scope that looks inside the bladder

Two facts carry this whole page. The most common finding of bladder cancer is PAINLESS HEMATURIA — blood with no burning, no pain, no warning. And cystoscopy is a scope inserted through the urethra to view the bladder — the exam tests what you teach before it and what you report after it. 🔦

CAUSE

STEP 1 · WHAT STARTS IT

Whatever the body filters out ends up sitting in the bladder — and the bladder lining soaks in it.

🚬 The three causes from the source — and why they work

🚬Tobacco usethe #1 risk factor
🧪Chemical exposuredyes · rubber · paint
👪Family historygenetics

Mechanism — say it in one sentence: carcinogens are filtered out of the blood into the urine, then SIT in the bladder and bathe the lining. The longer the urine sits, the longer the contact.

🧠 “The bladder is a holding tank.” 🪣 Anything the kidney throws away parks there. Smoke → blood → kidney → tank. That's why tobacco — an inhaled poison — causes a urinary cancer.

⚠️ The fuller risk list

  • 🚬 Tobacco use — smoking is the biggest single risk factor
  • 🏭 Occupational chemicals — aromatic amines and dyes: painters, hairdressers, and rubber, leather, textile & chemical workers
  • 👪 Family history
  • 👴 Age over ~55; more common in men
  • 🔁 Chronic bladder irritation — recurrent UTIs, long-term indwelling catheters, stones
  • 💊 Prior pelvic radiation or certain chemo agents (e.g. cyclophosphamide)
🧠 "Smoke, stain, and strain." Smoke = tobacco · Stain = dyes/chemicals · Strain = chronic irritation.

🧱 Where the tumour sits — the bladder wall in layers

🧠 How deep = how big the surgery. Surface tumour = scope & scrape. Into the muscle = the bladder may have to come out.
🔎

CLUES

STEP 2 · THE ONE SIGN

If you remember nothing else on this page, remember two words: painless hematuria.

🩸 PAINLESS HEMATURIA — the most common finding MOST TESTED

Answer first: asked “What is the most common finding of bladder cancer?” → painless hematuria. The blood may be bright red, tea-colored, or only visible on the dipstick, and it often comes and goes — which is exactly why people ignore it.

🧠 “No pain, still a problem.” Everyone assumes blood + no pain = "it's nothing." That assumption is exactly what the question is testing. Painless = look inside.

🔬 DIAGNOSTIC: cystoscopy — the gold standard

Cystoscopy = a scope inserted through the urethra to view the bladder. It's the definitive test because the provider can see the tumour AND biopsy it in the same visit.

  • Urine cytology — looks for cancer cells in the urine
  • CT urogram / ultrasound — staging & upper tract
  • Biopsy / TURBT — the tissue diagnosis
🧠 “Cysto = bladder, scopy = look.” Same root as cystitis (bladder inflammation).

🩸 Three causes of hematuria — tell them apart

Never chart adult gross hematuria as "no action needed" just because the client denies pain — painless is the whole point.

🧠 “Burns = bugs. Waves = rocks. Silence = scope.” Three kinds of bloody urine, three different next steps.

⭐ The exam's favorite distractors

  • ❌ "Painful hematuria" → that's a UTI or a stone, not cancer.
  • ❌ "Hematuria only with exercise" → not the classic sign.
  • ❌ "Wait and see if it clears" → never. Gross hematuria in an adult gets worked up.
  • ✅ "Painless hematuria" → the answer, every time.
🧠 Cover the word painless with your thumb and the question falls apart. That one word IS the answer.

BEFORE THE CYSTOSCOPY

STEP 3 · PREP & TEACH

Consent, an empty bladder, and one exact sentence the exam wants you to say.

💬 Cystoscopy teaching: “You will feel PRESSURE during insertion of the scope.” HESI

Not "you won't feel anything" (false reassurance) and not "it will be very painful" (fear). Pressure is honest and accurate — and it's the option to pick.

1
📝 Verify informed consent is signed and on the chart.
2
🍽️ NPO status per order — usually only if general or spinal anesthesia is planned. A simple office cystoscopy is often done with local anesthetic gel and no NPO.
3
🚽 Have the client empty the bladder right before the procedure (unless a urine specimen is being collected then).
4
🧪 Check labs & meds — coagulation studies; anticoagulants held per order; treat any known UTI first.
5
🛏️ Explain the position — lithotomy (on the back, legs in stirrups) and that sterile drapes will be used.
6
⏱️ Set expectations for after — burning, pink urine and frequent urges for a day or two are normal (see AFTER).
🧠 “Pressure, not pain.” Say it out loud once. Any answer choice promising no sensation, or predicting severe pain, is wrong.

🚻 Why the urethra matters — male vs female

🧠 Long road, more pressure. That's why the male client feels more of the "pressure" you warned him about — and why retention after the procedure is watched more closely.

🩺 Baseline you must have

  • Vital signs — especially temperature, so you can tell a new fever from a baseline one
  • Last void & usual voiding pattern
  • Allergies (including latex & anesthetics)
  • Current urine appearance — so post-procedure change is obvious
  • Anticoagulant & antiplatelet use
🧠 You can't call it "new" if you never wrote down the old.

🗣️ Answer their real question

  • "Will I be asleep?" → depends on the type; many are done awake with local gel.
  • "How long?" → the look itself is brief; longer if a biopsy or tumour resection is done.
  • "Will it hurt to pee after?" → burning and pink urine for a day or two are expected.
  • "Can I drive home?" → not if sedated — arrange a ride.
🧠 Prepared clients report symptoms sooner. Teaching before is what makes the "call us if…" list stick.

DURING — THE SCOPE

STEP 4 · WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENS

Sterile, through the natural opening, bladder filled with fluid so the walls can be seen.

🔦 A scope inserted through the urethra to view the bladder

🧠 Picture a flashlight going up a garden hose into a water balloon. 🔦 The balloon has to be filled to see its walls — that's why the client feels pressure and a strong urge to void.

🔪 TURBT — when the scope also treats

Transurethral resection of bladder tumour. Same route as a cystoscopy, but the tumour is shaved off the bladder wall and sent to pathology. It both stages and treats non-muscle-invasive disease.

  • Expect a catheter afterward, sometimes with continuous bladder irrigation (CBI)
  • More bleeding than a plain look — watch for clots
🧠 Look = cystoscopy. Look + shave = TURBT.

💉 Intravesical BCG — chemo/immunotherapy INTO the bladder

For non-muscle-invasive tumours, medication is instilled through a catheter into the bladder rather than given IV.

  • ⏳ The client holds it in the bladder for the ordered dwell (commonly about 2 hours) and changes position so it touches all the walls
  • 🚽 Sit to void afterward to avoid splashing
  • 🧴 Disinfect the toilet after each void per protocol (bleach, commonly for the first 6 hours)
  • 💧 Limit fluids before the instillation, then increase fluids after the dwell
🧠 “Hold it, roll it, then bleach it.” The drug has to bathe the whole lining — same soaking logic that caused the cancer, used on purpose.

🩹 If the bladder must come out — cystectomy & urinary diversion

For muscle-invasive disease, the bladder may be removed and the ureters diverted — most commonly an ileal conduit draining to a stoma and an external pouch.

🧠 Beefy red = alive. Dusky purple = dying. And mucus is expected — you routed urine through a piece of intestine, and intestine makes mucus.

AFTER — REPORT vs EXPECT

STEP 5 · THE TESTED LIST

"The nurse teaches the client to report which findings after a cystoscopy?" — know this list cold.

🚨 AFTER PROCEDURE — NOTIFY HCP COMMON NCLEX QUESTION

🧠 “CLOT · CAN'T · °C · CRAMP.” Four C's to call about: Clots & bright red blood · Can't urinate (>100 mL retained) · temperature over 38 °C / 100.4 °F · Cramping pain that analgesics won't touch.

⭐ The priority question — frequency, urgency & dysuria after cystoscopy KAPLAN

Scenario: a client has frequency, urgency and dysuria after cystoscopy. Those are all expected — so what is the highest nursing priority?

✅ OBTAIN VITAL SIGNS — monitor for FEVER
▼
Because those same symptoms are also the early signs of a UTI — and the scope just entered a sterile space.
▼
A temperature over 100.4°F (38°C) turns "expected" into "report."

Then: push fluids, offer a warm sitz bath, give the ordered analgesic, and document the urine's appearance.

🧠 Assess before you comfort. The symptoms are expected — the fever is what separates normal irritation from infection. Take the temperature first.

✅ Comfort & recovery teaching

1
💧 Increase fluids — dilutes the urine, eases the burning, flushes out small clots (unless fluids are restricted).
2
🛁 Warm sitz bath or moist heat to the lower abdomen for spasms.
3
💊 Mild analgesics as ordered; antispasmodics if prescribed. Note that some bladder analgesics turn the urine orange — expected, not bleeding.
4
📏 Measure output & check for retention — bladder scan if the client can't void; report retained volume over 100 mL per protocol.
5
🚫 Activity limits — avoid heavy lifting, strenuous exercise and (per provider) sexual activity for the ordered period, especially after a biopsy.
6
📅 Keep the follow-up — biopsy results and, for bladder cancer, lifelong surveillance cystoscopies, because it recurs.
🧠 Fluids · heat · pain med · measure. Then the two teaching lines: what's normal, what's a phone call.

🧠 Why "over 100 mL" matters

Answer first: urine left in the bladder after voiding is post-void residual. A residual of more than about 100 mL signals retention — from swelling, clots or spasm after the scope.

  • Retention ➜ pressure back up the ureters and a perfect medium for infection
  • Assess with a bladder scan — noninvasive, do it before you catheterize
  • Report per protocol; expect an order for catheterization if the client can't void
🧠 Full bladder, empty answer. If they "can't go," scan first — don't just keep waiting.

🎗️ Living with bladder cancer

  • 🚭 Smoking cessation — the single biggest thing the client can change
  • 💧 Fluids & frequent voiding — less carcinogen contact time
  • 🔁 Surveillance — repeat cystoscopies on schedule; recurrence is common
  • 🩸 Any new painless hematuria gets reported, forever
  • 💬 Body-image & sexuality support after cystectomy/urostomy; refer to ostomy resources
🧠 The sign that found it is the sign that finds it again. Painless blood — call, every single time.

🎯 Cover & check — 7 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What is the most common finding of bladder cancer?
Painless hematuria. Blood in the urine with no burning, no fever and no flank pain — and it often comes and goes.
Q2: Name the three causes the source highlights.
Tobacco use, chemical exposure and family history. Carcinogens are filtered into the urine and sit in the bladder, bathing the lining.
Q3: What exactly is a cystoscopy, and what do you teach beforehand?
A scope inserted through the urethra to view the bladder. Teaching: "You will feel pressure during insertion of the scope." Also verify consent, NPO per order if anesthesia is planned, have the client empty the bladder, and explain the lithotomy position.
Q4: Select all that apply — which findings does the client report after a cystoscopy?
Inability to urinate (retention over 100 mL), elevated temperature over 100.4°F (38°C), blood clots in the urine and bright red blood, and abdominal pain unrelieved by analgesics.
Q5: A client has frequency, urgency and dysuria after cystoscopy. Highest nursing priority?
Obtain vital signs — monitor for fever. Those symptoms are expected after the scope, but they are also early UTI signs; the temperature is what tells you which one it is.
Q6: Which findings after a cystoscopy are EXPECTED?
Pink-tinged urine, burning on urination, and frequency and urgency for about 1–2 days, plus mild lower abdominal discomfort. Treat with increased fluids, warm sitz baths and mild analgesics.
Q7: After a cystectomy with an ileal conduit, which findings are normal and which are reported?
Normal: a beefy red, moist stoma, continuous urine drainage, and mucus in the urine (the conduit is made of intestine). Report: a dusky or purple stoma, or urine output under 30 mL/hr.

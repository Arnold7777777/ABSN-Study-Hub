Isotonic · Hypotonic · Hypertonic 🔴

Fluid & Electrolyte Basics — tonicity, osmosis & what happens to a cell

NG-213 RENAL + FLUID ADHD-friendly visual edition

This page is the CONCEPT, not the bag. One law of physics — water chases salt across a membrane — explains every fluid shift you will ever be tested on. Learn it on a single red blood cell and the rest of fluid & electrolytes falls into place.

💦 Water chases saltWater moves toward the saltier side of a membrane. That's osmosis. That's the whole page.
🦛 HYPO = cell SWELLSWeak outside → water rushes in → the cell bloats and can burst.
🏃 HYPER = cell SHRINKSSalty outside → water pulled out → the cell crenates (shrivels).
⚖️ ISO = nothing moves275–295 mOsm/kg on both sides — equilibrium.

THE RULE

STEP 1 · OSMOSIS

One sentence, one picture — and then every fluid question is just this sentence again.

⭐ Osmosis = water moves through a semipermeable membrane toward the higher concentration of solute

Solute = the stuff dissolved (sodium, glucose, protein). Solvent = the water it's dissolved in. The cell membrane lets water through freely but holds most solute where it is — so the water is the only thing that can move, and it always moves toward the salt.

Tonicity is simply the answer to: compared to the inside of the cell, is the outside saltier, weaker, or the same?

🧠 “Salt sucks. Water follows.” Wherever the particles pile up, the water comes running. Say it every time you see the word tonicity.

💦 Osmosis in one picture — the membrane experiment

🧠 “The salty side wins the water.” Notice what actually happened: the concentrated side got bigger. That is exactly why hypertonic IV fluid grows the vascular space and shrinks the cell.

🧠 Osmosis vs the other three movements

ProcessWhat moves
Osmosis 💦Water, toward the higher solute — no energy used
Diffusion 🌫️Solute, high ➜ low concentration — no energy used
Filtration 🚿Water + solute pushed by pressure (this is how the glomerulus works)
Active transport ⚡Solute pushed uphill using ATP (the Na⁺/K⁺ pump)
🧠 “Osmosis = wOter.” Spell it wrong on purpose: osm-O-sis moves the O in H₂O. Everything else moves particles.

📏 The numbers that define “normal”

  • Serum osmolality 275–295 mOsm/kg — the reference band (adult)
  • Sodium 135–145 mEq/L — the biggest driver of osmolality
  • Estimate: 2(Na) + glucose/18 + BUN/2.8
  • Urine specific gravity 1.005–1.030 — concentrated urine = dry patient

Osmolality ↑ = concentrated blood = dehydration. Osmolality ↓ = dilute blood = overload / water excess.

🧠 “Sodium and water are best friends who can't stand each other's absence.” Where sodium goes, water follows — so a sodium result is really a water report.

🧲 Two pressures that decide where water sits

  • Osmotic (osmolar) pressure — the pull created by solutes, mostly sodium. This is what IV tonicity manipulates.
  • Oncotic (colloid) pressure — the pull created by proteins, mostly albumin (3.5–5.0 g/dL), holding water inside the vessel.
  • Hydrostatic pressure — the push of blood against the vessel wall, driving water out into the tissue.

⭐ Low albumin (liver failure, malnutrition, nephrotic syndrome, burns) = nothing left to hold water in the vessel → it leaks into tissue = edema and third spacing. The patient can be puffy everywhere and still be volume-depleted in the vessel.

🧠 “Albumin is the sponge, sodium is the magnet.” No sponge (low albumin) → the water drips out into the tissues no matter how perfect your IV fluid is.

THE 3 CELLS

STEP 2 · DRAW IT EVERY TIME

Put one red blood cell in each solution and watch what happens — this single picture answers most tonicity questions.

🔴 One red blood cell, three solutions

🧠 Draw this on your scratch paper before you answer. Three circles: fat, normal, spiky. Label which side has more dots. The dots tell you which way the arrows point — and the arrows give you the answer.

⚖️ ISO-tonic — “I-SO-perfect”

Perfect balance (equilibrium) of solutes both inside & outside the cell, so no fluid shifts are made. Human blood is isotonic, so very little osmosis occurs — isotonic solutions have the same osmolality as body fluids.

🔴 The cell: unchanged — the normal biconcave disc keeps its shape and its ~7–8 µm size.

🧠 “I'm so perfect — I don't move.” Equal in concentration = equilibrium = no fluid shift.

🦛 HYPO-tonic — think LOW

LOWer osmolarity & LOWer concentration of solutes than body fluids. They cause the movement of water into cells by osmosis, swelling the cells like a BIG fat hippo — so they must be given SLOWLY to prevent cellular edema.

🔴 The cell: swells → becomes a sphere → membrane fails → hemolysis (it bursts and spills hemoglobin).

🧠 HYPO-tonic = HIPPO-tonic. A hippo sitting in a bathtub, getting fatter. Cells swell with fluid.

🏃 HYPER-tonic — think HIGH & dry

HIGHer osmolarity & HIGHer concentration of solutes than body fluids. Very thick salty solutions with more solutes & less water, causing water to be moved outside the cells, making the cells skinny like a hyper person.

🔴 The cell: shrivels into a spiky ball — crenation — and stops working properly.

🧠 HYPER-tonic = HYPER person. The friend who never sits still and never eats: very skinny cells.

⭐ The vocabulary that shows up in the answer choices

WordMeansWhich solution caused it
Hemolysis 💥The RBC swelled until it burst, releasing hemoglobinHypotonic (e.g. sterile water, D5W after the sugar is burned)
Crenation 🌵The RBC shrivelled with a spiky borderHypertonic (e.g. 3% NS)
Cellular edema 🦛Any cell swollen with water — in the brain this is cerebral edemaHypotonic
Cellular dehydration 🌵Cells emptied of water while the vessel fillsHypertonic
Equilibrium ⚖️Equal concentration on both sides — no net movementIsotonic
🧠 “Hemolysis = Hippo exploded. Crenation = Crispy.” Two funny words, two opposite pictures — you only have to remember which one is wet and which one is dry.

IN THE BODY

STEP 3 · WHOLE-PATIENT VIEW

Now scale the one cell up to 42 litres of human — the compartments, the brain, and the blood pressure.

🫗 Where the water actually lives — the three compartments

🧠 “⅔ in, ⅓ out — and only 3 L is blood.” That tiny 3 L plasma box is why 1 L of isotonic fluid can spike a blood pressure, and why over-filling it so quickly becomes crackles and a BP over 180.

🚨 The brain is the cell that can't afford to swell

Same physics, opposite patient. ❌ Never give hypotonic fluid to a client with a head injury, stroke, or increased ICP. ✅ Hypertonic saline is therapy for that same brain.

🧠 “A swollen brain in a hard hat.” The skull will not stretch, so the first symptom of a swelling cell is always neuro: headache first, LOC changes next.

🚨 Cell SWELLING — what the patient looks like

  • 🤕 Headache — the first and earliest complaint
  • 🌀 New confusion, restless, agitated — “altered level of consciousness”
  • ⚡ Seizures & coma if it keeps going
  • 🧂 Often paired with hyponatremia — Na <135 mEq/L
  • 🤢 Nausea/vomiting, blurred vision, widening pulse pressure with ↑ICP

✅ Do: stop or slow the infusion, protect the airway, seizure precautions, neuro checks, notify the provider, check the sodium.

🧠 “Water on the brain talks first.” Any new neuro change during an infusion is a fluid-shift alarm until proven otherwise.

🚨 Cell SHRINKING — what the patient looks like

  • 🥵 Thirst, dry sticky mucous membranes, furrowed tongue
  • 🌀 Restlessness, weakness, confusion (dehydrated brain cells)
  • 🧂 Hypernatremia — Na >145 mEq/L
  • 💧 Low urine output, high urine specific gravity >1.030
  • 📈 Rising serum osmolality >295 mOsm/kg, ↑hematocrit

✅ Do: slow the infusion, recheck sodium, monitor I&O and daily weight, watch the BP — hypertonic fluid can overload the vessel while the cells dry out.

🧠 “Dry cells shout for water.” Thirst plus a rising sodium is the body begging you to stop pulling water out.

🚨 Too much of ANY tonicity → FVO → the same dangerous ending

💧 Infusion too fast / too much volume
▼
🫗 Fluid volume overload (FVO) — crackles, JVD, bounding pulse, edema, weight gain 1 kg = 1 L
▼
🩺 MONITOR: BLOOD PRESSURE — BP over 180 systolic = HTN crisis!
▼
🧠 Risk for CVA / stroke
🧠 “Different bag, same ending.” Isotonic over-fills the vessel, hypertonic drags more in behind it — both roads end at BP >180 and a stroke risk. Rate is a nursing decision. Own it.

⭐ Trap #1 — D5W changes teams

Isotonic in the bag (252 mOsm/L) · hypotonic in the body. Cells burn the dextrose within minutes and leave behind free water that swells them.

If the question asks “what is in the bag?” → isotonic. If it asks “what happens to the client?” → hypotonic.

🧠 “D5W = a hippo in a tuxedo.” Dressed as isotonic, behaves like hypotonic.

⭐ Trap #2 — the percent sign is your compass

  • Below 0.9% saline → hypotonic
  • Exactly 0.9% saline → isotonic
  • Above 0.9% saline (3%, 5%) → hypertonic
  • Bolt D5 onto an isotonic fluid → it becomes hypertonic in the bag
🧠 “Point-nine is the line.” Under it, over it, on it — that one number sorts every saline bag you'll ever see.

⭐ Trap #3 — “a lot of numbers” vs “very little numbers”

The source's own trick, and it works: hypotonic labels look busy (0.225 · 0.33 · 0.45) = a lot of fluid IN the cell. Hypertonic labels look tiny (3% · 5%) = very little fluid in the cell.

🧠 “Long label, fat cell. Short label, skinny cell.” Counter-intuitive on purpose — which is exactly why it sticks.

⭐ Trap #4 — swelling vs shrinking, sodium version

  • Hyponatremia <135 = dilute blood → water moves into cells → brain swells → seizures
  • Hypernatremia >145 = concentrated blood → water pulled out of cells → thirst, dry, confused
  • Both are corrected slowly — a fast fix causes its own brain injury
🧠 “Low sodium = swollen brain. High sodium = shrunken brain.” Same two pictures from the cell panel, just drawn at patient size.

✅ What the nurse actually does with all of this

1
Know the tonicity before you spike the bag — read the label, not the habit.
2
Pump + slow rate for anything that is not isotonic; never “catch up” a behind-schedule infusion by opening it up.
3
Baseline then serial assessment: neuro status, lung sounds, edema, BP, weight, I&O.
4
Trend the labs: sodium, potassium, serum osmolality, BUN/creatinine, hematocrit.
5
Stop and report early — new headache, new confusion, new crackles. Those are the first words the cell can say.
🧠 “The bag is the order. The rate is the nurse.” Providers choose tonicity; you control how fast the shift happens — and speed is what hurts people.

🎯 Cover & check — 6 rapid-fire questions
Q1: Define osmosis in one sentence.
Water moves across a semipermeable membrane from the area of LOWER solute concentration toward the area of HIGHER solute concentration. Water chases salt.
Q2: An RBC is placed in a hypotonic solution. Draw what happens.
Water rushes IN, the cell swells from a biconcave disc into a sphere, and it can burst — hemolysis.
Q3: An RBC is placed in a hypertonic solution. Draw what happens.
Water is pulled OUT, the cell shrivels with a spiky border — crenation.
Q4: Which compartment holds the most water, and which holds the least?
Intracellular holds the most — two thirds, about 28 L of the 42 L total. Intravascular (plasma) holds the least, only about 3 L, which is why small volumes change the blood pressure so fast.
Q5: Why is a hypotonic infusion contraindicated in a head-injured client?
It drives water into brain cells. The skull cannot expand, so cerebral edema raises ICP — headache, then altered LOC, then seizures and coma.
Q6: The provider orders 3% NS for a client seizing from a sodium of 116. What does it do, and what are the nursing musts?
It is hypertonic, so it pulls water out of the swollen brain cells and raises serum sodium. Musts: infusion pump, slow controlled rate, frequent serum sodium checks, hourly neuro checks, watch for fluid overload (crackles, BP over 180) — and correct slowly, because over-fast correction causes its own brain injury.

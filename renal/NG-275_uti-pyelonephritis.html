UTI & Pyelonephritis 🦠

Lower tract infection · and what happens when it climbs

NG-275 Renal + Fluid ADHD-friendly visual edition

One infection, two addresses. A UTI lives low — urethra and bladder (cystitis). If it gets bad enough it climbs the ureters and infects the kidney — that is pyelonephritis, and it is the serious one. Same bugs, same burning… plus fever and flank pain.

⬆️ It climbsUrethra → bladder → ureters → kidney. Low = UTI. High = pyelonephritis.
🌡️ Fever + flank = kidneyCVA tenderness is the one finding that moves it upstairs.
🧪 Culture FIRSTThen antibiotics. >10,000 organisms/mL = infection.
👵 New confusion = UTIIn an older adult, sudden confusion is a UTI until proven otherwise.
🧨

CAUSE

STEP 1 · THE CLIMB

Bacteria almost never fall into the kidney from above — they walk in the front door and climb.

⬆️ The ascending path — urethra ➜ bladder ➜ ureters ➜ kidney

EXAM TIP Bacteria enter at the urethra and climb upward — so every level of the urinary tract has its own infection name, and the higher it goes the sicker the client gets.

🧠 “Bugs take the stairs, never the elevator.” They start at the bottom step (urethra) and climb — so the higher the fever and the higher the pain, the higher the infection has climbed.

🏷️ Name the level, name the disease

  • Urethritis — infection of the urethra 🚪
  • Cystitis — infection of the bladder 🫙 (“cyst-” = bladder)
  • UTI — the umbrella term for the lower tract: urethra + bladder
  • Pyelonephritis — infection of the kidney 🫘 — more serious
  • Urosepsis — the infection is now in the blood 🩸 — emergency
🧠 “Pyelo = PILE-o problems.” Pyel- means renal pelvis, nephr- means kidney, -itis means inflammation. Kidney-level infection = a whole PILE of new symptoms.

🦠 E. coli is the #1 cause — and anatomy is why

Escherichia coli from the bowel/perineum causes roughly 80–85% of uncomplicated UTIs. It only has to travel a short distance in a female.

🧠 “Short pipe, quick trip.” Women get UTIs because the trip is 4 cm. A male with a UTI = go looking for a reason (prostate, stone, retention, catheter).

⚠️ Risk factors — anything that pools urine or plants bacteria

  • 🚻 Female anatomy · sexual activity · spermicide/diaphragm
  • 🧻 Wiping back to front · bubble baths · douching
  • 🩹 Indwelling catheter — the single biggest hospital risk
  • 🚽 Urinary retention / incomplete emptying · “holding it”
  • 🪨 Stones, strictures, BPH, tumors — anything obstructing flow
  • 🍬 Diabetes — sugary urine feeds bacteria + neuropathy
  • 🤰 Pregnancy — ureters dilate and drain slowly
  • 🌸 Postmenopause — low estrogen thins the tissue
  • 🛏️ Immobility · dehydration · immunosuppression
🧠 “Stagnant water grows bugs.” Every risk factor on this list either parks urine in the bladder or plants bacteria at the door. Flowing urine is the body's own irrigation system.

🩹 CAUTI — the catheter is a highway

Catheter-Associated UTI. Risk climbs with every single day the catheter stays in. The device gives bacteria a straight, protected road into the bladder.

  • ✅ Remove it as early as possible — day one is the goal
  • ✅ Keep a closed sterile system — do not disconnect to transport
  • ✅ Bag below bladder level, never on the floor
  • ❌ Never irrigate or change a catheter just to “treat” a UTI without an order
🧠 “The best CAUTI care is no catheter.” On any NCLEX question about preventing CAUTI, the winning answer is almost always remove the catheter as soon as it is no longer needed.
🔎

CLUES

STEP 2 · SPOT IT

Burning and frequency say bladder. Fever and flank say kidney. Confusion says older adult.

🔥 Lower UTI (cystitis) — the classic four

🔥Dysuriaburning with urination
🔁Frequencygoing constantly
🏃Urgencygotta go NOW
☁️Cloudy urine& foul-smelling
🤏Small volumesdespite urgency
😣Suprapubicpressure / pain
🩸Hematuriamay be present
🌡️Low / no feverusually afebrile

Key distinction: a simple bladder infection makes you miserable — it usually does not make you systemically ill. High fever, chills, nausea and back pain mean it has moved up.

🧠 “BURN · RUN · CLOUD” — it BURNs (dysuria), you RUN to the toilet (frequency + urgency), and the urine looks like a CLOUD. Three words = the whole lower UTI picture.

🚨 Pyelonephritis — “like a UTI, but worse” · the key difference is pain LOCATION

Everything above, plus a systemically sick patient:

  • 🌡️ High fever — often >101°F (38.3°C), with shaking chills (rigors)
  • 👊 Costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness — the hallmark finding
  • 🫸 Dull flank pain, extending around toward the umbilicus
  • 🤢 Nausea & vomiting, malaise, body aches
  • 🧪 WBC casts in the urine — proof the infection is in kidney tissue
🧠 “Flank + Fever = Fly upstairs.” Three F's move the infection from the bladder to the kidney. If the stem says the patient is grabbing her side with a temp of 102.5°F and cloudy urine — the answer is pyelonephritis.

⚖️ Tell them apart — lower UTI vs pyelonephritis

Feature🫙 Lower UTI / Cystitis🫘 Pyelonephritis

🧠 “Front & low = bladder. Back & high = kidney.” Point at the pain. Suprapubic pain points down and forward; flank pain points back and up. Location alone answers most exam questions.

👵 ⭐ The older adult presents differently

Answer first: in an older adult, new-onset confusion — not burning — is often the only sign of a UTI.

  • 🧠 Acute confusion / delirium, agitation, personality change
  • 🤸 New falls or sudden unsteadiness
  • 💧 New incontinence in someone previously continent
  • 🍽️ Poor appetite, lethargy, “just not themselves”
  • 🌡️ Often afebrile — a blunted immune response hides the fever
🧠 “Grandma is confused? Check her pee.” Sudden mental status change in an older adult = UTI until proven otherwise. Do not chart it as “normal aging” — dementia comes on over months; delirium comes on over hours.

🧪 Urinalysis (UA) — read the dipstick

  • ☁️ Cloudy & foul-smelling = bacteria & pus in the urine
  • ⚪ WBCs / leukocyte esterase + = inflammation & infection
  • 🟣 Nitrites + = gram-negative bacteria (E. coli) converting dietary nitrates
  • 🦠 Bacteria seen on microscopy
  • 🩸 RBCs may be present (hematuria)
  • 🧬 WBC casts = the infection is in kidney tissue = pyelonephritis
🧠 “LEuko + NItrite = INfectION.” Two positives on a dipstick and a burning patient is enough to start treating — but you still collect the culture first.

🔬 Normal urine vs infected urine — side by side

🧠 “Cloudy = bacteria.” HESI asks it straight: “A cloudy urine specimen is an indication of…” ➜ bacteria in the urine. Cloudiness is white cells and bacteria you can see with the naked eye.

🧫 Urine culture & sensitivity — culture FIRST, then antibiotics

1️⃣ Client reports dysuria & frequency ➜ anticipate a urine culture
▼
2️⃣ Collect a clean-catch midstream specimen in a sterile cup
▼
3️⃣ Send to lab within 1 hour — or refrigerate
▼
4️⃣ Culture names the bug · Sensitivity names the drug
▼
5️⃣ Then start the antibiotic (empiric first if the client is septic)

Growth threshold: the source uses >10,000 organisms/mL as significant. Many labs report ≥100,000 CFU/mL for an asymptomatic clean-catch and accept lower counts in a symptomatic patient — follow your lab's reference range.

  • 🧼 Teach: cleanse front to back, start the stream, then catch mid-stream
  • 🌅 First morning void is the most concentrated and the best sample
  • 💉 From a catheter: sterile syringe at the sampling port — never take the specimen from the drainage bag
  • ⏱️ A specimen left at room temperature >1 hour overgrows with bacteria and gives a false result
🧠 “Culture before cure.” Antibiotics given before the culture kill just enough bacteria to make the culture useless — and then you never learn which drug works.

🚨 Urosepsis — the complication that kills

Bacteria cross from the kidney into the bloodstream. This is the reason pyelonephritis gets admitted.

  • 🌡️ Fever or hypothermia · shaking chills
  • 💓 HR >90 · RR >20 · SBP <90 mmHg
  • 🧠 Altered mental status — often the very first sign
  • 💧 Urine output <30 mL/hr · rising lactate
  • 🩸 Blood cultures ×2 sites before antibiotics, then broad-spectrum IV fast
🧠 “Hot, fast, low and confused.” Hot (fever) · fast (HR & RR) · low (BP & urine output) · confused (mental status). That combination in a UTI patient = escalate now.

CARE

STEP 3 · TREAT & PREVENT

Kill the bug, flush the tract, and teach the habits that keep it from coming back.

Universal rule: finish the entire course even after symptoms disappear — stopping early is how resistant organisms are born.

🧠 “Nitro stays in the bladder.” Nitrofurantoin concentrates in urine, not tissue — perfect for cystitis, useless for a kidney infection. That single fact is a favorite distractor.

💊 Phenazopyridine (Pyridium) — numbs, does not cure

  • 🎯 It is a dye with a topical analgesic effect on the urinary lining — no anti-infective activity at all
  • 🟠 Turns urine and tears reddish-orange; stains fabric and contact lenses — this is normal
  • 🍽️ Take after meals
  • ⏱️ No more than 2 days when combined with an antibiotic — longer can mask the symptoms of a more serious disorder
🧠 “Pyridium paints, it doesn't punish.” It paints the urine orange and paints over the pain — but it never kills a single bacterium. Antibiotic still required.

✅ Prevention teaching — the habits that matter

1
💧 Fluids 2–3 L/day (unless heart or kidney disease limits it)
2
🚽 Void every 2–3 hours — never “hold it”; empty completely
3
🧻 Wipe front to back, every time
4
💞 Void right after intercourse — flush out what was pushed in
5
👖 Cotton underwear; no tight synthetic pants; shower over tub bath

Avoid: ☕ caffeine · 🍺 alcohol · 🥤 carbonation · 🌶️ spicy foods · 🫧 bubble baths, douches & scented feminine products — all bladder irritants.

🧠 “FLUSH” — Fluids 2–3 L · Let it out q2–3h · Urinate after sex · Swipe front to back · Hygiene & cotton. A urinary tract you keep flushing is a urinary tract bacteria can't settle in.

🛏️ Foley drainage set-up — the picture the exam draws wrong

🧠 “Down and dangling, never doubling back.” Urine only flows one way if gravity helps it. Bag below the bladder, tubing straight, bag off the floor, system closed.

The master rule for this whole topic: Assess 1st · Interventions 2nd. If both an assessment and an action look right, the assessment comes first — unless the client is unstable.

🧠 “Old pee, throw it away.” Any urine specimen sitting out longer than an hour is contaminated data. Recollecting takes five minutes; a wrong antibiotic takes weeks to fix.

🎯 Cover & check — 6 rapid-fire questions
Q1: What single assessment finding tells you the infection has reached the kidney?
Costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness — with dull flank pain radiating toward the umbilicus, high fever and chills. That is pyelonephritis.
Q2: An 84-year-old is suddenly confused and falling. She is afebrile. What do you suspect?
A UTI. New-onset confusion is often the only sign in an older adult, and they are frequently afebrile. Get a urinalysis and culture.
Q3: Order says start antibiotics and obtain a urine culture. Which comes first?
The culture — always collect cultures before the first antibiotic dose, or the results are useless. (In frank sepsis, draw fast and do not delay the drug.)
Q4: Why is nitrofurantoin the wrong choice for pyelonephritis?
It concentrates in the urine, not in kidney tissue — great for a bladder infection, ineffective for an upper-tract infection.
Q5: The client's urine turns orange and she is alarmed. Which drug, and what do you say?
Phenazopyridine (Pyridium). Reassure her: reddish-orange urine and tears are expected, it can stain fabric and contact lenses, take it after meals, and no more than 2 days when combined with an antibiotic.
Q6: Name four teaching points that prevent recurrent UTIs.
Fluids 2–3 L/day, void every 2–3 hours and empty completely, wipe front to back, void after intercourse. Also: cotton underwear, showers over tub baths, no bubble baths or douches, finish the full antibiotic course.

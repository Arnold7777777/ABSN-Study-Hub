Bladder Control 🫙

The anatomy & physiology of voiding — and how to retrain it

NG-104 Renal + Fluid ADHD-friendly visual edition

Two muscles decide everything. The detrusor squeezes the urine out; the sphincters hold it in. Continence is just those two taking turns on cue. Learn the wiring first — then bladder training, Kegels and timed voiding stop being random tips and start being mechanism. (The five types of incontinence live on the companion page, NG-272.)

HOW VOIDING WORKS

STEP 1 · THE WIRING

One muscle, two doors, one reflex loop, and a brain with veto power.

🫙 The bladder — detrusor muscle and two sphincters

Read it as a chain: kidneys make urine ➜ ureters deliver it ➜ the detrusor relaxes to store it ➜ the trigone stays flat ➜ two sphincters hold the door ➜ when the brain says yes, the detrusor squeezes and both doors open.

🧠 “IN-ternal = IN-voluntary. EX-ternal = EX-ercise it.” The internal sphincter you can never control; the external one is the muscle you actually train with Kegels. That one word pair is worth a whole exam question.

🔁 The micturition reflex — filling vs emptying

1️⃣ Bladder fills ➜ stretch receptors in the wall fire
▼
2️⃣ Signal travels to the sacral micturition centre — S2, S3, S4
▼
3️⃣ Message goes up to the pontine micturition centre & cortex ➜ “is now a good time?”
▼
4️⃣ Brain says YES ➜ parasympathetic fires: detrusor contracts, internal sphincter relaxes
▼
5️⃣ You voluntarily relax the external sphincter (pudendal nerve) ➜ 🚽 void
🧠 “S2, 3, 4 keep the pee off the floor.” Then: Sympathetic Stores · Parasympathetic Pees. Two rhymes cover the entire neuro physiology of voiding.

📏 Volumes & numbers to know

Typical adult: 1500 mL of urine a day over about 5–6 voids. Voiding more than 8 times in 24 hours is frequency; getting up more than once at night is nocturia.

🧠 “150 whispers, 400 shouts.” At 150 mL the bladder mentions it; by 400 mL it is yelling. Bladder training works by teaching the brain to ignore the whisper.

⚠️ What breaks the system

  • 🧠 Brain / spinal cord damage — stroke, MS, Parkinson's, SCI ➜ the veto signal never arrives ➜ neurogenic bladder
  • 🍬 Diabetic neuropathy ➜ the stretch receptors go silent ➜ the bladder over-fills without warning
  • 🚧 Obstruction — BPH, stricture, stones ➜ the detrusor can squeeze but urine can't leave
  • 🧵 Weak pelvic floor — childbirth, aging, low estrogen ➜ the external sphincter can't back up the internal one
  • 💊 Drugs — anticholinergics & opioids cause retention; diuretics cause urgency; sedatives blunt the signal
🧠 “Nerve, narrow, or noodle.” Bladder control fails for one of three reasons: the nerve is damaged, the outlet is narrowed, or the muscle is a noodle. Match the failure to the fix.

TRAIN THE BLADDER

STEP 2 · FIRST-LINE, ALWAYS

Behavioral therapy comes before drugs and before surgery — on the exam and in practice.

🏋️ Kegels (pelvic floor muscle training) — hold 10 · repeat 10 · 3 times a day

✅ DO❌ DON'T
Squeeze as if you are stopping urine and holding in gas at the same time Don't routinely stop the stream mid-void to practice — use it once only to find the muscle; repeating it causes incomplete emptying
Breathe normally and count out loud Don't hold your breath or bear down
Isolate the pelvic floor only Don't tighten the abdomen, buttocks or thighs
Do them anywhere — sitting, standing, lying, in line at the store Don't quit early — expect 4–6 weeks for improvement, up to 3 months for full benefit

Who benefits: stress incontinence ✅ · urge incontinence ✅ (quick contractions suppress the urge) · mixed ✅ · men after prostatectomy ✅ · pregnancy & postpartum ✅.

🧠 “Elevator, not a slam.” Ride the pelvic floor up like an elevator, hold it on the top floor for 10, then ride it back down slowly. And it is a lifetime habit — stop the exercises and the leaking returns.

⏰ Bladder training — stretch the interval, week by week

Who it's for: a cognitively intact client with urge or mixed incontinence. Goal: retrain the brain to postpone the urge.

Urge-suppression technique — teach this exact sequence for when the urge hits between scheduled times:

1
🛑 STOP and stand still or sit down — never run to the bathroom, running makes the spasm worse
2
💪 Quick flicks — several fast, strong pelvic floor squeezes to shut the urge down
3
😮‍💨 Breathe deeply and relax; distract yourself (count backward from 50)
4
🚶 When the urge passes, walk calmly to the toilet
🧠 “Freeze, squeeze, breathe, then leave.” Four steps in order. Running to the toilet jostles the bladder and triggers the detrusor — standing still and squeezing turns it off.

⏱️ Timed voiding & its cousins — know the difference

TechniqueWhat it isBest for
Timed / scheduled voidingVoid on a fixed clock schedule (e.g. q2h) whether or not there is an urgeAny client; the default in facilities
Habit trainingSchedule built around the client's own pattern from the bladder diaryClients with a predictable pattern
Prompted voidingCaregiver asks at intervals whether the client needs to go, and praises successCognitive impairment / dementia
Bladder trainingProgressively lengthens the interval with urge suppressionCognitively intact urge/mixed

Double voiding: void, wait 2–5 minutes, lean slightly forward and void again — helps empty a bladder with residual urine.

🧠 “The clock decides, not the bladder.” That is the whole idea of timed voiding — you empty before the bladder gets loud enough to win the argument.

✅ Habits that make training work

  • 💧 1500–2000 mL/day — never restrict fluids to control leaking; concentrated urine irritates the bladder
  • 🌙 Limit fluids 2–3 hours before bed for nocturia
  • ☕ Cut the irritants: caffeine, alcohol, carbonated drinks, artificial sweeteners, citrus, tomato, spicy foods
  • 📔 Keep a bladder diary — times, volumes, leaks, triggers, intake
  • 💩 Treat constipation; ⚖️ lose weight; 🚬 stop smoking (chronic cough)
  • 🚻 Set up the path: bedside commode, call light in reach, night light, elastic-waist clothing, non-skid shoes
🧠 “Drink enough, cut the buzz, clear the path.” Enough water, no caffeine or alcohol, and a bathroom you can actually reach. Simple, and it beats a drug in most first-line questions.

WHEN DRUGS ARE ADDED

STEP 3 · OXYBUTYNIN & TOLTERODINE

If behavioral therapy isn't enough for an overactive bladder, these are the drugs — and they dry out the entire body.

💊 Oxybutynin & tolterodine — anticholinergics that relax the detrusor

Indication: overactive bladder. Decreases urgency, frequency and nighttime bathroom visits (nocturia).

MOA: an anticholinergic — it blocks acetylcholine at the M₃ muscarinic receptors on the detrusor, so the muscle stops spasming and the bladder holds more.

Think: anticholinergic = anti-secretions = dry the body out.

🧠 “Oxy-Buty-nin — B for Bladder. The Ox is on your Bladder!” Picture a huge ox sitting on the bladder, pinning it still so it can't spasm. Given for urinary frequency.

🧠 “Can't see, can't pee, can't spit, can't poop.” The classic anticholinergic quartet — plus “hot as a hare, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter.” Every symptom is just drying.

🚨 Major adverse effect & the two “avoids”

Urinary retention — “no urination all day” ➜ REPORT TO THE HCP.

It is the cruel irony of the drug class: given for bladder symptoms, it can stop the bladder from emptying entirely.

  • 👁️ AVOID in glaucoma — anticholinergics can raise intraocular pressure (narrow-angle)
  • 🧔 AVOID in BPH — already obstructed; this tips him into full retention
  • 🚧 Caution in GI obstruction, ileus, myasthenia gravis, and in older adults (confusion, falls)
🧠 “Dry body — avoid Glaucoma & BPH. That's ALL anticholinergics.” Learn it once here and it transfers to every anticholinergic you will ever be tested on.

🗣️ Patient teaching

  • 🧍 Change positions slowly — prevents orthostatic hypotension
  • 🥵 Avoid hyperthermia — too much sun, hot tubs, heavy exercise in heat; the body can't sweat to cool itself
  • 🍬 Sugar-free gum or hard candy, sips of water for dry mouth
  • 🥗 Fiber + fluids for constipation; report no bowel movement or no urination
  • 👓 Expect blurred vision — no driving until the effect is known
  • 💊 Do not crush or chew extended-release tablets
🧠 “Slow up, stay cool, sip water.” Three sentences the client leaves with. The fourth — call if you haven't urinated all day — is the one that keeps her out of the ED.

⭐ Don't get tricked by sound-alike names

🧠 “Bladder · Opioid · Contractions.” Three “oxy” drugs, three completely different patients. Read past the first three letters — that is the entire trap.

🎯 Cover & check — 7 rapid-fire questions
Q1: Name the two sphincters and say which one you can control.
Internal urethral sphincter — smooth muscle, involuntary. External urethral sphincter — skeletal muscle, voluntary, pudendal nerve S2–S4. The external one is what Kegels train.
Q2: Which spinal levels control the micturition reflex?
S2, S3, S4 — the sacral micturition centre. "S2, 3, 4 keep the pee off the floor."
Q3: At what volume does the first urge to void appear, and what is normal adult capacity?
First urge around 150–250 mL; capacity roughly 400–600 mL. Normal post-void residual is under 50 mL.
Q4: Describe the Kegel prescription and one thing the client should NOT do.
Contract 10 seconds, relax 10 seconds, 10 repetitions, 3 sets a day. Do not hold your breath, do not tighten abdomen/buttocks/thighs, and do not routinely stop the urine stream to practice — use that once only to identify the muscle. Expect 4–6 weeks for improvement.
Q5: A client on bladder training gets a strong urge 40 minutes before her scheduled void. What do you teach her to do?
Stop and stand still (do not run), do several quick pelvic floor contractions, breathe deeply and distract herself, then walk calmly to the toilet when the urge passes.
Q6: Which are expected side effects of oxybutynin — hypertension, dry eyes, dry mouth, diarrhea, hypokalemia?
Dry eyes and dry mouth. Not hypertension, not diarrhea (expect constipation), not hypokalemia.
Q7: Two conditions where you avoid oxybutynin, and why?
Glaucoma — anticholinergics can raise intraocular pressure. BPH — the outlet is already obstructed and the drug can cause complete urinary retention. This applies to all anticholinergics.

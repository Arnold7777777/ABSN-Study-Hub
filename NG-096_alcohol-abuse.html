Alcohol Abuse 🍺

CNS depression, the withdrawal clock, and the two emergencies that follow it

NG-096 MENTAL HEALTH ADHD-friendly visual edition

Alcohol is a CNS depressant — chronic use quiets the brain, and the brain compensates by revving up its own excitability. Stop the alcohol suddenly, and that compensation is unopposed — vitals spike, seizures can happen, and delirium tremens (DTs) can kill. Two other life-or-death traps live on this page: Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome and the thiamine-before-glucose rule.

😴 Intoxication = low & slowCNS depressant — vitals drop, coordination & balance suffer.
📈 Withdrawal = high & fastVitals spike as the brain rebounds — seizures & DTs are the danger.
🧠 Thiamine BEFORE glucosePrevents Wernicke-Korsakoff — a rule that can be tested on its own.
🚫 Recovery goal = total abstinenceNo alcohol, ever — identify triggers, not moderation.

CAUSE

STEP 1 · WHAT ALCOHOL DOES

One mechanism explains both intoxication and withdrawal — alcohol depresses the CNS, and the brain fights back by upregulating itself.

😴 Alcohol = CNS depressant — vitals go low & slow

EXAM TIP Alcohol is a toxin that causes central nervous system depression — vital signs run low and slow, and coordination & balance suffer (ataxia, slurred speech).

🧠 "Low and slow means alcohol's in tow." Any time vitals trend down together with slurred speech and poor balance, think acute intoxication.

😖 Why people drink

  • Escape from problems
  • Cover up depression & anxiety
🧠 Alcohol use disorder is often a symptom of an underlying mood problem — that's why counseling's primary goal is identifying triggers, not just stopping the drink.

🩸 Hypoglycemia risk — CAUTION at night

Alcohol metabolism in the liver disrupts normal glucose production. Monitor blood glucose levels at night — hypoglycemia can happen hours after the last drink, especially in malnourished or diabetic clients.

🧠 Alcohol intoxication can look like a diabetic emergency — always check a glucose on an intoxicated or withdrawing client; don't assume it's "just the alcohol."
🔎

WITHDRAWAL & DTs

STEP 2 · THE CLOCK THAT CAN KILL

Withdrawal severity rises on a predictable timeline — DTs are the single most dangerous point on it.

⏱️ Alcohol withdrawal timeline — know every stage

🧠 "24 shakes, 48 seizes, 72 crazes." ~24 hr = anxious & shaky · ~48 hr = seizure risk · 48–72 hr = DTs, the peak danger window.

🌡️ DTs vital sign pattern — read the numbers

🧠 If a chart shows fever + tachycardia + hypertension + tachypnea in a client with a recent drinking history, think withdrawal — not just "anxiety."

🖐️ DTs — the full symptom list

  • Hyperreflexia — hand tremors
  • Diaphoresis (sweating)
  • Hallucinations
  • Tachycardia — HR over 100 bpm
  • Hypertension
  • Fever
  • Confusion & restlessness
🧠 "SHAKE-HHF" — Sweating · Hand tremors/hyperreflexia · Agitation/confusion · Krazy visions (hallucinations) · Elevated HR · Hypertension · Fever. DTs is a medical emergency.

📊 CIWA-Ar — how severity gets scored

The CIWA-Ar (Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol, revised) is a structured bedside scoring tool — tremor, sweating, anxiety, agitation, hallucinations, orientation, headache, nausea are each scored, and the total drives symptom-triggered treatment decisions. Higher score = more severe withdrawal = closer monitoring.

🧠 Score drives care, not the calendar. CIWA-Ar lets the team treat the client's actual symptom burden instead of a fixed timer. For the specific medication class and taper approach used to treat this withdrawal, see the Pharmacology batch's Withdrawal Meds (NG-277) page — this page focuses on recognizing and scoring the withdrawal itself.

SAFETY & RECOVERY

STEP 3 · PROTECT THE BRAIN

Seizure precautions come first; then protect against a second, quieter emergency — Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome.

🚨 PRIORITY — seizure precautions

Any client in alcohol withdrawal (or sudden benzodiazepine/barbiturate withdrawal — cross-tolerant, same mechanism) needs seizure precautions in place immediately: padded rails, bed in low position, suction & oxygen at bedside, quiet low-stimulation environment.

🧠 Sudden withdrawal from alcohol, benzodiazepines, or barbiturates can all cause seizures — they share the same GABA mechanism, so nursing precautions look the same across all three.

🧠 Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome — the "why thiamine first" rule

Chronic alcohol use causes poor nutrition and impairs thiamine (vitamin B1) absorption. Thiamine is required as a cofactor to metabolize glucose. If glucose is given before thiamine is replaced, the sudden glucose load consumes the little thiamine the client has left — precipitating or worsening Wernicke's encephalopathy.

🧠 "Thiamine before the sweet stuff." Any malnourished or chronic-alcohol-use client who needs IV glucose (D50, dextrose fluids) should get thiamine first — this is a classic standalone NCLEX point, tested even outside a full withdrawal scenario.

✅ Recovery teaching — total abstinence

After detox, the primary goal of recovery is total abstinence — meaning NO alcohol, forever. Moderation is not the treatment goal for alcohol use disorder.

🧠 Abstinence, not moderation — a very common wrong-answer trap is "cut back to a couple drinks a week."

🎯 Counseling — identify triggers

The primary goal of counseling is to help the client identify factors that trigger alcohol use — not to lecture about consequences or "cover up" the underlying depression/anxiety that drives the drinking.

🧠 "Triggers, not tricks." Effective intervention = develop motivation & self-help skills, and pinpoint the trigger, every time.

🔗 Cross-reference — treatment medications

📎 The specific drug class used to prevent seizures/DTs (benzodiazepines, dosed by CIWA-Ar symptom score) and the general safety comparison with opioid withdrawal are covered in detail on the Pharmacology batch's Withdrawal Meds (NG-277) page — refer there for drug names, taper approach, and monitoring parameters rather than duplicating them here.

Use Therapeutic Communication (NG-271) techniques when counseling: open-ended questions to explore triggers, reflecting instead of lecturing, and never giving false reassurance about "just cutting back."

🎯 Cover & check — 4 rapid-fire questions
Q1: A client reports drinking a pint of vodka daily and is 48–72 hours post-last-drink. What is the priority concern?
Delirium tremens — the most dangerous window (48–72 hours), with confusion, hallucinations, tachycardia, hypertension, and fever.
Q2: Why must thiamine be given before IV glucose in a chronic alcohol use client?
Glucose metabolism requires thiamine as a cofactor — giving glucose first in a thiamine-deficient client can precipitate or worsen Wernicke encephalopathy.
Q3: What is the primary goal of counseling for alcohol use disorder?
Assisting the client to identify the factors that trigger alcohol use — not just discussing consequences.
Q4: What tool is commonly used to score alcohol withdrawal severity at the bedside?
CIWA-Ar (Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol, revised) — a structured symptom score that guides symptom-triggered treatment.

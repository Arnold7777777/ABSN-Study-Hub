⭐ Suffix → class → side effect → answer

The chain works in one direction and it is reliable:

Read the last syllable
↓
Name the class
↓
Recall that class's side effects and cautions
↓
Eliminate the options that contradict them
🧠 You are almost never asked to recall a fact you have never seen — you are asked to classify and then reason.

The order is by speed, not by importance. All three are given — the steroid is given early precisely because it is slow.

PART 2 · EVERY MNEMONIC IN THE BATCH

MEMORY

Collected in one place. Say them out loud — that is what makes them stick.

🧠 AIM for the Acute Asthma attack

A — Albuterol first (minutes)
I — Ipratropium second (15–30 minutes)
M — Methylprednisolone (hours — so give it early)

Oxygen, upright positioning and continuous monitoring run alongside all three.

🧠 You AIM at an Acute Asthma Attack.

🧠 Albu-T-E-R-O-L — the three T's

T — Tachycardia and palpitations
T — Tremor (fine, both hands)
T — Tossing and turning (insomnia, jitteriness)

All three are expected, not allergic. Report chest pain or a rate that keeps climbing.

🧠 Albuterol tastes like nothing and feels like espresso.

🧠 S is for Salmeterol, Slow, Scheduled, Steroid-partnered

Salmeterol = Slow acting. NOT a rescue inhaler. Scheduled twice daily. Never used alone in asthma — it needs a Steroid alongside it.

🧠 Four S's, and none of them is "sudden".

🧠 B is for Buterol, B is for Brutal

Beta-2 agonist · "-buterol" · for Brutal asthma attacks.

And the receptor number tells you the organ: beta-TWO = TWO lungs, beta-ONE = ONE heart.

🧠 That one line explains both the therapeutic effect and the tachycardia.

🧠 Can't see, can't pee, can't spit, can't poop

Every anticholinergic — ipratropium, tiotropium, diphenhydramine, and the rest.

Blurred vision and dilated pupils · urinary retention · dry mouth and throat · constipation. Plus can't sweat, which is the overheating risk.

🧠 Full version: hot as a hare, dry as a bone, red as a beet, blind as a bat, mad as a hatter.

🧠 The three G's — never give an anticholinergic in…

Glaucoma (closed-angle) · Gotta-go problems (urinary retention, BPH) · a Gut that has stopped (obstruction, ileus).

Short version from the source: "we never give these to someone who is already dry."

🧠 Three G's, three automatic "question this order" answers.

🧠 The 3 T's of theophylline toxicity

T — Toxic over 20 mcg/mL (commonly cited; ranges vary by lab) — expect frequent blood draws.
T — Tachycardia and dysrhythmias — report before the next dose.
T — Tonic-clonic seizures — the emergency stage.

🧠 Toxicity walks upward: gut → heart → brain.

🧠 The two C's that make theophylline climb

Cimetidine and Ciprofloxacin slow the liver, so the level rises.

Add the third C — Caffeine — which stacks on top of the drug's own effects. And remember the reverse: quitting smoking can push a stable patient into the toxic range.

🧠 Cimetidine · Ciprofloxacin · Caffeine.

🧠 TIOTROPIUM = TIE it to one TIME a day

Once daily · maintenance only · never a rescue drug · and the capsule is inhaled, never swallowed.

🧠 The capsule is a cartridge, not a tablet.

🧠 Shake it before you take it

A metered-dose inhaler is a suspension. Unshaken, the first puffs can be mostly propellant.

And its opposite: a dry-powder inhaler is never shaken, and you breathe in fast and deep rather than slow.

🧠 MDI = slow and steady. DPI = fast and deep.

🧠 The FIRST ones make you FALL asleep

First-generation antihistamines (diphenhydramine, chlorpheniramine, hydroxyzine) cross into the brain → sedation plus every anticholinergic effect.

Second generation (loratadine, cetirizine, fexofenadine) mostly stay out. Cetirizine is the drowsiest of the second-generation group.

🧠 The SECOND ones let you keep going.

🧠 Suppress a useless cough. Help a useful one.

Dry, hacking, brings nothing up → antitussive (dextromethorphan, codeine).
Wet, rattly, brings mucus up → expectorant (guaifenesin) plus fluids.

Never suppress a productive cough — trapped secretions become pneumonia.

🧠 Guaifenesin without water is a pill with no partner. About 2 L/day.

🧠 HyperCAP → give BiPAP

High CO₂ (PaCO₂ > 45) — hypercapnic respiratory failure, classically a COPD exacerbation — is the BiPAP patient.

And count the letters: Continuous = ONE pressure. Bi = TWO.

🧠 Restless = low O₂. Sleepy = high CO₂.

🧠 HIGH in the way · LOW is missing

High-pressure ventilator alarm = something is in the WAY — secretions, biting, kinks, water, bronchospasm, pneumothorax.

Low-pressure alarm = something is MISSING — a disconnection, a cuff leak, a tube that has moved.

🧠 Can't fix it fast? Disconnect and BAG on 100% oxygen, and call for help.

🧠 Confirming a tube: WAVE · LISTEN · FILM

Wave — waveform capnography, the most reliable bedside check.
Listen — bilateral breath sounds and over the epigastrium.
Film — chest X-ray for the depth.

Auscultation alone is NOT confirmation.

🧠 Silent on the LEFT = too deep on the RIGHT.

🧠 BAM and SLiM

Bronchodilators = Beta-2 · Anticholinergic · Methylxanthine. "BAM — the airway is open."

Anti-inflammatories = Steroid · Leukotriene inhibitor · Mast-cell stabiliser. "SLiM the swelling down."

🧠 Two acronyms, six families, the whole lower-respiratory drug list.

PART 3 · THE TRAP DRILLS

HOW THEY CATCH YOU

Two grids that answer whole categories of question before you even read the options.

"Which prescription should the nurse question?" is one of the most common respiratory item types there is — and it is almost always one of the red cells on this grid.

🚨 The five "question this order" pairings

  • Beta blocker + asthma — the chemical opposite of a beta-2 agonist; can cause bronchospasm.
  • NSAID + asthma — a subset of asthmatics bronchospasm badly with aspirin and NSAIDs.
  • Anticholinergic + closed-angle glaucoma — pupil dilation can shut the drainage angle.
  • Anticholinergic + urinary retention / BPH — the outlet is already narrowed.
  • Theophylline + cimetidine (or ciprofloxacin) — the level climbs toward toxicity.
🧠 Two questions cover all five: does it DRY? and does it fight the asthma?

✅ The pairing that is NOT a problem

An ARB such as losartan in a client with diabetes is appropriate — it is commonly used specifically because it is kidney-protective in diabetes.

It appears in this question set purely as the "safe" option to make sure you are not just flagging everything.

SEENG-137 ARBs

Do this before you look at the options. Most wrong answers on these items are not knowledge failures — they are reading failures.

⭐ The "requires further teaching" flip

These stems ask for the WRONG statement:

  • "Which statement requires further teaching?"
  • "Which statement indicates a need for further instruction?"
  • "Which response by the client requires follow-up?"

The exact same four options, written as "which statement indicates understanding?", would have a completely different answer.

🧠 Underline the verb in the stem before you read a single option.

🚨 The distractor families to recognise on sight

  • The slow drug offered for a fast problem — salmeterol, fluticasone, montelukast or cromolyn during an attack.
  • The right effect on the wrong drug — "insomnia" attached to codeine, "drowsiness" attached to albuterol.
  • The near-identical name — ipratropium vs tiotropium. Read to the last syllable.
  • The vital sign used as a scare — HR 130 offered as a reason not to give albuterol in a severe attack.
  • The plausible-but-not-first action — suctioning, silencing an alarm, or calling someone before you have assessed.
🧠 Sort every option into "speeds up" or "slows down" and half of them fall away.

🎓 Two more phrases worth reading carefully

"Which is the priority / first action?" — assessment first unless the stem describes an immediate life threat. Airway beats everything.

"Select all that apply." — judge each option as a separate true/false question. Do not look for a pattern, and do not assume there must be exactly three.

🧠 SATA: cover the other options with your hand and answer one at a time.

PART 4 · THE QUESTIONS, WORKED

DO THESE

These are the source infographic's own items — every option explained, not just the key.

📝 QUESTION 1 — inhaled corticosteroid teaching

A client is receiving discharge instructions for an inhaled corticosteroid metered-dose inhaler. Which teaching should the nurse include?

1
"Stop using the fluticasone if the albuterol relieves your symptoms." — Wrong. The steroid is the controller. It is taken every day whether or not the rescue inhaler is working. Stopping it is how the next attack gets built.
2
"Rinse your mouth after each use and do not swallow the water." ✅ — Correct. Rinsing and spitting removes steroid left in the mouth and throat and prevents oral thrush and hoarseness.
3
"If you take albuterol, use it after the steroid." — Backwards. The bronchodilator goes first; it opens the airway so the steroid can reach the small airways.
4
"Steroid inhalers should be used before beta-2 agonists." — Same error as option 3, phrased as a rule instead of an action.
What is really being tested: two facts about steroid inhalers — rinse and spit, and the bronchodilator goes first. Options 3 and 4 are the same wrong idea offered twice, which is a strong hint that the order rule is the point of the question.

📝 QUESTION 2 — which statement requires further teaching?

Which statement by the client requires further teaching?

1
"I will use cromolyn to prevent activity-induced asthma." — Correct as stated. A mast-cell stabiliser is used prophylactically before a known trigger.
2
"I will use montelukast to prevent asthma attacks." — Correct as stated. Montelukast is an oral daily preventer.
3
"I will use a spacer to prevent oral thrush while using beclomethasone." — Correct as stated. A spacer reduces the amount of steroid deposited in the mouth, which is exactly why it reduces thrush.
4
"I will take cromolyn 45 minutes before physical activity." ✅ — This is the statement that needs correcting. Cromolyn is taken shortly before the trigger — commonly taught as around 15 minutes beforehand. Timings differ between products and sources, so follow the specific label and your course material; the testable point is that 45 minutes is too far ahead.
Notice the shape of the item. Three options are straightforwardly true, and the fourth is true in kind but wrong in detail. When three options are clearly correct, stop hunting for a concept error and start checking numbers, timings and doses.

📝 QUESTION 3 — which prescriptions should the nurse question?

Which of the following prescriptions should the nurse question? Select all that apply.

A
Naproxen for a client with asthma ✅ — NSAIDs can trigger bronchospasm in a subset of asthmatics.
B
Ipratropium for a client with glaucoma ✅ — an anticholinergic dilates the pupil, which can close the drainage angle and raise intraocular pressure in closed-angle glaucoma.
C
Losartan for a client with diabetes ❌ — appropriate, and often actively desirable. ARBs are used for their kidney protection in diabetes. This is the safe option planted to catch over-flagging.
D
Theophylline for a client taking cimetidine ✅ — cimetidine slows theophylline clearance, so the serum level climbs toward toxicity.
E
Atenolol for a client with asthma ✅ — beta blockers oppose bronchodilation and can precipitate bronchospasm.
Four out of five are correct answers here, which feels wrong and makes people second-guess. SATA items have no quota. Judge each line on its own: does this drug fight the disease, or does it collide with another drug or a contraindicated condition?

📝 QUESTION 4 — teaching for four drugs at once

What patient teaching should be included with new prescriptions of albuterol, ibuprofen, tiotropium and beclomethasone? Select all that apply.

1
"Tinnitus is an expected side effect." ❌ — ringing in the ears is a warning sign associated with salicylate toxicity, not an expected effect to accept. Never teach a toxicity sign as "expected".
2
"Tachycardia is expected after albuterol." ✅ — beta-1 spill-over. Expected and monitored.
3
"Report dark, tarry stool to the provider." ✅ — this is the ibuprofen in the list. NSAIDs cause GI bleeding, and melaena is the sign.
4
"Drink fluids to prevent dry mouth and throat." ✅ — this is the tiotropium. Anticholinergic dryness, treated with fluids and sugar-free gum.
5
"Ipratropium is used first during an attack." ❌ — two errors. Albuterol is used first, and ipratropium is not even on this list — the client was prescribed tiotropium, which is maintenance only.
This is a list-comprehension question. Work drug by drug: albuterol → tachycardia · ibuprofen → GI bleeding · tiotropium → dry mouth · beclomethasone → rinse and spit. Then check whether the option is even talking about a drug that is on the list. Option 5 fails that test twice.

📝 QUESTION 5 — tachycardia AND dysrhythmias

Which medication prescribed for asthma causes tachycardia and dysrhythmias?

1
Phenobarbital ❌ — a barbiturate sedative. Not an asthma drug; it would lower a theophylline level if anything.
2
Aminophylline ✅ — the IV methylxanthine. Dysrhythmias are a hallmark of its toxicity, and it has a narrow therapeutic index with a serum level you can measure.
3
Salmeterol ❌ — a LABA. Some cardiac effect, but it is not the drug whose toxicity is defined by dysrhythmias.
4
Albuterol ❌ — causes tachycardia, yes, but as an expected effect, not a monitored toxicity.
The discriminating word is "dysrhythmias". Beta-2 agonists give a fast but regular heart. Methylxanthine toxicity gives a fast and irregular one — and it is the drug you draw levels for. Whenever a stem adds a second, more specific symptom, that symptom is the discriminator.

This is the question the source labels "most commonly missed". It is missed because the tachycardia in the stem looks like a reason to withhold albuterol.

📝 QUESTION 6 — the most commonly missed one

A client with severe asthma has a heart rate over 120, a respiratory rate over 30, an SpO₂ under 90% on room air, and a peak expiratory flow under 40% of predicted. Which medications would you anticipate? Select all that apply.

1
Inhaled salmeterol ❌ — a LABA. Slow onset, and never a rescue drug. This is the trap option and it appears in nearly every version of this item.
2
Albuterol inhaler ✅ — the rescue drug, first.
3
Nebulised ipratropium ✅ — added to albuterol in severe exacerbations. The "I" in AIM.
4
"IV methamphetamines" ❌ — not a real prescription. Read the drug names carefully; this option exists to catch pattern-matching on "meth-".
5
IV methylprednisolone ✅ — the systemic steroid, given early because it takes hours to work.
Why it is missed: students see HR > 120, remember that albuterol causes tachycardia, and withhold it. But the tachycardia is caused by hypoxia and the work of breathing — treating the asthma is what fixes the heart rate. Airway first. Always. The answers here are simply A · I · M.

⭐ Four more one-liners from the batch, as quick self-tests

A client using albuterol has had no relief after 3 doses. What now?

Notify the provider. Do not keep dosing and hoping — this is an escalation point. Also assess for a silent chest, rising drowsiness, and inability to speak a sentence.

How do you know the albuterol worked?

Respiratory rate falling (for example 34 → 24), SpO₂ at least 90%, wheeze quieter, accessory-muscle use easing, sentences getting longer.

Which respiratory failure gets BiPAP?

Hypercapnic — PaCO₂ over 45, classically a COPD exacerbation. Two pressures move air, and moving air is what clears CO₂. HyperCAP → BiPAP.

What confirms an endotracheal tube is in the trachea?

Waveform capnography at the bedside, supported by bilateral breath sounds and confirmed for depth by chest X-ray. Auscultation alone is not confirmation.

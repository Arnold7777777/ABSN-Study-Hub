🦠 COPD — Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease

Air gets in and cannot get back out. Everything else — the barrel chest, the pursed lips, the low oxygen target, the reason a high flow of oxygen is dangerous — follows from that one sentence.

🧬 What it is

COPD is airflow limitation that is not fully reversible. The problem is getting air OUT, not in. Air trapped behind narrowed, floppy airways stacks up breath after breath.

Air trapping is the one idea. Barrel chest, prolonged expiration, low diaphragm, high residual volume — all of it is trapped air.

Most patients have both. The two pictures are a teaching device — use them to explain findings, not to label a real patient.

🤵 What causes it

  • Smoking — far and away the biggest cause, and the only one she can change
  • Long-term occupational dust, fumes and chemical exposure
  • Indoor biomass smoke from cooking fires
  • Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency — suspect it in emphysema under 45, or with no smoking history

👀 How it shows up

  • Dyspnea on exertion that creeps up over years — the first real complaint
  • Prolonged expiration — an I:E ratio of 1:3 or 1:4 instead of 1:2
  • Pursed-lip breathing — back-pressure that splints the airways open. She invented it herself; teach it, do not stop it
  • Tripod position — leaning forward on the arms to recruit accessory muscles
  • Barrel chest — anteroposterior diameter approaching the transverse. Trapped air
  • Wheezes and diminished breath sounds; a hyperresonant percussion note
  • Clubbing, chronic cough, weight loss, and a hematocrit that rises to compensate

A silent chest in a wheezing COPD patient is worse, not better. Wheezing needs moving air. When the wheeze disappears and the patient is working harder, that is impending respiratory failure — call, do not reassure.

🧪 How it is confirmed

Spirometry after a bronchodilator is the diagnosis. An FEV1/FVC ratio under 0.70 that does not fully reverse confirms obstruction. That ratio is what separates COPD from asthma, which does reverse.

Read the bicarbonate to date the problem. A high CO2 with a high bicarbonate and a normal pH is chronic and compensated — her baseline. A high CO2 with a normal bicarbonate and a low pH is acute, and she is decompensating now.

🩺 What you do

💊 The drugs, in the order they are given

1 · Bronchodilator firstShort-acting beta-2 agonist (albuterol) or ipratropium — it opens the airway
2 · Inhaled steroid secondFluticasone, budesonide — it reaches deeper once the airway is open
3 · Rinse the mouthAfter every steroid dose, or oral candidiasis follows
4 · MaintenanceLong-acting agents (tiotropium, salmeterol) scheduled — never for a rescue
  • Theophylline — narrow window, therapeutic 10-20 mcg/mL. Toxicity: nausea, tachycardia, seizures
  • Systemic steroids for exacerbations, then tapered — not maintenance
  • Antibiotics when sputum turns purulent or increases in volume
  • Vaccines — influenza yearly and pneumococcal. One of the few things that lowers mortality

🧠 Nursing care that changes the outcome

  • Stop smoking. The single intervention that slows the decline. Everything else treats symptoms
  • Teach pursed-lip breathing — in through the nose 2 counts, out through pursed lips 4 counts
  • Diaphragmatic breathing, and tripod positioning when breathless
  • High Fowler or leaning forward over a table
  • Small, frequent, high-calorie, high-protein meals — a full stomach pushes on a flattened diaphragm, and the work of breathing burns calories
  • Rest before eating; eating is exertion
  • Fluids 2-3 L/day unless restricted, to thin secretions
  • Pulmonary rehabilitation, and pace activity with rest between

🧤 Oxygen — the part that is tested hardest

Target 88-92%, not 95%. Give 1-3 L/min by nasal cannula, or a Venturi mask when the percentage must be exact.

A chronic CO2 retainer's drive to breathe can shift from CO2 to low oxygen. Flood that with high-flow oxygen and the drive to breathe goes away.

But never withhold oxygen from a hypoxic patient. If she is severely hypoxic, give it — and watch the respiratory rate and level of consciousness, ready to support ventilation.

Never give a nonselective beta blocker (propranolol) in COPD — it causes bronchospasm. A cardioselective one (metoprolol) is used with care when the heart needs it.

The three questions this page answers on an exam. Why is expiration prolonged? — air trapping. What is the oxygen target? — 88-92%. Which comes first, the inhaler or the steroid? — the bronchodilator.

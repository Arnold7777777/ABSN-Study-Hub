Lung volumes & pulmonary function tests

See the air you can move, the air you cannot blow out, and the equipment that measures each.

TLC = ALL the air after the biggest breath in.
VC = the air you can move from a full breath in to a full breath out.
The difference is residual volume (RV) — the air left behind.

🫁 The whole lung-volume picture

Volumes are individual amounts of air; capacities combine two or more volumes. TLC and VC are measurements within pulmonary function testing (PFT), rather than names for separate machines. [1]

TLC = VC + RV

The exam trap: ordinary spirometry measures air moving through the mouth. It cannot measure the air that stays inside after maximal exhalation, so it cannot directly measure RV, FRC or TLC. Lung-volume testing is needed. [1]

What each volume or capacity measures

TV / VT · Tidal volume

Air moved in or out during one quiet breath.

Tidal = the ordinary tide moving in and out.

IRV · Inspiratory reserve volume

Extra air you can breathe IN after a normal inspiration.

I = extra IN.

ERV · Expiratory reserve volume

Extra air you can breathe OUT after a normal expiration.

E = extra EXIT.

RV · Residual volume

Air remaining after your biggest possible exhalation.

R = REMAINS; the locked basement.

VC · Vital capacity

Maximum movable air between full inspiration and full expiration. It can be measured with a slow or a forced maneuver.

VC = IRV + TV + ERV

TLC · Total lung capacity

All air in the lungs at the end of a maximum inspiration, including RV.

TLC = IRV + TV + ERV + RV

IC · Inspiratory capacity

Maximum air you can inhale after a normal, relaxed exhalation.

IC = TV + IRV

FRC · Functional residual capacity

Air left in the lungs after a normal, relaxed exhalation.

FRC = ERV + RV

Definitions and formulas: NHLBI [1]; measurement methods: ERS/ATS [3]. Compare with the lab’s predicted values and lower limit of normal; there is no single normal lung size for every person.

What the tests physically look like

💨 Spirometry · moving air

  • Looks like: nose clip, mouthpiece and a tube or handheld flow sensor connected to a computer.
  • You do: breathe in fully, seal your lips, then blast the air out hard and keep blowing as coached; repeat for usable results.
  • Measures: FVC, FEV₁ and flows; may be repeated after a bronchodilator.

ATS patient guide [2]

🚪 Body plethysmography · lung size

  • Looks like: a clear, enclosed booth with a seat, nose clip and mouthpiece. A technologist coaches you.
  • You do: breathe and gently pant against a briefly closed shutter.
  • Measures: thoracic gas volume from pressure changes; combined maneuvers give TLC, RV and FRC.
  • Alternative: gas-dilution/washout testing uses a breathing circuit. It may miss some poorly communicating trapped gas.

NHLBI [1]; ERS/ATS [3]

🫧 DLCO · gas transfer

  • Looks like: a mouthpiece and nose clip at the lung-function machine; usually no exercise.
  • You do: inhale a test-gas mixture, briefly hold your breath, then exhale as directed.
  • Measures: transfer of a very small amount of carbon monoxide from alveoli toward the blood. It estimates gas-transfer capacity, not oxygen saturation.

NHLBI [1]

🌬️ Peak flow · fastest blow

  • Looks like: a small handheld meter with a mouthpiece and a scale or display.
  • Measures: peak expiratory flow (PEF), the fastest airflow during a forced expiration.
  • Remember: flow is a rate (often L/min); lung volume is an amount (L or mL).
  • An incentive spirometer is a breathing-exercise device; it does not replace diagnostic PFTs.

NHLBI [1]; MedlinePlus [6]

📈 What the results look like

A report commonly includes a table of measured and predicted values, a volume–time curve and a flow–volume loop. The graphs below are teaching examples, not real patient results.

FEV₁ · first second

Forced expiratory volume in 1 second: the amount exhaled during the first second of the forced maneuver. This is a volume, not a flow rate.

The “1” is your one-second stopwatch.

FVC · full forced breath out

Forced vital capacity: the total volume forcefully exhaled after maximum inspiration. FVC can be lower than slow VC when airways close early during forced expiration.

FVC = FULL forced volume.

FEV₁/FVC · fraction out in 1 second

Example: FEV₁ 3.2 L ÷ FVC 4.0 L = 0.80 or 80%. These are teaching numbers, not universal normal values.

A reduced ratio suggests airflow obstruction; interpretation depends on the clinical question and reference standard.

Check quality before the numbers

Weak effort, coughing or stopping early can change results. A low FVC can reflect air trapping, incomplete exhalation or restriction; TLC is needed to confirm restriction.

NHLBI [1]; ERS/ATS interpretation standard [4].

COPD vs. restriction: air trapped or room too small?

Obstructive pattern · OUT is slow

  • FEV₁/FVC falls: airflow gets out slowly.
  • RV may increase: air trapping.
  • TLC may increase: hyperinflation; it is not elevated in every person with COPD.
  • For COPD diagnosis, post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.70 supports persistent airflow obstruction in the appropriate clinical setting. FEV₁ helps grade airflow limitation.

Restrictive pattern · ROOM is small

  • TLC below the lower limit of normal confirms restriction.
  • FVC is often reduced, with FEV₁/FVC preserved or increased.
  • A small FVC by itself does not confirm restriction.
  • DLCO adds information about gas transfer; it is interpreted alongside the cause and other findings.

ERS/ATS [4]; GOLD Science Committee [5].

Nursing prep and coaching

  1. Check instructions: follow the testing service’s directions for inhalers, smoking, exercise and meals. Do not independently stop prescribed medicines.
  2. Report concerns: recent chest/abdominal/eye surgery, pneumothorax, unstable cardiac symptoms or an acute respiratory infection may require review before testing.
  3. Coach technique: upright posture, good mouth seal, nose clip and the full maneuver. Expect repeated attempts for reproducible results.
  4. Watch tolerance: stop and notify the testing clinician for chest pain, marked dizziness or significant distress.
  5. Document context: symptoms, medication timing and bronchodilator administration, effort/quality and the actual measured results.

ATS patient guide [2]; MedlinePlus [6]. Local laboratory instructions govern the appointment.

NG-418

🫁 Chest Tubes & Drainage Systems

Three chambers, and a question for each. Nearly every chest-tube item on an exam is really asking which chamber is this about — because the same bubbling means opposite things depending on where you see it.

⭐ The one idea

The tube is in the pleural space, not in the lung. It drains air or fluid out of the gap around the lung so the lung can re-expand. Nothing about a chest tube makes sense until that is fixed in your head.

And the drainage unit is not one thing — it is three chambers in a row, each answering a different question. Bubbling in the water seal is a problem. Bubbling in the suction chamber may be the system working. Same bubbles, opposite meaning.

🖼️ The whole system on one plate

💧 The three chambers, left to right

🧿
1 · Collection

How much, and what does it look like? Measure the output and mark the level with the time. Note the colour and whether it has changed.

Sudden heavy bright-red drainage, or output that stops abruptly, both need reporting.

🌊
2 · Water seal

Is air still leaking out of the chest? This chamber is a one-way valve — air can leave, nothing comes back in.

Tidaling (the water rising and falling with her breathing) is expected. Continuous bubbling here suggests an air leak.

💨
3 · Suction control

How hard is the system pulling? On a wet system, gentle bubbling here may be expected — it is the water column regulating the suction, not a leak.

A dry-suction system behaves differently and does not bubble the same way. Read the unit in front of you.

The trap: “bubbling” on its own is not an answer. Water seal bubbling = leak. Suction chamber bubbling on a wet system = working. The question always tells you which chamber — read it twice.

🔍 What you actually see, and what it means

Tidaling in the water sealWater rises and falls with each breath. Expected. It shows the tube is patent and connected to the pleural space.
Tidaling stopsEither the lung has re-expanded (good, and the rest of the picture will agree) or the tube is kinked, clamped or obstructed. Check the tubing along its whole length first.
Continuous bubbling in the water sealSuggests an air leak. Work from the client outward — dressing, connections, then the unit. Report it.
Gentle bubbling in the wet suction chamberMay be expected on that kind of unit. Vigorous bubbling just evaporates the water faster; it does not increase the suction.
Crepitus round the insertion siteAir in the subcutaneous tissue — it crackles under your fingers. Mark the border and report; it can spread.
New or sudden breathlessnessAssess her, not the device, first. Sudden distress is an escalation, not a troubleshooting exercise.

✅ Day-to-day care

  • Unit upright and below the level of her chest, always. Above chest level and drainage runs back toward her.
  • Keep the tubing free of kinks and dependent loops. A loop full of fluid stops the drainage without anything looking wrong.
  • Do not routinely clamp or strip the tubing. Stripping generates high negative pressure inside the chest, and clamping a tube that is still leaking air can trap it. Both are device- and order-specific, not routine nursing.
  • Encourage deep breathing, coughing and incentive spirometry. Re-expanding the lung is the point of the whole thing.
  • Assess the dressing and the site each shift — drainage, odour, crepitus, and that the dressing is occlusive where it should be.
  • Document output with a time-marked level, not a running total in your head.

🚨 When something comes apart

Assess her breathing first. Then follow the device and facility emergency protocol — the specifics differ by system, and the protocol is the authority, not a rule you memorised.

Tubing disconnects from the unit. Call for help and follow the unit's protocol. Some facilities direct submerging the tube end in sterile water or saline to restore a seal while help arrives — check what yours says, and do not improvise.

The tube comes out of her chest. Cover the site as directed and get help immediately. Never reinsert a chest tube. That is not a nursing procedure under any circumstance.

The unit tips over or breaks. Set it upright, check the water levels, assess her, and report. A broken unit gets replaced, not repaired.

✂️ Coming out

The provider removes it. What the nurse does is the coaching: she takes a deep breath and either holds it or bears down while the tube comes out — that raises the pressure inside her chest so air cannot be sucked back in through the tract.

An occlusive dressing goes straight on. Afterwards watch for breathlessness, chest pain and crepitus, and expect a chest X-ray to confirm the lung stayed up.

⭐ High yield

  • The tube is in the pleural space, not the lung
  • Tidaling in the water seal = expected. Tidaling stopping = re-expanded or obstructed
  • Continuous bubbling in the water seal = air leak. Bubbling in the wet suction chamber may be normal
  • Unit upright, below chest level, tubing unkinked and off the floor in loops
  • Do not routinely clamp or strip — and never reinsert a tube that has come out
  • On removal: deep breath, hold or bear down, then an occlusive dressing
  • Sudden breathlessness → assess her first, then the device

Magnesium sulphate🚨 DANGER

In obstetrics, magnesium sulfate is given IV to PREVENT AND TREAT SEIZURES in preeclampsia and eclampsia. It is also used for fetal neuroprotection in preterm birth under 32 weeks and for short-term tocolysis. It is a CNS depressant and smooth muscle relaxant, and a high-alert drug.

  • The FIRST sign of toxicity is LOSS OF DEEP TENDON REFLEXES. Check the patellar reflex before each dose and hourly during infusion.
  • HOLD the infusion and notify the provider for absent DTRs, respirations under 12 to 16 per minute, urine output under 30 mL/hr (magnesium is renally cleared), or a decreased level of consciousness.
  • ANTIDOTE: CALCIUM GLUCONATE IV. Keep it at the bedside whenever magnesium is infusing.
  • Therapeutic serum range in preeclampsia is roughly 4 to 7 mEq/L (normal is 1.3 to 2.1). Levels around 10 and above cause respiratory paralysis, then cardiac arrest.
  • Flushing, warmth, sweating, and lethargy are expected, not toxicity. Monitor the fetal heart rate; the newborn may be hypotonic. Non-obstetric uses: torsades de pointes, severe asthma exacerbation, and hypomagnesemia.

Reflexes, Respirations, urine Output. Lose a reflex, stop the drip and grab calcium gluconate.

Introduction to Pharmacology & Herbal Supplements

Bioavailability⭐ HIGH YIELD

Bioavailability is the fraction of an administered dose that actually reaches systemic circulation in active form. IV is 100% by definition; oral is lower and more variable because of incomplete absorption plus first-pass metabolism.

  • Rough order: IV (100%) > IM/subQ > sublingual, inhaled, transdermal > oral > rectal (variable).
  • Anything that reduces absorption or increases first-pass metabolism reduces bioavailability.
  • Formulation matters: brand-to-generic switches can matter for narrow-therapeutic-index drugs like levothyroxine, warfarin, and phenytoin.
  • Food, gastric pH, and other drugs change oral bioavailability; IV has none of those variables.

Bioavailability = how much made it to the party, not how much left the house.

Black cohosh

Black cohosh is taken for MENOPAUSE symptoms: hot flashes, night sweats, irritability, and sleep disturbance, plus PMS and painful periods. Its main contraindications are pregnancy, liver disease, and estrogen-sensitive cancers.

  • Contraindicated in pregnancy because it may stimulate uterine contractions, and in breastfeeding.
  • HEPATOTOXICITY is the key safety warning. Teach the patient to report jaundice, dark urine, right upper quadrant pain, nausea, and unusual fatigue. Avoid with other hepatotoxic drugs.
  • Use cautiously with breast or uterine cancer, hormone therapy, and tamoxifen.
  • May add to antihypertensives and cause hypotension; may reduce the effect of some statins.
  • Do not confuse with blue cohosh, which is a different and more toxic plant.

Black cohosh cools the flash but watch the liver.

Echinacea⭐ HIGH YIELD

Echinacea is taken to PREVENT or shorten the common cold and upper respiratory infections by stimulating the immune system. That is exactly why it is CONTRAINDICATED in autoimmune disease and in transplant or immunosuppressed patients.

  • Avoid in multiple sclerosis, lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, HIV, tuberculosis, and with cyclosporine, tacrolimus, or corticosteroids: it works against the drugs suppressing the immune system.
  • Cross-allergy with ragweed, daisies, chrysanthemums, and marigolds (Asteraceae family).
  • Inhibits CYP1A2 and CYP3A4, so it can raise levels of some drugs. Limit continuous use (commonly 8 weeks or less) because of reported hepatotoxicity.
  • Evidence for actual benefit is weak and inconsistent.
  • Common effects: GI upset, rash, and a temporary tingling of the tongue.

Echinacea REVS the immune system, so never give it to someone whose immune system we are trying to shut down.

Ginger

Ginger is taken for NAUSEA AND VOMITING (motion sickness, postoperative nausea, and morning sickness of pregnancy) and for osteoarthritis pain. Like the other G-herbs, high doses increase bleeding risk.

  • It is one of the safer options for pregnancy-related nausea, but doses should stay modest and be cleared with the provider.
  • Caution with warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel, and NSAIDs; hold before surgery.
  • May lower blood glucose and blood pressure, so it is additive with antidiabetics and antihypertensives.
  • High doses cause heartburn and GI upset. Use caution with gallstones because it increases bile flow.

Ginger for the gut. Still a G-herb, still bleeds.

Pharmacokinetics – absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion⭐ HIGH YIELD

Pharmacokinetics is what the BODY does to the DRUG, in four steps: Absorption (into the bloodstream), Distribution (blood to tissues), Metabolism (the liver changes it), and Excretion (the kidney removes it). Pharmacodynamics is the reverse: what the drug does to the body.

  • Only FREE, unbound drug is pharmacologically active.
  • Liver disease means reduce the dose. Kidney disease means reduce the dose or lengthen the interval.
  • Half-life, steady state, bioavailability, and the first-pass effect all come out of these four steps.
  • Two organs run the whole show: the liver metabolizes, the kidney excretes.

Physiological changes in the elderly⭐ HIGH YIELD

Older adults have decreased gastric acid and GI motility, decreased total body water with increased body fat, decreased serum albumin, decreased hepatic mass and CYP enzyme activity, and decreased GFR. The net effect is higher drug levels lasting longer, plus increased CNS sensitivity. Start low and go slow.

  • Increased CNS sensitivity means more sedation, confusion, and falls from benzodiazepines, opioids, and anticholinergics.
  • Blunted baroreceptor reflex means more orthostatic hypotension; thinner skin changes topical and transdermal absorption.
  • Pair this with the Beers criteria and polypharmacy: the three geriatric pharmacology topics are always tested together.

Routes of administration – How do different routes of administration affect bioavailability?⭐ HIGH YIELD

Bioavailability is the fraction of a dose that reaches systemic circulation. Routes that bypass the GI tract and the liver's first pass have the HIGHEST bioavailability, with IV at 100% by definition. ORAL is the lowest and most variable because of incomplete absorption plus first-pass metabolism.

  • Same drug, different dose by route: oral morphine is dosed roughly three times the IV dose for the same effect. That gap IS bioavailability.
  • Food, gastric pH, GI motility, and other drugs change oral bioavailability. IV has none of those variables.
  • Enteric-coated and extended-release forms deliberately slow absorption, which is why they are never crushed.
  • Nitroglycerin is given sublingually precisely because oral nitroglycerin is destroyed on first pass.

Saw palmetto⭐ HIGH YIELD

Saw palmetto is taken for BENIGN PROSTATIC HYPERPLASIA: urinary frequency, urgency, nocturia, and weak stream. It acts like a weak 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor, reducing conversion of testosterone to DHT.

  • Contraindicated in pregnancy and in anyone who could become pregnant because of possible harm to a male fetus. Pregnant women should not handle it.

Saw palmetto saves the stream and saws down the PSA number.

Therapeutic index⭐ HIGH YIELD

Therapeutic index (TI) = TD50 divided by ED50 (the toxic dose in 50% of subjects over the effective dose in 50%). A HIGH/wide TI means a large safety margin; a LOW/narrow TI means toxic and therapeutic doses are close together and the drug needs serum level monitoring.

  • The therapeutic range or window is the concentration between the minimum effective level and the toxic level.
  • For narrow-TI drugs also watch renal and hepatic function, drug interactions, and brand-to-generic switches.
  • Practical example: a digoxin level of 2.5 ng/mL is already toxic while the target is roughly 0.5 to 2 ng/mL.

HIV antiretroviral drug classifications⭐ HIGH YIELD

The main antiretroviral classes are NRTIs, NNRTIs, protease inhibitors, integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs), and entry inhibitors (CCR5 antagonists, fusion inhibitors, attachment inhibitors). Standard therapy combines at least 3 drugs from 2 or more classes, most often 2 NRTIs plus an INSTI.

  • ADHERENCE is everything. Missed doses breed resistance, so the regimen is never skipped or stopped.
  • Abacavir requires HLA-B*5701 testing before use because the hypersensitivity reaction can be fatal.
  • Ritonavir and cobicistat are 'boosters': CYP3A4 inhibitors used to raise other drugs' levels, which means huge interaction potential.
  • NRTI class effect: lactic acidosis with hepatomegaly and steatosis. Zidovudine causes bone marrow suppression.
  • St John's wort and rifampin destroy antiretroviral levels and cause treatment failure.

Bone marrow suppression – What are potential causes of this adverse effect? What are the🚨 DANGER

Bone marrow suppression is caused most often by cytotoxic chemotherapy and radiation, but also by immunosuppressants and a long list of ordinary drugs. It presents as neutropenia (infection), anemia (fatigue and pallor), and thrombocytopenia (bruising and bleeding), with the nadir typically 7 to 14 days after chemotherapy.

  • Antineoplastics: alkylating agents, antimetabolites (methotrexate, 5-FU), anthracyclines, platinum agents, taxanes.
  • Immunosuppressants: azathioprine, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide, tacrolimus, cyclosporine, TNF inhibitors.
  • Non-cancer drugs: chloramphenicol, CLOZAPINE (agranulocytosis, requires mandatory ANC monitoring), methimazole and PTU, carbamazepine, linezolid, ganciclovir, zidovudine, sulfonamides, phenytoin.
  • Supportive treatment: filgrastim or pegfilgrastim for neutrophils, epoetin or darbepoetin for red cells, romiplostim for platelets, plus transfusions.
  • Nursing: monitor CBC with differential and ANC, neutropenic and bleeding precautions, no live vaccines, and treat a fever of 100.4 F as an emergency.

Chemo hits the fastest-growing cells: marrow, mouth, hair, and gut. That is why counts crash, mouths ulcerate, hair falls, and stools loosen.

Live vaccines – Which vaccines are examples of live vaccines? When should live vaccines be🚨 DANGER

Live attenuated vaccines include MMR, varicella, the old live zoster vaccine (Zostavax; Shingrix is NOT live), rotavirus, intranasal influenza (LAIV), yellow fever, oral typhoid, BCG, and smallpox. AVOID them in pregnancy, in severely immunocompromised patients, and in anyone on high-dose immunosuppressive therapy.

  • Severely immunocompromised includes active chemotherapy, hematologic or solid tumors, transplant, congenital immunodeficiency, advanced HIV, and prednisone at 20 mg/day or more (or 2 mg/kg) for 2 weeks or longer.
  • Pregnancy: avoid live vaccines, and avoid pregnancy for about 4 weeks after MMR or varicella.
  • If two live vaccines are not given on the same day, separate them by at least 28 days.
  • Recent immune globulin or blood products blunt the response to live vaccines. Wait the product-specific interval, often 3 to 11 months.
  • Household contacts of an immunocompromised patient CAN generally receive MMR and varicella. This is a common exam distractor.

Live vaccines and low immune systems do not mix: MMR, Varicella, Rotavirus, Yellow fever, LAIV, BCG, oral Typhoid.

Levodopa/carbidopa⭐ HIGH YIELD

Levodopa is a dopamine precursor that crosses the blood-brain barrier and is converted to dopamine to treat Parkinson's disease. Carbidopa blocks that conversion in the PERIPHERY, so more levodopa reaches the brain and there is far less nausea.

  • Avoid HIGH-PROTEIN meals with the dose: dietary amino acids compete with levodopa for absorption and blunt the effect.
  • Harmless but alarming: sweat, saliva, and urine may turn dark red, brown, or black. Warn the patient in advance.
  • Orthostatic hypotension is common. Rise slowly and use fall precautions.
  • Long-term: dyskinesias, wearing-off, and the on-off phenomenon (abrupt loss of effect). Doses must be given ON TIME, to the minute.
  • Do NOT stop abruptly: risk of a neuroleptic malignant-like syndrome. Avoid with MAOIs (hypertensive crisis) and with antipsychotics or metoclopramide, which are dopamine blockers that cancel it out. Report hallucinations, confusion, sudden sleep attacks, and impulse-control behaviors such as gambling, spending, and hypersexuality.

Carbidopa is the bodyguard: it keeps levodopa from being mugged before it reaches the brain.

Opioids – What adverse effects are associated with this class of drugs?🚨 DANGER

The dangerous adverse effect is RESPIRATORY DEPRESSION; the universal one is CONSTIPATION, which never improves with tolerance. Also expect sedation, nausea and vomiting, pruritus, urinary retention, orthostatic hypotension, miosis, and physical dependence and tolerance.

  • SEDATION PRECEDES RESPIRATORY DEPRESSION. Increasing drowsiness is your early warning, so assess sedation level along with respiratory rate.
  • Antidote is naloxone. Watch for re-narcotization because naloxone's half-life is shorter than that of most opioids.
  • Constipation: start a stimulant laxative plus a stool softener at the same time as the opioid. Tolerance to constipation never develops.
  • Overdose triad: pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression, and decreased level of consciousness or coma.
  • Highest risk: opioid-naive patients, sleep apnea, older adults, and concurrent benzodiazepines or alcohol (a black-box warning combination). Meperidine is avoided because of a neurotoxic metabolite causing seizures, especially in older adults and renal impairment.

Pinpoint pupils, pinpoint breathing. Sedation comes before respiratory arrest, so catch it there.

Smoking cessation treatment options⭐ HIGH YIELD

Three pharmacologic options: nicotine replacement therapy (patch, gum, lozenge, inhaler, nasal spray), bupropion SR (Zyban), and varenicline (Chantix). Varenicline is generally the most effective, and combining a long-acting patch with a short-acting NRT beats either one alone.

  • Both pills are started BEFORE the quit date, and the patient keeps smoking during that lead-in period.
  • Never combine bupropion (Zyban) with bupropion (Wellbutrin): it is the same drug and the seizure risk stacks.
  • Pair every drug with counseling or behavioral support. The combination roughly doubles quit rates.
  • Smoking INDUCES CYP1A2, so quitting RAISES levels of clozapine, olanzapine, theophylline, and warfarin. Doses may need to come down.

Extrapyramidal symptoms – What are the signs/symptoms? What causes this adverse effect?⭐ HIGH YIELD

Extrapyramidal symptoms are drug-induced movement disorders caused by DOPAMINE (D2) BLOCKADE in the nigrostriatal pathway. The classic causes are first-generation antipsychotics (haloperidol, fluphenazine) and also metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, and promethazine.

  • Second-generation (atypical) antipsychotics cause much less EPS, but not zero, especially risperidone at higher doses.
  • Anticholinergics treat dystonia and pseudoparkinsonism but can WORSEN tardive dyskinesia.
  • Screen with the AIMS (Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale) at baseline and regularly.
  • Do not confuse with neuroleptic malignant syndrome: fever, lead-pipe rigidity, altered mental status, autonomic instability, and elevated CPK. That is an emergency treated with dantrolene and bromocriptine.

Adrenergic receptors – What are the effects on body tissues from stimulation of alpha-1, alpha-⭐ HIGH YIELD

Alpha-1: vasoconstriction (raises BP), pupil dilation, bladder neck and prostate contraction (urinary retention). Alpha-2: presynaptic feedback, so stimulating it DECREASES sympathetic outflow (lowers BP and HR, sedation). Beta-1: the HEART, increased rate, contractility, conduction, and renin release. Beta-2: the LUNGS and smooth muscle, bronchodilation, vasodilation, uterine relaxation, raised glucose. Beta-3: bladder relaxation and lipolysis.

  • Non-selective beta blockers hit beta-2 and cause bronchospasm, which is why they are avoided in asthma.

Malignant hyperthermia – What are the risk factors for developing this condition?🚨 DANGER

The core risk factor is a GENETIC predisposition, autosomal dominant, usually an RYR1 ryanodine receptor mutation (also CACNA1S), combined with exposure to a trigger: volatile inhaled anesthetics or succinylcholine.

  • A personal or FAMILY history of malignant hyperthermia, or of an unexplained anesthesia death or high fever during surgery, is the biggest red flag. Ask about it at every preoperative assessment.
  • Associated muscle disorders raise risk: central core disease, multiminicore disease, King-Denborough syndrome, and some muscular dystrophies.
  • More often reported in males, children, and young adults, but it can happen at any age.
  • PRIOR UNEVENTFUL ANESTHESIA DOES NOT RULE IT OUT. Many patients have had safe anesthesia before their first episode.
  • Susceptibility is confirmed by caffeine-halothane contracture testing or genetic testing. Susceptible patients get a trigger-free anesthetic: propofol, ketamine, opioids, nitrous oxide, or regional anesthesia.

Genes plus gas (or sux) equals crisis. Always ask about family reactions to anesthesia.

Sympathetic response – What are the effects of sympathetic stimulation on different body⭐ HIGH YIELD

Sympathetic (fight or flight) stimulation shifts blood and energy toward survival: heart rate and contractility UP, blood pressure UP, bronchioles DILATE, pupils DILATE, blood glucose UP, and everything non-essential (digestion, urination, salivation) slows DOWN.

  • Neurotransmitters: sympathetic uses norepinephrine and epinephrine at adrenergic receptors; parasympathetic uses acetylcholine at muscarinic receptors.
  • Adrenergic drugs mimic this response; beta blockers and alpha blockers oppose it.
  • Anticholinergics look sympathetic because they remove the parasympathetic brake: dry mouth, tachycardia, dilated pupils, urinary retention.

Asthma – What are the treatment options for an acute asthma exacerbation?🚨 DANGER

SABA first: inhaled albuterol by nebulizer or MDI with spacer, repeated, often with ipratropium added. Give SYSTEMIC CORTICOSTEROIDS early (oral prednisone or IV methylprednisolone) and oxygen to keep SpO2 at 90 to 92% or higher. For severe or refractory attacks add IV magnesium sulfate, continuous nebulized albuterol, subcutaneous epinephrine or terbutaline, and prepare for intubation.

  • The standard emergency pattern: albuterol 2.5 to 5 mg nebulized every 20 minutes for 3 doses, then reassess.
  • Steroids take 4 to 6 hours to work, which is exactly why they are started EARLY rather than last.
  • Do NOT use long-acting beta agonists (salmeterol), cromolyn, or montelukast for an acute attack. Those are controllers.
  • Ominous signs: a SILENT CHEST (no wheeze because no air is moving), inability to speak in full sentences, accessory muscle use, altered mental status, and exhaustion.
  • A rising or 'normalizing' PaCO2 in a struggling asthmatic means impending respiratory failure, not improvement. Sit the patient upright, keep them calm, and monitor peak flow and pulse oximetry continuously.

Oxygen, albuterol, steroids. And a silent chest is the loudest warning you will get.

Eye drop administration⭐ HIGH YIELD

Wash hands, verify the drug and which eye (OD right, OS left, OU both), tilt the head back, pull the LOWER LID down to form a pocket, instill the drop into the conjunctival sac without touching the eye or lashes, have the patient close the eye gently, then press the inner canthus for 30 to 60 seconds.

  • Punctal occlusion (pressing the inner corner) blocks the tear duct and prevents systemic absorption. This matters most with timolol and other beta blocker drops, which can cause bradycardia and bronchospasm.
  • Rest the dominant hand on the patient's forehead to stabilize it, and have the patient look up and away from the tip.
  • Wait 5 MINUTES between different eye drops. Give drops before ointments. Remove contact lenses first and wait about 15 minutes to reinsert.
  • Never touch the dropper to the eye, lashes, or any surface, and recap immediately; contamination causes serious eye infections.
  • Blot excess with a tissue from the inner to the outer canthus, and never share eye drops between patients.

Antiulcer, proton pump inhibitor, Benzimidazole

What it is for

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), severe erosive esophagitis, poorly responsive systemic GERD, pathologic hypersecretory conditions (Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, systemic mastocytosis, multiple endocrine adenomas) …

How it works

Suppresses gastric secretion by inhibiting hydrogen/potassium ATPase enzyme system in gastric parietal cells; characterized as gastric acid pump inhibitor because it blocks the final step of acid production

Watch for

CNS: Headache, dizziness, asthenia GI: Diarrhea, abdominal pain, vomiting, nausea, constipation, flatulence, acid regurgitation, abdominal swelling, anorexia, irritable colon, esophageal candidiasis, dry mouth, hepatic failure, Clostridium difficile–associated diarrhea (CDAD) INTEG: Rash, dry skin …

Teaching
  • To report severe diarrhea; black, tarry stools; abdominal cramps/pain; or continuing headache; product may have to be discontinued
  • That if diabetic, hypoglycemia may occur
  • To avoid hazardous activities because dizziness may occur
  • To avoid alcohol, salicylates, NSAIDs; may cause GI irritation

💉 Ondansetron

Antiemetic, 5-HT3 antagonist

What it is for

Prevention of nausea, vomiting associated with cancer chemotherapy, radiotherapy; prevention of postoperative nausea, vomiting

How it works

Prevents nausea, vomiting by blocking serotonin peripherally, centrally, and in the small intestine

Watch for

CNS: Headache, dizziness, drowsiness, fatigue GI: Diarrhea, constipation, abdominal pain, dry mouth MISC: Rash, bronchospasm (rare), musculoskeletal pain, wound problems, shivering, fever, hypoxia, urinary retention

Teaching
  • To report diarrhea, constipation, rash, changes in respirations, discomfort at insertion site, serotonin symptoms, EPS symptoms
  • That headache requiring analgesic is common
  • Drink with whole glass of water (PO)
  • Oral disintegrating tabs: remove strip from pouch, place on tongue, and allow to dissolve; drink water

Peptic ulcer disease triple therapy⭐ HIGH YIELD

Classic H. pylori TRIPLE therapy is a PPI plus CLARITHROMYCIN plus AMOXICILLIN (substitute metronidazole if penicillin-allergic) for 14 days. Because of rising clarithromycin resistance, current US guidelines now favor BISMUTH QUADRUPLE therapy first-line: PPI plus bismuth plus tetracycline plus metronidazole for 14 days.

  • ADHERENCE is everything. The full course must be finished or resistance develops and the ulcer returns.
  • Teach expected effects so the patient does not quit: bismuth turns the STOOL AND TONGUE BLACK (harmless, not GI bleeding), and metronidazole causes a metallic taste and a DISULFIRAM-LIKE REACTION with alcohol. No alcohol during treatment and for 3 days after.
  • Clarithromycin: QT prolongation and many CYP3A4 interactions with statins and warfarin. Tetracycline: photosensitivity, avoid dairy and antacids, and avoid in pregnancy and children under 8.
  • Confirm eradication at least 4 weeks after treatment with a urea breath test or stool antigen. Hold the PPI 1 to 2 weeks and antibiotics 4 weeks before testing or you get a false negative.
  • Also address the other ulcer causes: NSAIDs, aspirin, smoking, and alcohol.

💉 Sucralfate

Protectant, antiulcer, Aluminum hydroxide, sulfated sucrose

What it is for

Duodenal ulcer, oral mucositis, stomatitis after radiation of head and neck

How it works

Forms a complex that adheres to ulcer site, adsorbs pepsin

Teaching
  • To take on empty stomach
  • To take full course of therapy; not to use for >8 wk; to avoid smoking
  • To avoid antacids, milk, alkaline water within 1 hr of this product
  • To increase fluids, bulk, exercise to lessen constipation
